Episode 3

I Got a Jury Summons — Now What?

A jury summons isn’t an invitation — it’s a court order. Here’s what to expect, how to reschedule once, and what to do if you truly can’t serve.

About 2 minutes · October 2026

Listen on YouTube

Read the transcript

Miles: That jury summons sitting in your mailbox? Do not throw it away. In this episode, we are walking through exactly what to do with it, step by step.

Marisol: Because a lot of people see that envelope and their first thought is, can I just ignore this?

Miles: You cannot, and here is why. A jury summons is not an invitation. It is a court order. Ignore it and you can be fined, or even charged with contempt of court.

Marisol: Okay, so ignoring it is off the table. But what if the date just does not work? Say you have work, or the kids, or a trip you already booked.

Miles: Good news. You can usually reschedule once. Call the number printed on the summons, or go to the court's website, and ask for a deferral. They will give you a new date. One call, and the problem is handled.

Marisol: So step one is read it, and step two, if the date is bad, call and move it. What happens on the actual day?

Miles: Show up on time. Bring the summons and your I D. Leave your phone in your pocket. And do not worry about not knowing what to do, because they will walk you through everything once you get there.

Marisol: And I have heard you might sit there all day and never even get picked. Is that right?

Miles: That happens all the time. You might sit for a day and get sent home. And here is the part people do not realize. That counts as serving. You did your duty.

Marisol: What about someone who truly cannot serve at all? Maybe a health problem, or they are caring for someone full time.

Miles: Then do not just not show up. Call them and explain. There are exemptions for hardship, health, and caregiving, but you have to ask. Nobody is going to guess for you.

Marisol: Let me run it back. One, a summons is a court order, not an invitation, so do not ignore it. Two, you can usually move the date once, just call the number on the summons or use the court's website. Three, on the day, show up on time with your summons and your I D. And four, if you cannot serve at all, call and ask about an exemption.

Miles: That is the whole playbook. And one quick note. We are not lawyers, and this is not legal advice, just information to get you started. Every state's rules are a little different, and the plain-language guides for your state are all free at the legal buffet dot com.

Marisol: Share this with someone who just got that envelope in the mail. We will be back next week with more practical legal know-how.

Episode 2

What to Do Right After a Car Crash

Just had a car crash? Here’s what to do first — in plain language.

About 2 minutes · October 2026

Listen on YouTube

Read the transcript

Miles: You just got hit. Your hands are shaking, traffic is backing up behind you, and your mind goes blank. If that ever happens to you, there are four things to do at the scene, and in this episode, we're walking through all four.

Marisol: Because those first few minutes after a crash are when people make the mistakes that cost them later, right?

Miles: Exactly. So, thing one: safety first. Check if anyone is hurt, call 911, and if you can, get to a safe spot out of traffic. Nothing else matters until everybody is safe.

Marisol: Okay, everyone's safe, 911 is called. What's next?

Miles: Thing two: document everything, right then, with your phone. Photos of both cars, the license plates, the street, any skid marks. Get the other driver's name, insurance, and plate number. And if the police come out, ask how you can get a copy of the report.

Marisol: And thing three is the one people mess up because they're trying to be nice.

Miles: That's right. Watch what you say. Do not admit fault at the scene, not even to be polite, not even a casual "I'm so sorry, that was my fault." A simple "is everyone okay?" is enough. What you say at the scene can be used later, word for word.

Marisol: So be kind, be brief, and let the facts do the talking. What's the fourth thing?

Miles: Once you're home: call your insurance company, and get checked by a doctor even if you feel fine. Some injuries show up a day or two later, and having that visit on record matters. And know this: the deadline to file a claim is different in every state, and some are short, so don't wait.

Marisol: Let me run it back. One: safety. Check for injuries, call 911, get out of traffic. Two: document everything, photos, plates, insurance info. Three: don't admit fault. Four: call your insurance, see a doctor, and mind your state's deadline.

Miles: That's the whole playbook. And a quick note: we're not lawyers, and this isn't legal advice, just information to get you started. If you want the plain-language guides for your state, they're all free at the legal buffet dot com.

Marisol: Share this with someone who drives, which is pretty much everybody. We'll be back next week with more practical legal know-how.

Episode 1

Served With Court Papers? Here’s What to Do First

Someone just handed you a stack of court papers and you have no idea what to do first. Miles and Marisol walk through the six-step playbook: the deadline, what to read, what to save, and how to get help fast.

About 3 minutes · October 2026

Listen on YouTube

Read the transcript

Miles: Someone just handed you a stack of court papers. Your heart is pounding, your hands are shaking, and you have no idea what you're supposed to do first. If that's you, or someone you love, this episode is for you.

Marisol: Getting served feels like an ambush. One minute it's a normal Tuesday, the next a stranger is handing you a lawsuit. But there is a playbook for this, right?

Miles: There is. And step one is the hardest and the least satisfying: don't panic, and don't ignore it. Those papers come with a deadline. In most states it's somewhere between twenty and thirty days to respond. If you miss it, the other side can win automatically, without you ever getting to tell your side.

Marisol: So the clock is already ticking the moment you're handed the papers.

Miles: Exactly. So step two: read everything, carefully, today. Not tomorrow. Find out who is suing you, what they're asking for, which court it's in, and what that deadline actually is. Circle the date. Put it on your fridge, in your phone, everywhere.

Marisol: Okay, so read it all, find the deadline. What's step three?

Miles: Do not throw anything away, and start a folder. Every paper, every envelope it came in, every text or email related to this — it all goes in the folder. Courts care about proof, and that envelope with the postmark on it might matter more than you think.

Marisol: That's a good one. People toss the envelope and keep the letter, and then nobody can prove when it arrived.

Miles: Happens all the time. Step four: don't try to fix this by calling the other side and arguing it out. Anything you say can end up in front of a judge. Be polite, be brief, and keep it in writing if you have to communicate at all.

Marisol: So no angry phone calls, no long text messages at midnight.

Miles: None. Save it for the courtroom, or better yet, for your lawyer. Which brings us to step five: get help, and do it fast. If money is tight, every state has free legal aid, and most bar associations will point you to a lawyer who'll do a low-cost first consultation. There's a free site called the legal buffet dot com that lists verified legal aid and lawyer referral links for all fifty states, plus a checklist for exactly this situation.

Marisol: And I assume step six is: actually respond before the deadline.

Miles: That's the big one. You generally have to file a written answer with the court, not just show up and explain yourself. The court clerk can tell you which form to use, and legal aid can help you fill it out. But the key word is written. A phone call to the court doesn't count.

Marisol: Let me recap, because this is a lot when you're stressed. One: don't panic, don't ignore it. Two: read everything today and find your deadline. Three: save every paper and envelope in one folder. Four: don't argue it out with the other side. Five: get help fast, legal aid is free. Six: file a written response before the deadline.

Miles: That's the playbook. And one last thing: we're not lawyers, and this isn't legal advice. It's a starting point. Every situation is different, which is exactly why step five matters so much.

Marisol: If this helped you, share it with someone who might need it. We'll be back with more practical legal know-how next time.

We’re not lawyers, and the podcast isn’t legal advice — it’s a starting point. For advice about your situation, talk to a licensed attorney in your state.
