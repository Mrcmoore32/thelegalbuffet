Be prepared

Court checklists

Showing up prepared is half the battle. These checklists cover the most common situations — print one out or pull it up on your phone before you go. Rules vary by state and courthouse, so confirm the details with your local court.

Small claims court — what to bring

Small claims court is designed for regular people, no lawyer needed. But judges decide based on what you can show, not just what you say. Bring proof.

  • Your claim paperwork — the filed claim form plus proof you served the other side (the court needs to see they were notified).
  • Three copies of everything — one for you, one for the judge, one for the other side. Courts love this.
  • Contracts and receipts — the agreement, invoice, or receipt behind the dispute. Highlight the key lines.
  • Photos and messages — printed photos of damage or problems, plus printed texts or emails, with dates visible.
  • A one-page timeline — what happened, in order, with dates. Judges hear dozens of cases a day; make yours easy to follow.
  • Your math — exactly how much you're asking for and how you got that number (bills, estimates, receipts).
  • Photo ID — some courthouses require it to get in the building.
  • Witnesses, if any — people who saw what happened, in person. A written note from someone who isn't there carries little weight.

Tip: each state caps how much you can claim in small claims court (often $5,000–$10,000). Check your state's small claims section on this site before you file.

Uncontested divorce hearing — what to bring

"Uncontested" means you both agree. The hearing is usually short — the judge just confirms the paperwork is in order. Missing documents are the main reason these get delayed.

  • Photo ID — required at most courthouses.
  • Your filed petition and summons — copies of everything you filed to start the case.
  • Proof of service — the document showing your spouse was properly served (or their signed waiver/answer, if they cooperated).
  • The settlement agreement — signed by both of you, covering property, debts, and support. Bring the original plus copies.
  • Financial disclosures — many states require both sides to exchange income, asset, and debt information. Bring your copies.
  • Parenting plan, if you have kids — custody schedule, decision-making, and child support worksheet, signed.
  • Proposed final decree — some courts want you to bring the draft order for the judge to sign. Check your court's self-help page.
  • A pen — you'll likely sign something.

Tip: arrive 30 minutes early. Parking, security lines, and finding the right courtroom take longer than you'd think.

First meeting with a lawyer — what to bring

Many lawyers offer a low-cost first consultation. Make it count: the more organized you are, the better advice you'll get in the time you have.

  • A one-page summary — who, what, when, in plain order. Write it beforehand so you don't burn consultation time explaining.
  • Every relevant document — contracts, letters, court papers, police reports, photos. Bring originals and let them copy what matters.
  • A timeline with dates — deadlines matter in law. When did it happen, when were you notified, what have you filed so far?
  • Names and contact info — the other people or companies involved, plus any witnesses.
  • What you've already done — calls made, forms filed, money paid. Don't make them guess.
  • Your questions, written down — what will this cost, how long will it take, what are my options, what happens if I do nothing?
  • Payment for the consultation — ask the fee when you book so there's no surprise.

Tip: don't sign a retainer on the spot unless you're sure. It's fine to take the fee agreement home and think it over.

Traffic court — what to bring and how to prepare

Most traffic cases are decided in a few minutes. Judges respond well to drivers who show up organized, honest, and respectful. Bring the paperwork and let it speak for you.

  • The ticket (citation) — the paper copy or a printout. It has your case number, the court date, and exactly what you're charged with.
  • Driver's license — valid and current. If it's suspended, be honest about it and have a plan for getting to work.
  • Proof of insurance — a current card. If you were cited for no insurance but were covered, bring proof the policy was active on the date of the stop.
  • Vehicle registration — bring it if the stop involved plates or registration.
  • Proof you fixed the problem — cited for a broken taillight, tint, or expired registration? A repair receipt dated before your court date can get the fine reduced or dismissed.
  • Your notes or dashcam footage — notes written down the day of the stop (weather, road conditions, what the officer said) and any dashcam video on your phone.
  • A way to pay — fines, fees, and possible payment plans vary by court. Know whether you want to pay in full, ask for a payment plan, or request traffic school where it's available.
  • Dress neatly and arrive early — business-casual, phone silenced, 30 minutes early. Address the judge as "Your Honor."
  • Stay calm and polite — don't argue with the officer in court. State your case briefly and let the judge decide.
  • Know your plea options — guilty, not guilty, or no contest. Asking for a reduced fine or traffic school is normal and common.

Tip: check your state's traffic/DUI section on this site before your date — it explains common defenses and what the real costs look like.

Eviction defense — what to bring and your first steps

Eviction cases move fast — deadlines are often measured in days, not weeks. The good news: landlords must follow strict legal steps, and tenants have real defenses. Organize your proof right away.

  • Every notice you received — the pay-or-quit, cure-or-quit, or termination notice. The legal timeline starts from when you were properly served, and the deadlines are short.
  • Your lease or rental agreement — the signed copy. The judge will check what it actually says, not what anyone claims it says.
  • Proof of every payment — rent receipts, bank statements, money order stubs, payment-app screenshots. If the landlord claims you didn't pay, this is your answer.
  • A simple payment ledger — month, amount paid, and how, on one page. Courts love a clean summary.
  • Photos of conditions — mold, leaks, broken heat, pests — plus dated repair requests you sent the landlord. Habitability problems can be a defense.
  • Written communication, printed — texts and emails with the landlord, with dates visible. Verbal promises are hard to prove.
  • Your ID and proof you're the tenant on the lease.
  • Witness contact info — anyone who saw the conditions or heard what the landlord said.
  • Don't just move out — leaving before a judge rules can hurt your case and your rental record. Show up to the hearing even if you plan to leave.
  • Get free help fast — legal-aid offices handle evictions every day. Find one at LawHelp.org, or call 211 (211.org) for legal help and emergency rental assistance in your area.

Tip: some cities have free eviction-defense programs — ask 211 whether yours does before you hire anyone.

Name-change hearing — what to bring

Adult name changes are usually straightforward if your paperwork is complete. These hearings are often short — a few questions under oath, then the judge signs the order.

  • Copies of your petition — the filed petition and any court orders so far. Bring two copies.
  • Photo ID — driver's license, state ID, or passport.
  • Birth certificate — most courts require a certified copy (the raised-seal one from the state, not the hospital keepsake).
  • Proof of publication, if your state requires it — the newspaper's affidavit showing your name-change notice ran for the required weeks. Many states require this for adults; requirements for minors differ.
  • The filing fee — usually $100–$300 depending on the state. Call the clerk ahead of time to confirm, and ask about a fee waiver if money is tight.
  • Proof of residency — a utility bill, lease, or ID showing you live in the county where you filed.
  • Background check results — some states require one; check your court's self-help page before the hearing so you're not caught short.
  • Custody or support orders, if they exist — if you share custody of a minor, the court will want to see the other parent was notified.

Tip: after the judge signs the order, you'll need certified copies for Social Security, the DMV, and your bank — ask the clerk how many to buy.

Preparing for a custody hearing — what to bring and how to present

Judges decide custody on one question: what's best for the child. Your job is to show — with records, not just words — that you're the stable, cooperative parent. Organization wins here.

  • A parenting calendar — the last 3–6 months: who had the kids when, pickups, missed visits. This is often your most persuasive document.
  • School records — attendance, report cards, teacher notes. They show stability and involvement.
  • Medical records — doctor visits, who took them, immunization records.
  • Your proposed parenting plan — a written schedule: weekdays, weekends, holidays, transportation, and decision-making. Bring two copies.
  • Proof of a stable home — lease or mortgage statement, photos of the child's bedroom and living space.
  • Communication with the other parent — printed texts and emails showing you're cooperative — or documenting real problems.
  • Your work and childcare schedule — who watches the kids while you work. Show the plan is realistic.
  • One or two character references — short letters from teachers, coaches, or family who have seen you parent.
  • Three copies of everything — one for you, one for the judge, one for the other parent.
  • Demeanor matters — dress neatly, stay calm, and never badmouth the other parent in court. Judges watch this closely. Speak about the kids' needs, not your grievances.

Tip: keep a simple daily log starting today — bedtimes, meals, homework, doctor visits. A few weeks of it can speak louder than testimony.

What to do when you're served papers — your first 72 hours

Being served feels alarming, but it's just the legal process starting — not a verdict. What you do in the first three days protects everything that comes after.

  • Don't ignore it — not responding can mean an automatic loss (called a default judgment). The clock starts the day you're served.
  • Read everything carefully — which court, who's suing you, what they want, and the deadline to respond. Circle the deadline.
  • Write the response deadline down — it's often 20–30 days. Missing it is the single most common and most costly mistake.
  • Don't contact the other party to argue — anything you say can be used. From now on, keep all communication in writing.
  • Save all evidence as-is — don't delete texts or emails or throw away documents related to the dispute.
  • Write down your version — what happened, in order, with dates, while it's fresh in your memory.
  • Check whether you were served properly — wrong person, wrong address, or a missing signature where one is required can matter. Note anything odd.
  • Find help fast — look up free help at LawHelp.org, call 211 (211.org), or check your state court website's self-help center for the response forms.
  • Ask about fee waivers — if you can't afford the filing fee for your response, ask the clerk about a waiver before the deadline.
  • File your response even if you plan to settle — it protects your rights while you talk, and it costs you nothing to keep your options open.

Tip: bring the papers (or photos of them) to any lawyer or legal-aid meeting — they'll read the deadline and the claims in seconds.

Meeting with a legal-aid office — what to bring

Legal-aid offices provide free help to people who qualify, usually based on income. Intake staff screen a lot of people every day — showing up organized gets you routed to the right program faster.

  • Photo ID — for you and anyone else involved in the case.
  • Proof of income — pay stubs, benefit letters (Social Security, unemployment), or a recent tax return. Legal aid is income-based, so they need this first.
  • A one-page timeline — what happened, in order, with dates. Keep it to one page.
  • Every related document — court papers, letters, contracts, notices, tickets. Bring the originals in a folder.
  • Everyone involved — names, addresses, and phone numbers of the other side and any witnesses.
  • Proof of address — a lease, utility bill, or mail showing where you live. Some programs only serve certain counties.
  • What outcome you need — be specific: "stop the eviction," "get custody papers filed." It helps them route you to the right program.
  • Your questions, written down — what kinds of cases they take, how long the wait is, and what to do while you wait.

Tip: find your local legal-aid office at LawHelp.org — or call 211 (211.org) and ask for legal help in your area.
