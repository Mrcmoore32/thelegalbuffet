Car repossession in Mississippi

If you're behind on car payments, know your rights before the tow truck shows up — and after.

Your rights

What they can't do

No breaking into a locked garage, no physical force, no threats — that's "breach of the peace" and it's illegal everywhere. They must return your personal belongings from the car.

Get it back

Cure and redemption

No state cure right — only what your loan contract allows. Pay the full balance plus costs any time before the car is sold. If you've paid 60%+ of the loan, the lender must sell within 90 days.

Money owed

Deficiency balance

The lender can sue for the remaining balance and must send a written explanation.

Notice

What they must tell you

No advance warning required. At least 10 days' notice before the earliest sale date.

What to do next:

  1. If your car was taken, call the lender immediately and ask for the exact payoff or cure amount in writing.
  2. Remove valuables from your car now — don't wait for repossession.
  3. If the repo agent used force, threats, or broke into a locked space, document everything and contact free legal help in Mississippi.

Health insurance & medical bills in Mississippi

A denied claim or a shocking hospital bill is not the end of the story. Health insurance denials can be appealed twice — first to the insurer itself, then to an independent reviewer whose decision the insurer must accept — and federal law now bans most surprise out-of-network bills outright. Separately, if you cannot afford a hospital bill, nonprofit hospitals are required to have a financial assistance program, and many people who qualify never apply. This section is about getting a denial or a bill fixed. It is not about suing a doctor or hospital for harm from care itself — for that, see the Medical malpractice section on this page.

Step 1

Internal appeal: 180 days from the denial

When your plan denies a claim or refuses to pay for care, you have 180 days from the date you got the denial notice to file an internal appeal — ask the insurer to review its own decision with a different reviewer. The plan must decide within 30 days if the service has not happened yet, or 60 days if it already has; urgent cases must be decided within 72 hours. Your strongest move is a letter from your doctor explaining, in plain terms, why the care is medically necessary — attach it, keep a copy of everything, and write down the date you filed and every name you spoke to.

Step 2

External review: an independent reviewer, and the decision binds the insurer

If the internal appeal says no, you can ask for external review within 4 months of the final internal denial. An independent reviewer with no ties to your insurer looks at the medical evidence and decides — and the insurer is legally required to accept that decision. Standard reviews are decided within 45 days; urgent ones within 72 hours. If your plan uses the federal process, it is free: call 1-888-866-6205 to request a form, or use the HealthCare.gov external review page, which also lists state programs.

HealthCare.gov: external review →
Surprise bills

Surprise bill? The No Surprises Act may make it illegal

Since January 1, 2022, federal law bans balance billing in three situations: emergency care, non-emergency care from an out-of-network provider at an in-network hospital or surgery center (the anesthesiologist you never chose, for example), and air ambulance rides. In those cases you generally owe only your normal in-network cost-sharing — the provider and the plan fight over the rest, not you. Ground ambulance rides are not covered by the federal law, though some state laws cover them. If you get a bill that looks like a banned surprise bill, do not just pay it: dispute it in writing with the provider and the plan, and complain to the federal No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059.

File a No Surprises complaint with CMS →
No insurance

Uninsured or paying yourself: get the estimate, then dispute the gap

If you have no insurance or are paying out of pocket, you have the right to a good faith estimate before scheduled care. If the final bill is at least $400 more than that estimate, you may dispute it through the federal patient-provider dispute process within 120 days of the first bill. Keep the estimate and the bill side by side — the gap is the whole dispute.

Charity care

Hospital financial assistance (charity care): ask before you pay

Nonprofit hospitals — most community hospitals — are required by federal tax law to have a written financial assistance policy, publish it, and cap what qualifying patients are charged at the amounts generally billed to insured patients. Each hospital sets its own income limits in its policy, so read that policy, not a rumor about it. Call the hospital’s billing or financial counseling office and ask, by name, for the financial assistance application. Nonprofit hospitals must accept applications for at least 240 days from your first bill after discharge, and a complete application in that window pauses collection efforts while it is decided. If you are denied, ask for the reason in writing.

In Mississippi: your state insurance department and your denial letter

Your denial letter or Explanation of Benefits must tell you how to appeal and, in many states, names the organization that handles your external review. Your state insurance department also takes complaints about state-regulated plans and can point you to the right review program. This page does not list one Mississippi phone number or form because the right office depends on your plan — a plan you bought yourself, a plan through a small employer, and a large employer’s self-funded plan follow different tracks — so start with the contact information on your denial letter and the HealthCare.gov external review page linked above, which lists state programs.

What to do:

  1. Read the denial or bill the day it arrives. Circle the reason given, the amount, and the appeal deadline — 180 days for an internal appeal, 4 months after that for external review.
  2. Ask for an itemized bill and compare it line by line with your Explanation of Benefits. Wrong codes, duplicate lines, and services you never got are common — and a billing-office correction is faster than an appeal.
  3. File the internal appeal in writing, with your doctor’s letter attached, and keep copies plus proof you sent it.
  4. If the internal appeal fails, file for external review — do not stop there. Independent reviewers overturn denials often enough that skipping this step leaves money on the table.
  5. For a hospital bill you cannot afford, ask for the financial assistance application before you agree to a payment plan, and get any payment-plan or hold-on-collections promise in writing.
  6. Never ignore a bill while you dispute it: tell the provider and any collector in writing that you dispute it, and ask them to pause collections while the dispute is pending.

Medical malpractice in Mississippi

A bad outcome is not always malpractice. To have a case, you usually must show the doctor or hospital fell below the accepted standard of care — what a careful provider would have done — and that the mistake caused your injury. These cases turn on strict deadlines and expert review, so the clock matters here more than almost anywhere else.

Take action

Complain to the Mississippi State Board of Medical Licensure

Filing a complaint with the state medical board is free. The board can investigate and discipline a doctor, but it cannot award you money — that is what a lawsuit is for. Filing a complaint does not pause your lawsuit deadline.

File a complaint or look up a doctor →
Deadline

Mississippi's deadline to sue

You generally have 2 years from the act or omission, or from when it was or with reasonable diligence might have been first discovered — and never more than 7 years from the act. Miss the deadline and your case is usually over, no matter how strong it is — talk to a lawyer well before it runs out.

Free help

Talk to a malpractice lawyer

Most malpractice lawyers give a free first consultation and work on contingency — you pay nothing up front, and they take a percentage only if you win. Many states also require an expert to certify the case before you can file; a lawyer handles that.

Your records

Get your medical records

You have the right to copies of your own medical records. Ask every provider involved, in writing, and keep the copies somewhere safe — records are the backbone of any malpractice case.

What to do next:

  1. Request complete copies of your medical records from every provider involved.
  2. Write down your timeline — dates, symptoms, what you were told — while it is fresh.
  3. Do not sign anything from the hospital or its insurer, and do not accept a quick settlement, before a lawyer looks at it.
  4. Get a free consultation with a malpractice attorney well before Mississippi's deadline above.
  5. If you want the doctor investigated, file the free board complaint too — just remember it does not stop your lawsuit clock.

Being accused of a crime you did not commit is frightening — but you have rights from the first minute. You are presumed innocent, you do not have to talk to police, and if you cannot afford a lawyer, the court must appoint one to defend you for free. What you do in the first days matters most.

Know your rights

Your first court appearance

At your first appearance (arraignment), the judge reads the charges and asks how you plead. Pleading not guilty keeps every option open — it does not lock you into a trial. This is also where you ask the court to appoint a public defender if you cannot afford a lawyer. Do not plead guilty just to get it over with.

What to do next:

  1. Do not talk to police without a lawyer. Say: “I am using my right to remain silent, and I want a lawyer” — then stop talking.
  2. Do not consent to searches of your home, car, or phone. Saying “I do not consent to a search” protects you later, even if police search anyway.
  3. Write down your timeline and where you were, plus the names of anyone who can confirm it, while it is fresh.
  4. Save everything — texts, emails, receipts, photos — and do not delete anything, even if it looks bad.
  5. Do not contact the person who accused you, and do not post about the case online.
  6. Ask the court for a public defender at your first appearance if you cannot afford a lawyer.

Know your rights

Guardianship, conservatorship & long-term care in Mississippi

When an adult can no longer make safe decisions about their own health, home, or money — after dementia, a brain injury, or a serious disability — someone has to be legally able to decide for them. Guardianship and conservatorship are the court’s answer, but they are also the most restrictive answer: a court order takes away an adult’s right to decide. That is why courts are supposed to try the least restrictive option first, and why this section starts with the alternatives. The Wills & probate section on this page covers what happens after death; this section covers what happens while the person is still alive and needs help deciding.

What it means

Guardianship vs. conservatorship: who decides what

A guardian is a person a court appoints to make personal decisions for an adult who cannot safely make them — where they live, what medical care they get, and day-to-day care. A conservator is a person a court appoints to handle money and property — paying bills, managing accounts, and protecting assets. In many states those are two separate jobs; in others, one appointment covers both, or the state uses only one of the words. The exact titles and powers in Mississippi come from Mississippi law and from the judge’s order itself, so read the order, not just the label: a good order is narrow, naming only the decisions the person truly cannot make, and leaving the rest with the person.

Step 1

Who can petition, and how the court process works

Usually a family member, close friend, or concerned professional files a petition in the probate court of the county where the adult lives, explaining what decisions the person can no longer make safely and what evidence supports that. The adult must be given notice and a chance to be heard, and in most states the court orders an independent evaluation — often by a doctor or a court visitor — and appoints a lawyer for the adult if they do not have one. At a hearing, the judge decides whether the person lacks capacity for specific decisions and, if so, who should serve and how broad the powers should be. Bring medical records, a list of the person’s existing helpers (family, home aides, a power of attorney), and any written wishes the person expressed while they could still decide.

Contested?

If family members disagree, or the adult objects

Guardianships are contested more often than people expect — siblings disagree about who should serve, or the adult themselves says they do not need a guardian. Anyone with an interest can object, present their own evidence, and ask the judge to choose a different person or a narrower order. If you object, say so in writing by the court’s deadline and show up: an unopposed petition is usually granted. After appointment, the guardian or conservator must report to the court — typically an annual report on the person’s condition and, for money, an accounting of every dollar in and out. If a guardian misuses money, ignores the person’s wishes, or is no longer needed, anyone can ask the court to review, limit, replace, or end the guardianship.

Try first

Less-restrictive alternatives: try these before court

Courts are supposed to prefer the least restrictive option that keeps the person safe, and these often avoid guardianship entirely: a durable power of attorney lets a trusted person handle money and sign documents (durable means it stays valid if the person later loses capacity); a health care directive or proxy names who makes medical decisions; supported decision-making — now recognized in a growing number of states — is a written agreement where the person keeps their legal rights and names supporters who help them understand options and communicate choices, rather than deciding for them; and practical tools like joint accounts, automatic bill pay, and a Social Security representative payee can solve a single problem without a court case. If the person can still understand and communicate with support, an alternative usually fits better than a guardianship — and it is far cheaper and faster.

Nursing home

Nursing-home resident rights and discharge protections

A nursing-home resident keeps their rights: to be treated with dignity, to take part in their own care plan, to have visitors, to manage their own money or choose someone who will, and to complain — to the facility, to the state, or to the ombudsman — without retaliation. A facility generally cannot just throw a resident out: federal rules require written notice of a discharge or transfer, usually 30 days in advance, stating the reason and the right to appeal, with shorter notice only in emergencies such as a medical crisis or an immediate safety risk. If you get a discharge notice, do not move the resident out quietly and do not miss the appeal deadline on the notice — call the ombudsman the same day (below). Before choosing a facility, compare inspection results, staffing, and star ratings on Medicare’s Care Compare.

Wills & probate in Mississippi

When someone dies, their property does not just move on its own. Probate is the court process that proves the will, pays the debts, and transfers what is left to the family. Every state also has a shortcut for small estates — and if you are making your own will, Mississippi’s signing rules decide whether it counts. Here is where to start in Mississippi.

Small estates

Mississippi’s small-estate shortcut

Estates of $75,000 or less (the whole probate estate, not counting liens) can use Mississippi’s small-estate affidavit after a 30-day wait (Miss. Code §91-7-322).

Making a will

Are handwritten wills valid in Mississippi?

Only in narrow cases. Mississippi recognizes a handwritten will only if it is entirely written and signed by the person who made it (Miss. Code §91-5-1).

First steps

When someone dies: the first week

Order several certified copies of the death certificate — banks, insurers, and agencies each want one. Find the original will (check the home, a safe deposit box, and the court or lawyer who prepared it). Secure the home and vehicles. Do not pay the person’s debts from your own money — debts are paid from the estate, in an order the law sets. Notify Social Security and the person’s employer and insurers.

What to do next:

  1. Get certified death certificates and locate the original will.
  2. Check the estate’s size against Mississippi’s small-estate shortcut above — it can save months and court fees.
  3. If the estate is larger, or includes a house or a dispute, open probate at the court above — and ask legal aid or an estate lawyer about a free first consultation.
  4. Do not distribute property, sell the car, or clean out accounts until the court says who has authority — handing things out early can make you personally responsible.
  5. Making your own will? Follow Mississippi’s signing and witness rules exactly, tell your executor where the original is, and review it after marriages, divorces, births, and deaths.

Bankruptcy in Mississippi

Bankruptcy is a legal way to wipe out or reorganize debts you cannot pay. Chapter 7 erases most unsecured debts like credit cards and medical bills, usually in a few months. Chapter 13 is a 3-to-5-year payment plan that can stop a foreclosure and let you catch up. The day you file, an “automatic stay” stops most collections, lawsuits, wage garnishments, and shutoffs. Some debts survive — most student loans, recent taxes, and child support among them.

Workers’ compensation in Mississippi

If you are hurt at work or made sick by your job, workers’ compensation is the system that pays for your medical care and replaces part of your lost wages — and you do not have to prove your employer did anything wrong to use it. But the system runs on deadlines and paperwork, and the first steps matter more than people expect: report the injury in writing right away, get medical care and tell the provider it is a work injury, and keep a copy of everything. This section walks through the claim from the day of the injury to a denial or a return to work. It is the dedicated guide; the Workplace rights section on this page only mentions workers’ compensation in passing.

Step 1

Report the injury in writing, the same day if you can

Tell your supervisor or employer about the injury or illness as soon as possible, in writing — an email or a dated note counts, and keep a copy for yourself. Say what happened, when, where, and what part of your body was hurt. Deadlines for reporting vary by state, and in some states they are short (days or weeks), so do not wait to see if it gets better on its own. A late report is one of the most common reasons claims get denied. If your injury or illness developed over time — a bad back, a breathing problem, a repetitive-strain injury — report it as soon as a doctor connects it to your work.

Step 2

Get medical care, and say it is a work injury

Get care promptly, and tell every provider the injury or illness is work-related so the visit is billed to workers’ compensation, not to you or your health plan. In some states your employer or its insurer picks the first doctor; in others you pick. Follow the treatment plan and go to every appointment — missed care is used against claims. Keep every bill, work note, and restriction slip (for example, “no lifting over 10 pounds”), and give copies to your employer.

Step 3

Your employer reports it, the insurer decides

Veterans & military legal help in Mississippi

Veterans and active-duty service members have three separate legal toolkits that most people never hear about in one place: a way to challenge a VA benefits decision, a way to upgrade a less-than-honorable discharge, and a federal law that pauses or caps civilian debts and lawsuits while you serve. All three are federal, so they work the same in every state — what changes by state is only where you find free local help. The one veteran resource already on this page, the VA homelessness hotline, is not this guide: this section is about benefits decisions, discharge upgrades, and service-member protections.

VA decision

VA denied your claim? Pick the lane that matches what is wrong

You have three decision-review options, and the right one depends on your evidence. Higher-Level Review: a senior reviewer looks at the exact same file for an error — you cannot add new evidence, but you can request a one-time informal conference to point out the mistake. Supplemental Claim: you bring new and relevant evidence the VA did not have before, such as a new medical opinion or a buddy statement. Board Appeal: a Veterans Law Judge decides — on the existing record (Direct Review), with new evidence added within 90 days (Evidence Submission), or after a hearing where you testify (Hearing docket). You can choose a different option for each issue, but you cannot use two options for the same issue at the same time, and you cannot request a second Higher-Level Review or a second Board Appeal in a row on the same issue.

Deadline

The one-year VA clock — counted from the date on the letter

For most benefits, you have 1 year from the date printed on your VA decision letter — not the date you received it — to request a Higher-Level Review or a Board Appeal. A Supplemental Claim can be filed at any time, but filing it within that same year keeps your claim continuously pursued, which protects your original effective date and your back pay; filed later, the effective date generally moves to the new filing date. Exception: in a contested claim, where two people claim a benefit only one can receive, a Board Appeal is due within 60 days. Some benefit types have shorter limits stated on the letter itself, so read your letter first. After a Board decision you disagree with, you can file a Supplemental Claim with new and relevant evidence, or appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims within 120 days. VA’s published average goals are about 125 days for Higher-Level Reviews and Supplemental Claims, and 365 days (Direct Review), 550 days (Evidence Submission), or 730 days (Hearing) for Board appeals — goals, not guarantees.

Discharge

A discharge below Honorable can block VA benefits, jobs, and education aid, and it can be challenged. If you were discharged within the last 15 years, apply to your branch’s Discharge Review Board with DD Form 293 — the board can upgrade the characterization and change the reason, and it cannot make your discharge worse, but it cannot review a general court-martial discharge. If you are past 15 years, apply to your branch’s Board for Correction of Military Records with DD Form 149; its usual 3-year filing limit is often waived in the interest of justice. A denial from the review board can still go to the correction board. Include your DD 214, a short statement of why the discharge was unjust or in error, medical records, and proof of your life since service — and if PTSD, traumatic brain injury, or sexual trauma during service played a role, say so plainly: the boards must give that evidence liberal consideration.

SCRA

On active duty? The SCRA caps interest at 6% and pauses lawsuits

The Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (federal law, 50 U.S.C. §§ 3901–4043) protects active-duty members, reservists and Guard members on federal orders, and commissioned Public Health Service and NOAA officers, plus many protections for their dependents. The three most-used protections: (1) 6% interest cap on debts from before you entered service — credit cards, car loans, mortgages — but you must request it in writing from each creditor with a copy of your orders; the interest above 6% is forgiven, not deferred. (2) Lease termination without penalty when you receive orders for a permanent change of station, a deployment of 90 days or more, or separation/retirement — give written notice with your orders. (3) Default-judgment protection: a court cannot enter a default judgment against you while service keeps you from responding; it must appoint an attorney for you and can stay the case. Landlords also generally cannot evict you without a court order, and a pre-service mortgage cannot be foreclosed without a court order. None of this is automatic — you have to ask, in writing, with your orders attached.

Unemployment benefits in Mississippi

Losing a job is expensive enough without losing benefits to a missed week or a missed deadline. Unemployment benefits replace part of your pay while you look for new work, but they are not automatic: you must file promptly, prove you are able to work and looking for work, and certify every week to keep the money coming. This is the dedicated guide — the Workplace rights section on this page only gives unemployment one paragraph. The rules below are the ones that hold in most states; where a number varies by state, this section says so and sends you to the Mississippi agency for the exact figure, because trusting a national number is how people miss a deadline.

Step 1

File in your first week — benefits generally are not retroactive

File your claim in the first week you are out of work, even if you are still waiting on a final paycheck or severance paperwork. In most states you will not be paid for weeks before the week you filed, no matter how good the reason you waited. You will need your Social Security number, your employer’s name and address, your dates of employment, and your recent earnings. File in the state where you worked, not necessarily the state where you live now. If you were laid off, fired, or quit, answer honestly about why — the agency checks with your employer, and a mismatch delays everything.

Step 2

Certify every week, and keep looking for work

Once your claim is open, you must certify — usually online or by phone, every week or every two weeks depending on the state — that you were able to work, available for work, and looking for work, and you must report any earnings. Miss a certification and that week is not paid, even if you were eligible. Keep a written log of every job you apply for, with dates and employer names: the agency can ask for it, and a thin log is a common reason benefits stop. If you are sick, in school, or unavailable for a week, report it rather than certifying something untrue.

Denied?

If you are denied, appeal the day you get the notice

A denial (often called a determination) can be appealed to a hearing, usually before an administrative law judge, where you can present evidence and witnesses. The deadline varies by state — commonly about 10 to 30 days — and the clock usually runs from the mailing date printed on the notice, not the date you opened it, so read every notice the day it arrives and file your appeal that same day even if your evidence is not ready yet; you can add it before the hearing. If you miss the deadline, you may still file late with a good-cause reason, such as serious illness or never receiving the notice, but “I did not understand” is rarely enough. Keep certifying while your appeal is pending — if you win, you can only be paid for weeks you certified.

Overpayment

Overpayment notice? Do not ignore it — appeal it or ask about a waiver

An overpayment notice says you were paid benefits you were not entitled to, and it states an amount and a reason. In most cases overpayments must be repaid even if you already spent the money, and the agency can take it from future benefits or tax refunds if you do nothing. But you have options, and both have deadlines: appeal if the decision is wrong (for example, earnings you did report), and ask about a waiver if you were not at fault and repayment would cause real hardship — many states waive non-fault overpayments on hardship grounds, but the rules and the form are state-specific, so use the agency link below and do not assume a waiver is automatic. Never ignore the notice while you decide: an unanswered overpayment grows penalties and collection action.

Part-time work

Part-time work: report every dollar, and partial benefits may still be possible

Taking part-time or temporary work does not automatically end your benefits — but you must report all earnings for the week you earned them, not the week you were paid, and failing to report earnings is the fastest route to an overpayment and a fraud penalty. In most states part of your earnings is disregarded and the rest reduces your weekly benefit dollar-for-dollar or by a formula; earn too much in a week and that week pays nothing, but the claim itself usually stays open. The exact disregard and formula are set by Mississippi law, so check the agency page below before you turn down a shift out of fear of losing everything, or accept one without reporting it.

Adoption, paternity & parentage in Mississippi

Two different questions live in this section, and both come down to one thing: who counts, legally, as a child’s parent. Adoption creates a legal parent-child relationship where none existed before. Parentage (often called paternity when the father is the parent being established) proves that relationship when a child is born to unmarried parents. Parentage matters beyond the birth certificate: until parentage is legally established, a court generally cannot order custody, visitation, or child support. The Child custody and Child support sections on this page cover what happens after parentage exists; this section covers how you get there, and how adoption works from the other side.

Adoption types

The four paths to adoption

Agency adoption goes through a licensed public or private agency, which matches children with families and completes the required home study. Private (independent) adoption is arranged directly between birth parents and adoptive parents, usually with lawyers on both sides and a court-approved home study. Stepparent adoption is when a spouse adopts their partner’s child — often the simplest path, but it still requires the other birth parent’s rights to be terminated or surrendered, unless that parent is deceased or their rights were already terminated. Foster-care adoption is adopting a child already in state care, usually after parental rights have been terminated; children adopted from foster care who meet the state’s definition of special needs may qualify for federal or state adoption assistance to help with costs. Every path ends the same way: a court hearing that finalizes the adoption and issues a new birth certificate naming the adoptive parents.

Adoption steps

How adoption works, step by step

First, the birth parents’ legal rights must end — by voluntary surrender or consent, or by a court order terminating rights after a hearing. Second, the adoptive family completes a home study: interviews, background checks, a home visit, and references, done by a licensed agency or social worker. Third, the child is placed with the family for a supervision period set by state law. Fourth, the court holds a finalization hearing and signs the adoption decree. Two warnings: never pay for a child or promise payment in exchange for consent — baby-selling is illegal in every state, and legitimate adoption costs are fees for agencies, lawyers, and the home study, not payments to birth parents — and never skip the home study or the court hearing, because an adoption that is not finalized by a court is not an adoption.

Parentage

Establishing paternity and parentage: three ways

When parents are married, the spouse is usually presumed to be the legal parent automatically. When they are not, parentage must be established one of three ways. Voluntary acknowledgment: both parents sign a Voluntary Acknowledgment of Paternity (VAP) — hospitals are required to offer this form at birth, and it can also be signed later. Signing is a legal act with real consequences, including child support, so do not sign it casually or under pressure; most states give a short window to rescind it. Court order: either parent, or the child-support agency, can file a parentage case and ask a judge to decide. Genetic testing: the court can order DNA testing, and the child-support agency can usually arrange testing without a full court case. If the test excludes a man, he is not the legal father; if it matches, the court declares parentage.

Why it matters

Why parentage comes first: no parentage, no custody or support order

Parentage is the key that unlocks everything else. Without it, a father generally cannot get a court order for custody or visitation, and a mother generally cannot get a court order for child support from him — the court has no legal parent to order. Parentage also affects the child’s rights: inheritance, health-insurance coverage through a parent, Social Security benefits on a parent’s record, and access to family medical history. If you are unsure whether parentage is established for your child, check whether a VAP was signed or a court order exists; a name on the birth certificate alone does not always settle the legal question, and the rules vary by state.

It publishes plain-language guides to every type of adoption, to parentage, and to post-adoption help, plus state-by-state information on adoption assistance. It is free, and it is the best first stop before you pay anyone for adoption advice.

Financial scams in Mississippi

Scammers are professional liars, and they are good at it — Americans lose billions of dollars to fraud every year, and most of it is never reported. Falling for one is not a character flaw; these crews run scripts tested on thousands of people. The good news: almost every financial scam shares the same handful of tells, and once you know them, the spell breaks.

The golden rule: anyone who pressures you to pay right now with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, a cash app, or cash is running a scam — full stop. No real agency, bank, utility, or business demands payment that way. The second someone insists on secrecy (“don’t tell your family or your bank”), hang up. Urgency plus secrecy plus a strange payment method is the whole game.

Know the signs

Government impostors

A call, text, or email claims to be the IRS, Social Security, Medicare, or the sheriff: you owe money, your benefits will stop, or there is a warrant for your arrest. Real agencies almost always contact you by mail first, never threaten arrest over the phone, and never ask for gift cards or wire transfers. Hang up and call the agency yourself at the number on its official website or your paperwork.

Know the signs

The family emergency — now with cloned voices

“Grandma, I’m in jail, I need bail money — please don’t tell Mom.” Scammers can now clone a loved one’s voice from a few seconds of online audio, so it can sound exactly like them. Defenses that work: agree on a family code word in advance, and always hang up and call your loved one back on the number you already have. Real trouble survives a callback; a scam can’t.

Know the signs

Romance and investment scams

An online sweetheart who can never meet — and eventually needs money — is a script, not a relationship. So is the friendly stranger pitching can’t-miss crypto or investment returns, sometimes showing you a fake account that looks like it is growing (“pig butchering”). Guaranteed high returns with no risk do not exist. If you have to pay fees or taxes before you can withdraw your own money, it was never real.

Know the signs

Fake checks, phishing, and tech support

Motorcycle lane splitting in Mississippi

Lane splitting is a motorcycle riding between lanes of traffic. Lane filtering is the narrower version — moving between stopped or very slow cars, usually to the front at a light. Only six states allow either one, and every one of them puts strict speed limits on it. Two motorcycles riding side by side in one lane is different — that is lane sharing, and it is legal almost everywhere.

Not allowed

The rule in Mississippi

Lane splitting and lane filtering are not allowed in Mississippi. Mississippi law (Miss. Code § 63-3-1313) gives motorcycles full use of a lane and bars a rider from passing another vehicle in the same lane or riding between lanes of traffic. A rider who does it can be ticketed for improper passing or lane use.

If you drive a car

Know the numbers — they protect you

Most drivers don’t know the numbers — and the numbers are what protect you. In most states, fault in a crash is shared out between everyone involved, so a rider who splits outside the legal limits can carry a share of the blame — and that matters for insurance claims and tickets. A few states — Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, and Virginia — use a stricter rule where even a small share of fault can block a claim entirely. And the strongest record of what happened is a dash cam: it shows the rider’s speed and position better than any argument afterward.

What to do:

  1. Riders: stay in your lane. Splitting or filtering can get you ticketed, and it can count against you if there is a crash.
  2. Drivers: before changing lanes, check your mirrors and your blind spot — a motorcycle is small and easy to miss, legal or not.
  3. Drivers: if a rider hits you while splitting illegally, save any dash cam footage and note the time and place — it is the clearest record of what the rider was doing.
  4. If there is a crash: get the police report and talk to a lawyer before giving a recorded statement to an insurer — fault is often shared, and the details matter.

Nonconsensual intimate images & AI deepfakes in Mississippi

If someone shares, or threatens to share, an intimate image of you without your consent — a real photo or video, or an AI-generated fake that looks real — that is image-based sexual abuse, sometimes called “revenge porn.” It is not your fault, and you do not have to handle it alone. The law in this area is moving fast: most states now have a law against nonconsensual sharing, and a federal law now requires covered platforms to act quickly on removal requests. Work the steps below in order — preserve evidence first, then report and request removal, then consider criminal and civil routes — and get support early, because the process can take weeks.

The law now

State laws and the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act

Most states criminalize knowingly sharing an intimate image without consent, and many also allow the person depicted to sue for damages and a court order; some states also specifically cover AI-generated sexual deepfakes, and others reach them through the general nonconsensual-image law. The exact elements, penalties, and deadlines vary sharply by state, and this area changes fast, so this page does not print a single Mississippi statute or penalty — the Cyber Civil Rights Initiative (CCRI) Safety Center below maintains a list of state laws, and a lawyer or legal-aid office can confirm the current Mississippi law for your situation. Juvenile law & school-to-court issues in Mississippi

Juvenile court is not adult court with smaller defendants. It was built around rehabilitation rather than punishment, it uses different words, different hearings, and usually no jury, and a young person’s record is handled very differently from an adult’s. That difference is good news, but it is not automatic: records are not always sealed on their own, school discipline can still lead to court, and some serious cases can be moved to adult court. This section covers the map; the Child safety and Special education sections on this page cover abuse reporting and school disability rights, and this section does not repeat them.

How it differs

How juvenile court differs from adult court

In juvenile court, a young person is usually “taken into custody” rather than arrested, charged by petition rather than indictment, and “adjudicated delinquent” rather than convicted — the judge, not a jury, usually decides the case, in a hearing that is often closed to the public. The court’s dispositions lean toward supervision, counseling, community service, restitution, and placement in a juvenile facility rather than adult prison, and parents or guardians are usually required to attend and can be ordered to participate. Young people still have core rights — to notice of the charges, to a lawyer, to confront witnesses, and against self-incrimination — so if your child is questioned by police or school resource officers, the safest first move is to ask for a lawyer before answering questions, and to say so calmly and clearly. Age limits, the right to a jury, and the rules for detention all vary by state, so confirm the Mississippi rules with the juvenile court or a juvenile-defense lawyer rather than assuming another state’s rules apply.

Common matters

Common juvenile matters: delinquency, truancy, and status offenses

Delinquency means an act that would be a crime if an adult did it — theft, assault, drug offenses, vandalism. Status offenses are acts that are only offenses because of age: running away, truancy (unexcused school absence), curfew violations, and underage drinking in some states. Truancy and other status offenses usually start with school and family interventions, not jail, but repeated truancy can reach court, where the judge can order counseling, supervision, or services for the family — so answer school letters early, keep records of illness and appointments, and ask the school in writing what support it has offered before the case reaches a judge. Emancipation is a separate court process by which some minors can be declared legally independent adults before 18; it generally requires showing the young person can support themselves and manage their own affairs, it is not available in every state, and it ends parental support obligations in ways that should never be rushed into without legal advice.

School to court

When school discipline leads to court involvement

School discipline and court are connected in both directions: a suspension or expulsion hearing is a school process with its own notice and hearing rights (see the Special education section on this page for students with disabilities), while fights, threats, drug possession, or property damage at school can also be referred to police and juvenile court. If your child faces both, handle both tracks: respond to the school in writing by its deadline, keep every notice, and do not let the school process substitute for legal advice about the court case — statements made to a principal can be used later. Ask the school what alternatives it considered (restorative practices, counseling, behavior plans) and whether the referral to police was required or discretionary; that record matters to a juvenile judge deciding whether the case belongs in court at all. If your child has a disability, tell the school and the court in writing: federal disability law can change what discipline is lawful.

Records

Juvenile records: sealing and expungement are not automatic

A juvenile record is not one file: police reports, court files, probation records, and school records may each be held separately, and sealing one does not always seal the others. Sealing generally hides the record from the public and most employers, while law enforcement and courts may still see it; expungement generally erases or destroys it, where the state offers it. In many states neither happens automatically at 18 — someone must apply to the juvenile court after the case closes and any waiting period passes, and serious offenses or later adult convictions can block eligibility. The practical steps: ask the juvenile court clerk in the county where the case was heard for the sealing/expungement forms and the waiting period, confirm which agencies hold records, and get the order in writing; then check that employers’ background checks actually come back clean. The Clearing your criminal record section on this page covers adult records; juvenile sealing uses a different process and different forms, so do not file adult forms for a juvenile case.

Transferred cases

When a juvenile case can move to adult court

For the most serious offenses, and for older teens, Mississippi law may allow a prosecutor to ask a judge to transfer (waive) the case to adult court, or may require it for certain charges. Transfer is the highest-stakes decision in juvenile law: an adult conviction carries adult penalties and an adult record that is far harder to clear. If transfer is raised, get a juvenile-defense lawyer immediately, attend every hearing, and gather school records, counseling records, and evidence of rehabilitation — judges deciding transfer weigh the seriousness of the offense, the young person’s history, and whether juvenile services can still help. Do not discuss the facts of the case with police, school staff, or on social media while transfer is possible.

What to do:

  1. Questioned or taken into custody? Ask for a lawyer before answering questions; parents should attend every hearing.
  2. Truancy or status offense? Answer school letters early, keep illness/appointment records, and ask in writing what support the school offered.
  3. School discipline plus a court referral? Handle both tracks in writing; do not let school statements substitute for legal advice.
  4. Emancipation? Get legal advice first — it is not available everywhere and it ends parental support duties.
  5. Record still showing? Ask the juvenile court clerk for sealing/expungement forms and waiting periods; sealing one file may not seal them all.
  6. Transfer to adult court raised? Get a juvenile-defense lawyer at once and gather school and counseling records.

Special education & student rights in Mississippi

If your child has a disability that affects learning, two federal laws protect them at school. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) gives eligible children an Individualized Education Program (IEP) — specially designed instruction, written down, with goals the school must work toward. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act covers children who have a disability that substantially limits a major life activity but do not need specially designed instruction — they get a 504 plan of accommodations instead, like extra time, preferential seating, or a quiet room for tests. You do not have to know which one fits before you ask: ask for an evaluation in writing, and the school must consider it.

IEP

How an IEP works — and the federal clocks

Ask for an evaluation in writing, addressed to the principal or the district’s special education director, and keep a copy with the date. The school must get your written consent before it evaluates. Under the federal default, the evaluation is completed within 60 days of your consent — your state may set its own timeline, so ask what Mississippi’s is — and once your child is found eligible, the school must hold the IEP meeting within 30 days. The IEP is reviewed at least once a year, and your child must be reevaluated at least every 3 years. You are a member of the IEP team: you can bring someone with you, you can disagree, and you can ask for another meeting at any time.

504 plan

If an IEP is not the fit: a Section 504 plan

A 504 plan does not come with specially designed instruction, but it does require the school to remove barriers: accommodations in the regular classroom, changes to how work or tests are given, and access to school activities. Ask the school who its Section 504 coordinator is and put your request in writing, with a note from your child’s doctor if you have one. If the school refuses an evaluation or a plan, ask for that refusal in writing — a written refusal (called prior written notice) must explain why, and it starts your right to challenge it.

Discipline

School discipline: extra protections apply

A student with an IEP or 504 plan cannot simply be suspended or expelled like any other student. If the school wants a disciplinary change of placement lasting more than 10 school days in a row, the team — including you — must hold a manifestation determination review: was the behavior caused by, or directly related to, the disability, or did the school fail to carry out the IEP? If the answer to either is yes, the student generally must return to the placement, unless the case involves weapons, drugs, or serious bodily injury, which have their own rules. If your child is suspended, ask in writing on day one whether a manifestation review is required, and do not let the days run while you wait.

Disagreements

If you and the school disagree

Work up the ladder in this order, and keep everything in writing: first, ask the IEP team to meet again and say plainly what you want changed. Second, ask about mediation — a free, voluntary meeting with a neutral mediator that your state provides. Third, file a state complaint with Mississippi’s department of education (your PTI, linked above, gives you the current form and address) if the school broke a rule or missed a deadline. Fourth, a due process hearing, where a hearing officer decides — this is the formal route, and deadlines apply, so ask your PTI or free legal help right away rather than waiting. You can also file a complaint with the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights if you believe the school discriminated because of disability.

What to do:

  1. Put every request in writing — evaluation, meeting, change to the plan — and keep a dated copy. Spoken requests are easy for a school to lose.
  2. Call your PTI before the meeting, not after. It is free, and a short prep call changes how the meeting goes.
  3. Do not sign an IEP you disagree with on the spot. You can take it home, ask the PTI to review it, and reply in writing about what you accept and what you dispute.
  4. Track the clocks: 60 days (federal default) for the evaluation, 30 days to the IEP meeting after eligibility, a review every year, a reevaluation every 3 years — and ask what Mississippi’s own timelines are, since states may differ.
  5. If discipline starts, act on day one: ask in writing whether a manifestation determination review is required, and keep a log of every school day missed.

Disaster & insurance recovery in Mississippi

After a storm, fire, or flood, the order of operations matters more than speed: stay safe, document everything before you clean up, file the insurance claim promptly, and only then argue about the amount. First 24 hours

Safety and documentation first — before you clean up

Do not re-enter a damaged home until authorities say it is safe, and do not touch downed power lines, gas leaks, or floodwater, which can hide hazards and contamination. Once it is safe, photograph and video every room and every damaged item before you move, clean, or throw anything away — insurers and FEMA decide from photos, not from memory. Make a room-by-room list with approximate purchase dates and values, save receipts for emergency repairs and temporary housing, and keep a log of every call (date, name, what was promised). If you must make emergency repairs to prevent further damage — tarping a roof, shutting off water — do so, keep the receipts, and photograph the damage first: your policy usually requires you to mitigate further damage, but it does not require you to repair everything before the adjuster sees it.

Neighbor & HOA disputes in Mississippi

Most neighbor fights are not about the law — they are about a fence, a tree, a dog, or a dues bill, and about two people who stopped talking. The law still matters, because it decides who pays and who has to stop, but the cheapest wins in this section come before any courtroom: a survey, a dated letter, and a paper trail. Work the ladder in order — talk, letter, mediation, small claims — and do not skip steps, because a judge will ask what you tried first. The Deed fraud section on this page covers title and ownership scams; this section covers the everyday disputes between people who live next to each other.

Property lines

Property lines and surveys: never argue from a fence line

Fences, hedges, and driveways are often not on the real boundary — they were put up by guesswork years ago. Before you argue, or build, get the facts: your deed and plat map from the county recorder’s office, and, if the line is genuinely disputed, a licensed land surveyor’s survey. A survey costs money, but it ends arguments that a fence never will, and courts treat a surveyor’s markers as strong evidence. Do not move, remove, or destroy survey markers — tampering with them is illegal in many states. If the survey shows your neighbor’s fence, shed, or driveway is on your land, talk first and put any agreement in writing; if they refuse to move it, that written record and the survey are what a court or mediator will want to see.

Fences

Fences: who pays for a boundary fence depends on your state

Some states require neighbors to share the cost of a boundary fence equally; others put the cost on the person who wants it, or have special fence laws that apply mainly to farmland and livestock; and many cities add their own height, setback, and permit rules on top. There is no single national rule, so do not quote one to your neighbor — check Mississippi law and your city or county fence ordinance before you demand half the cost or refuse to pay it. Practical rules that hold almost everywhere: get any cost-sharing agreement in writing before work starts, keep the contractor’s invoice and your payment proof, and do not build on a disputed line without a survey. If a fence is dangerous — leaning, broken, electrified without warning — report it to code enforcement as well as writing to the owner.

Trees

Trees: overhanging branches, fallen trees, and who pays

The general principles courts commonly apply are these, with state-by-state variations: you may usually trim branches and roots that cross onto your property, up to the property line, but you may not trespass onto your neighbor’s land to do it, and you must not cut so much that you kill or destabilize the tree — killing a neighbor’s tree can make you liable for its full value. If a healthy tree falls in a storm, the damage usually falls where it lands, and each owner’s insurance handles their own property; but if the tree was dead, diseased, or visibly hazardous and the owner knew or should have known, the owner may be liable for the damage. The move that protects you in both cases is a dated written notice: if a neighbor’s tree looks dead or dangerous, write to them (keep a copy) and to your city’s tree or code office before it falls, so “nobody told me” is no longer available.

Noise & nuisance

Noise and nuisance: document it, then report it

Noise limits, quiet hours, barking-dog rules, and junk or odor complaints are almost always set by city or county ordinance, not state law — so the first step is finding your local rule, usually through your city or county code-enforcement office or animal control. A complaint without a record goes nowhere: keep a dated log (date, time, duration, what happened), save any recordings or photos, and keep copies of every message you sent the neighbor. Report in writing, not just by phone, and keep the complaint number. If the problem is a rental, write to the landlord or property manager too — landlords can often fix a tenant problem faster than a city can. Repeated, documented violations are what get fines issued and what win in mediation or court; a single angry call is not.

HOA

HOA fines: your rights are in the documents — read them before you pay or fight

An HOA or condo association can fine you only for rules it actually has the power to enforce, and that power comes from three places: the declaration and bylaws (the CC&Rs), the association’s rules, and Mississippi’s HOA or condo statute, which you can find through the state legislature’s website. In many states you have a right to a hearing before a fine becomes final, and a right to see the association’s records — budgets, meeting minutes, and the violation file on your home. If you get a fine: ask in writing for the exact rule you allegedly broke, the evidence, and the hearing procedure; attend the hearing and bring your evidence; and do not stop paying regular dues to protest a fine unless a lawyer tells you to — unpaid dues can lead to late fees and, in many states, a lien on your home, which is a far bigger problem than the fine. If the hearing fails, mediation or small claims court (below) is usually the next step, not ignoring the letters.

Escalation

The escalation ladder: talk, letter, mediation, small claims

Work it in order. Talk: calmly, once, with a specific ask and a deadline (“Can we split the fence repair by the end of the month?”). Letter: if talking fails, send a dated letter — by email plus certified mail for anything expensive — stating the facts, the rule or survey, what you want, and a deadline; keep a copy. Mediation: many courts and communities offer low-cost mediation, where a neutral mediator helps you reach an agreement you both sign; it is faster and cheaper than court and it preserves the relationship you still have to live next to. Small claims: if the other side will not talk or mediate, file in small claims court for the money you can prove. Going straight to court without the earlier steps does not help your case — judges notice who tried to settle.

Public benefits & appeals in Mississippi

A letter that cuts, denies, or reduces your benefits is not the final word. SNAP (food assistance), Medicaid, SSI, and SSDI all give you a right to appeal — usually called a fair hearing for SNAP and Medicaid, and a reconsideration and hearing for Social Security. The deadlines are the whole game: miss one and you may have to start over. Read every notice the day it arrives, circle the deadline and the date the change takes effect, and file before that effective date if you can — that is often what keeps your benefits flowing while you appeal.

SNAP

SNAP: ask for a fair hearing within 90 days

If Mississippi denies your SNAP application, cuts your benefit, or says you were overpaid, you can ask for a fair hearing within 90 days of the agency’s action. At the hearing you can bring evidence, bring witnesses, and have someone speak for you, including free legal help. If you ask before the cut takes effect, your benefits usually stay at the old level while the appeal is pending — but if the agency wins, you may owe back the extra amount, so ask about that risk when you file.

Medicaid

Medicaid: fair hearing — the notice controls the deadline

Federal rules give you up to 90 days from the date the Medicaid notice is mailed to ask for a fair hearing, but some states set shorter deadlines — as few as 30 days. The deadline printed on your notice is the one that counts. If you are already getting a service and you ask before the effective date on the notice, your coverage or service usually continues while the appeal is pending. If you are in a Medicaid managed care plan, you usually appeal to the plan first, and if the plan says no, you can then ask for a state fair hearing.

Social Security

SSI / SSDI: 60 days at every stage

Social Security gives you 60 days to appeal at each stage, and it assumes you got the notice 5 days after the date printed on it. The stages, in order, are: reconsideration (a fresh reviewer looks at your file), a hearing before an administrative law judge, Appeals Council review, and then federal court. Appeal online or with the paper forms — ask for reconsideration with Form SSA-561 and for a hearing with Form HA-501. If Social Security says your disability has ended, ask in writing within 10 days of that notice if you want your SSI payments to continue during the appeal.

This section is general information for people starting a small business — a different audience from most of this site, but the same plain-language, verified-links approach. Starting a business is mostly paperwork done in the right order: pick a structure, register it, get your tax IDs, check licenses, and put agreements in writing before money changes hands. The details, fees, and forms are set by Mississippi and by your city or county, so this page gives the national steps and the official places to check, and does not print a single Mississippi fee or form number that it has not verified.

Pick a structure first: sole proprietorship, LLC, partnership, corporation

The structure decides your personal liability, your taxes, and your paperwork. A sole proprietorship is the simplest — you and the business are the same legal person, so you are personally liable for its debts. An LLC (limited liability company) separates your personal assets from the business’s debts in most cases, with simpler paperwork than a corporation. A partnership splits ownership between two or more people and needs a written partnership agreement even when the law does not require one. A corporation offers the strongest separation but the most formalities (directors, bylaws, annual filings). There is no universally best choice: liability, taxes, and how many owners you have should drive it, and a tax adviser or small-business counselor can help you compare them for your situation before you file anything.

LLC formation

Forming an LLC in Mississippi: the general steps

The usual sequence is: (1) choose and check a business name against Mississippi’s business registry so it is not already taken; (2) name a registered agent with a physical address in Mississippi to receive legal papers; (3) file articles of organization (the name varies by state) with Mississippi’s secretary of state or equivalent business-filing office, usually online; (4) pay the state filing fee, which varies widely by state and is not printed here because it changes and was not verified for all 50 states in this section; (5) write an operating agreement, even if Mississippi does not require filing it — it settles who owns what, who decides what, and what happens if an owner leaves; and (6) keep the business’s money separate from your own from day one. File through the secretary of state’s own site, not a paid middleman site that charges extra for the same filing: search “Mississippi secretary of state business filings” and check that the site is the official .gov address before paying.

Tax IDs: get an EIN free from the IRS

An Employer Identification Number (EIN) is a nine-digit tax ID the IRS assigns to a business; you need one if you hire employees, operate as a corporation or partnership, file certain tax returns, or open a business bank account (many banks require one). Apply online directly from the IRS — it is free, and you will usually get the number immediately on completion. Any site charging a fee for an EIN is selling you a free government service. Keep the IRS confirmation letter: banks, licensing offices, and tax filings will ask for it. Separately, check whether Mississippi or your city requires a state tax ID or sales-tax registration — that is a different registration from the federal EIN, handled by Mississippi’s tax or revenue department.

Contracts

Simple contracts: what a solid written agreement needs

Handshake deals fail exactly when they matter, so put it in writing before work starts. A solid simple agreement names the parties, describes the work or goods precisely, states the price and payment schedule, sets start and finish dates, says who owns the work product, covers changes (written change orders, not verbal), and states how disputes are handled and how either side can end the deal. Both sides sign and date it, and each keeps a copy. For partnerships and multi-owner LLCs, the operating or partnership agreement is the contract that matters most: it should cover ownership percentages, contributions, decision rules, what happens if an owner dies or leaves, and how the business is valued. Template contracts from the internet are a starting point, not a substitute for advice on a high-value or long-term deal — have a lawyer review anything that could sink the business if it goes wrong.

Licenses

Business licenses and permits: where to check

There is no single national business license. Check three levels: (1) Mississippi — the secretary of state’s business office and the state tax/revenue department for state registration and tax permits; (2) your city or county — the clerk’s or licensing office for a general business license and any home-occupation permit if you work from home; and (3) your industry — health, food, construction, childcare, and professional services are licensed by separate state boards with their own exams and requirements. Also check zoning before signing a lease: a business that is legal in one part of town may not be permitted in another, and a home-based business may need a home-occupation permit even if no customers visit. The SBA’s guide above and your local Small Business Development Center (free counseling, found through the SBA site) can walk you through the licenses for your specific business.

Traps

Common first-timer traps

Mixing personal and business money (open a business account and pay yourself properly); signing a commercial lease or a personal guarantee without reading the term, renewal, and exit clauses; hiring a first worker as a “contractor” when the law treats them as an employee, which creates payroll-tax liability; skipping insurance (general liability at minimum, plus professional liability or workers’ compensation where required); ignoring sales tax on what you sell; and paying a middleman for a free government filing (EIN, and in many states the basic registration). Each of these is cheaper to fix before it happens than after, and each is a reason to spend one session with a small-business counselor or accountant before launch.

What to do:

  1. Pick a structure. Compare liability and taxes (sole proprietorship vs. LLC vs. partnership vs. corporation) before you file.
  2. Form it properly. Check the name, name a registered agent, file articles of organization with Mississippi’s secretary of state, and write an operating agreement.
  3. Get an EIN free from the IRS. Apply on irs.gov directly; never pay a middleman for it.
  4. Check licenses at three levels: state, city/county, and your industry board — plus zoning before you sign a lease.
  5. Put agreements in writing. Scope, price, dates, ownership, changes, and exit terms, signed by both sides.
  6. Separate the money and get insured. Business bank account from day one; liability insurance and workers’ compensation where required.
