Lemon law in Vermont

Bought a new car that's constantly in the shop? Vermont's lemon law may get you a refund or a replacement.

Coverage

Which vehicles qualify

new vehicles

Threshold

When it's legally a lemon

3+ repair attempts (first during warranty) or 30+ calendar days out of service during the express warranty term

Remedy

What you get

refund or replacement vehicle, at the consumer's option

Tenant rights in Vermont

Renting comes with rights most people never hear about until something goes wrong. Here are the key ones in Vermont.

Eviction

Notice before eviction

14-day notice to pay or quit for nonpayment; 60 days to end a month-to-month tenancy without cause (90 days if tenancy lasted 2+ years)

Security deposit

Deposit limits & return

No statewide cap; return within 14 days; written itemized statement required

Repairs

When the landlord won't fix things

May repair minor defects and deduct up to half a month's rent after 30 days' written notice; rent withholding allowed for major code violations

Gun laws in Vermont

Gun laws vary a lot from state to state — what's perfectly legal in one state can be a crime in another. Here's the big picture for this state. Laws change often, so double-check anything before you act.

Concealed carry

Carrying a concealed handgun

Permitless carry — no permit required and Vermont has no permit system at all

Open carry

Carrying openly

Generally allowed

Buying a gun

Background checks & waiting periods

Private sales: Universal background checks required, including on private transfers Waiting period: 72 hours.

Red flag law

Extreme risk protection

Yes. Courts can temporarily order firearms removed from someone found to be a danger.

Out-of-state permits

Reciprocity

Vermont issues no permits; anyone who may lawfully possess a firearm may carry without one, so reciprocity is not a factor

Filming the police: your rights in Vermont

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (No state statute; one-party rule applies by default under federal law, 18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d))

Distance rules

Buffer-zone law — in effect

none in effect (a 25-foot first-responder buffer bill, H.697, was debated in early 2026 but held for further drafting, not enacted)

The Vermont Supreme Court in State v. Geraw (2002) barred secret recording where there is a reasonable expectation of privacy (e.g., inside a home), but on-duty police in public have no such expectation. The Vermont League of Cities and Towns advises that recording may take place in any public space, including police station lobbies.
