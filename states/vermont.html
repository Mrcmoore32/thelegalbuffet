Car accident injuries in Vermont

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for Vermont: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

Vermont’s system: Vermont is an at-fault state: the driver who caused the crash — and their insurance — is responsible for your injuries.

Deadline: In Vermont you generally have 3 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Whistleblower protection in Vermont

Fired, demoted, or punished for reporting something illegal or unsafe at work? The law may protect you — but deadlines can be as short as 30 days, so don’t wait.

Workplace safety: Federal law protects workers who report safety or health hazards. Complaints go to OSHA.

Financial fraud: The SEC runs a whistleblower program for securities fraud, with possible awards. The IRS has one for major tax fraud too.

Vermont: Vermont workers can report employment discrimination or other unfair employment practices to the Attorney General's Civil Rights Unit by phone, email, or online complaint form — the unit investigates and can go to court if it isn't resolved.

Bail & bail bondsmen in Vermont

Someone you love is sitting in jail and you need them out. Here's how bail works in Vermont.

Two ways to post bail: pay the full amount to the court yourself (you get it back when the case ends), or hire a bail bondsman — they post it for a fee, usually around 10% of the bail amount, which you do not get back.

Vermont specifics: Commercial bail bonding companies operate, but Vermont has no bail-agent licensing statute, so there is no state license lookup.

Protect yourself: get every fee in writing before paying, and never hand over cash without a receipt and paperwork.

Filming the police: your rights in Vermont

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (No state statute; one-party rule applies by default under federal law, 18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d))

Distance rules

Buffer-zone law — in effect

none in effect (a 25-foot first-responder buffer bill, H.697, was debated in early 2026 but held for further drafting, not enacted)

The Vermont Supreme Court in State v. Geraw (2002) barred secret recording where there is a reasonable expectation of privacy (e.g., inside a home), but on-duty police in public have no such expectation. The Vermont League of Cities and Towns advises that recording may take place in any public space, including police station lobbies.

Domestic abuse & protection orders in Vermont

If someone is hurting you or your kids, or threatening to, Vermont courts can order them to stay away. It’s called a Relief From Abuse (RFA) order.

How to get one: go to your county courthouse and ask for the protection-order paperwork — filing is free. A judge reviews it, usually the same day, and can grant a temporary order immediately. A hearing is scheduled within a couple of weeks where both sides can speak.

If you are in danger right now, call 911. For free confidential help anytime, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788.
