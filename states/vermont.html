What to do next:

  1. Make sure you meet the residency requirement listed above before you file.
  2. Get the court’s free forms or online filing tool (linked above) and fill them out completely.
  3. File with the district court in the county where either spouse lives, and pay the filing fee.

Car repossession in Vermont

If you're behind on car payments, know your rights before the tow truck shows up — and after.

Your rights

What they can't do

No breaking into a locked garage, no physical force, no threats — that's "breach of the peace" and it's illegal everywhere. They must return your personal belongings from the car.

Get it back

Cure and redemption

No state cure right — only what your loan contract allows. Pay the full balance plus costs any time before the car is sold.

Money owed

Deficiency balance

The lender can sue for the remaining balance after a fair sale.

Notice

What they must tell you

No advance warning required. Notice before the sale is required.

What to do next:

  1. If your car was taken, call the lender immediately and ask for the exact payoff or cure amount in writing.
  2. Remove valuables from your car now — don't wait for repossession.
  3. If the repo agent used force, threats, or broke into a locked space, document everything and contact free legal help in Vermont.

Medical malpractice in Vermont

A bad outcome is not always malpractice. To have a case, you usually must show the doctor or hospital fell below the accepted standard of care — what a careful provider would have done — and that the mistake caused your injury. These cases turn on strict deadlines and expert review, so the clock matters here more than almost anywhere else.

Take action

Complain to the Vermont Board of Medical Practice

Filing a complaint with the state medical board is free. The board can investigate and discipline a doctor, but it cannot award you money — that is what a lawsuit is for. Filing a complaint does not pause your lawsuit deadline.

File a complaint or look up a doctor →
Deadline

Vermont's deadline to sue

You generally have the later of 3 years from the incident or 2 years from when the injury was or reasonably should have been discovered — and never more than 7 years from the incident. Miss the deadline and your case is usually over, no matter how strong it is — talk to a lawyer well before it runs out.

Free help

Talk to a malpractice lawyer

Most malpractice lawyers give a free first consultation and work on contingency — you pay nothing up front, and they take a percentage only if you win. Many states also require an expert to certify the case before you can file; a lawyer handles that.

Your records

Get your medical records

You have the right to copies of your own medical records. Ask every provider involved, in writing, and keep the copies somewhere safe — records are the backbone of any malpractice case.

What to do next:

  1. Request complete copies of your medical records from every provider involved.
  2. Write down your timeline — dates, symptoms, what you were told — while it is fresh.
  3. Do not sign anything from the hospital or its insurer, and do not accept a quick settlement, before a lawyer looks at it.
  4. Get a free consultation with a malpractice attorney well before Vermont's deadline above.
  5. If you want the doctor investigated, file the free board complaint too — just remember it does not stop your lawsuit clock.

Wrongly accused in Vermont

Being accused of a crime you did not commit is frightening — but you have rights from the first minute. You are presumed innocent, you do not have to talk to police, and if you cannot afford a lawyer, the court must appoint one to defend you for free. What you do in the first days matters most.

Free help

Get a free defense lawyer

If you have been charged and cannot afford a lawyer, you are entitled to a free public defender. You usually apply through the court at your first appearance — the Vermont Office of the Defender General site explains how Vermont's system works and how to reach your local office.

Vermont Office of the Defender General →
Take action

Wrongfully convicted? Ask New England Innocence Project for a case review

The New England Innocence Project reviews wrongful-conviction cases across New England, including Vermont. If you or a loved one was wrongfully convicted in Vermont, you can ask them to review the case.

Learn how to ask for a case review →
Know your rights

Your first court appearance

At your first appearance (arraignment), the judge reads the charges and asks how you plead. Pleading not guilty keeps every option open — it does not lock you into a trial. This is also where you ask the court to appoint a public defender if you cannot afford a lawyer. Do not plead guilty just to get it over with.

What to do next:

  1. Do not talk to police without a lawyer. Say: “I am using my right to remain silent, and I want a lawyer” — then stop talking.
  2. Do not consent to searches of your home, car, or phone. Saying “I do not consent to a search” protects you later, even if police search anyway.
  3. Write down your timeline and where you were, plus the names of anyone who can confirm it, while it is fresh.
  4. Save everything — texts, emails, receipts, photos — and do not delete anything, even if it looks bad.
  5. Do not contact the person who accused you, and do not post about the case online.
  6. Ask the court for a public defender at your first appearance if you cannot afford a lawyer.

Immigration help in Vermont

Immigration law is mostly federal — the same rules apply in every state — but the help is local. The organizations below give free or low-cost immigration legal help in Vermont, and they are recognized to do this work. If money is tight, start there instead of paying someone who is not a lawyer.

Free help

Vermont Asylum Assistance Project helps people in Vermont with immigration cases — green cards, citizenship, asylum, deportation defense, and work permits. Call before your situation becomes an emergency; these offices are busy and waitlists are common.

Get immigration legal help →
Know your rights

If immigration officers stop you or come to your home

You have the right to remain silent, and you do not have to answer questions about where you were born. You do not have to open your door unless officers show a warrant signed by a judge — an ICE administrative warrant is not the same thing. Do not sign anything, and do not show false documents, before talking to a lawyer. Say: “I want to remain silent and I want to speak to a lawyer.”

Driver’s licenses

Can you get a license in Vermont?

Vermont lets residents get a driver’s license regardless of immigration status. Ask the legal help organization above which documents you will need to bring.

State resources

State resources for immigrants →

What to do next:

  1. Call the legal help organization above before any deadline or court date — immigration deadlines are strict and missing one can end a case.
  2. Beware of “notarios” and immigration consultants who are not lawyers or accredited representatives. In the U.S., a notario is not a lawyer, and bad paperwork help can get you deported.
  3. Gather your documents — passport, any papers you have ever received from immigration, court notices — and keep copies somewhere safe.
  4. Never sign a document you do not understand, and never pay in cash without a written agreement and a receipt.
  5. Write down important dates: entries, filings, hearings. Immigration cases turn on dates.

Wills & probate in Vermont

When someone dies, their property does not just move on its own. Probate is the court process that proves the will, pays the debts, and transfers what is left to the family. Every state also has a shortcut for small estates — and if you are making your own will, Vermont’s signing rules decide whether it counts. Here is where to start in Vermont.

Where probate happens

Vermont Judiciary — Estates and Wills

This is the official court starting point for wills and estates in Vermont — forms, filing steps, and the court that handles the estate.

Start with the probate court →
Small estates

Vermont’s small-estate shortcut

Estates of $45,000 or less — personal property only — can use Vermont’s small-estate procedure (14 V.S.A. §1901).

Making a will

Are handwritten wills valid in Vermont?

No. Vermont does not recognize unwitnessed handwritten wills. A valid will must be in writing, signed, and witnessed as the law requires (14 V.S.A. §5). A handwritten note left in a drawer will not control what happens to your property.

First steps

When someone dies: the first week

Order several certified copies of the death certificate — banks, insurers, and agencies each want one. Find the original will (check the home, a safe deposit box, and the court or lawyer who prepared it). Secure the home and vehicles. Do not pay the person’s debts from your own money — debts are paid from the estate, in an order the law sets. Notify Social Security and the person’s employer and insurers.

What to do next:

  1. Get certified death certificates and locate the original will.
  2. Check the estate’s size against Vermont’s small-estate shortcut above — it can save months and court fees.
  3. If the estate is larger, or includes a house or a dispute, open probate at the court above — and ask legal aid or an estate lawyer about a free first consultation.
  4. Do not distribute property, sell the car, or clean out accounts until the court says who has authority — handing things out early can make you personally responsible.
  5. Making your own will? Follow Vermont’s signing and witness rules exactly, tell your executor where the original is, and review it after marriages, divorces, births, and deaths.

Bankruptcy in Vermont

Bankruptcy is a legal way to wipe out or reorganize debts you cannot pay. Chapter 7 erases most unsecured debts like credit cards and medical bills, usually in a few months. Chapter 13 is a 3-to-5-year payment plan that can stop a foreclosure and let you catch up. The day you file, an “automatic stay” stops most collections, lawsuits, wage garnishments, and shutoffs. Some debts survive — most student loans, recent taxes, and child support among them.

Your court

The bankruptcy court for Vermont

Bankruptcy is a federal case — you file in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court, not a state court. Vermont is served by the court below; the court site has the forms, filing fees, courthouse locations, and local rules.

U.S. Bankruptcy Court — District of Vermont →
Keeping your home

How much home equity does Vermont protect?

Vermont protects $125,000 of home equity (a married couple cannot double it) (27 V.S.A. §101).

Your choice: Vermont lets you pick either the state exemption list or the federal exemption list — whichever protects more of what you own. The federal list protects about $31,575 of home equity per filer (adjusted for inflation), plus a wildcard amount that can cover more. A bankruptcy lawyer or legal aid can run both lists against your situation before you file.

Free help

Free bankruptcy guidance: Vermont Legal Aid / Legal Services Vermont

Vermont Legal Aid / Legal Services Vermont helps people in Vermont understand whether bankruptcy is the right move, and what the alternatives are. Many bankruptcy lawyers also offer a free first consultation — ask what the full cost will be before you hire anyone.

Get free bankruptcy guidance →
Before you file

Credit counseling is required

Within 180 days before filing, you must complete a short credit counseling course from an approved agency and file the certificate with the court — without it, your case can be dismissed. The U.S. Trustee Program keeps the official list of approved agencies.

Find an approved credit counseling agency →

What to do next:

  1. List everything: every debt, every asset, your income, and your monthly expenses. A lawyer or counselor can only help with the full picture.
  2. Get the free guidance above first — bankruptcy is powerful, but it stays on your credit report for up to 10 years, and sometimes a payment plan or negotiation works better.
  3. Stop using credit cards once you decide to file, and do not transfer or give away property to friends or family — the court can undo transfers and it can sink your case.
  4. Complete the required credit counseling and keep the certificate.
  5. Ask the court about the filing fee: if you cannot afford it, you can ask to pay in installments, or to have the Chapter 7 fee waived.

Workplace rights in Vermont

Two problems hit workers hardest: being treated unfairly on the job, and losing the job altogether. Both have agencies, both have deadlines, and both are free to use. Here is where each one goes in Vermont.

Discrimination

Filing a discrimination charge in Vermont

Two offices split the work. For most employers, the Attorney General’s Civil Rights Unit takes employment discrimination complaints. If you work for state government, your complaint goes to the Vermont Human Rights Commission. You have 1 year to file under Vermont law — but file within 300 days to protect a federal claim too.

Attorney General Civil Rights Unit → State employees: Human Rights Commission →
Unemployment

Lost your job? File for unemployment in Vermont

Unemployment benefits are run by the Vermont Department of Labor. File in your first week out of work — benefits do not backdate just because you waited, and most states make you certify every week to keep them coming. You will need your Social Security number, your employer’s details, and your recent work history.

File for unemployment benefits →
Pay & hours

Minimum wage, overtime, and unpaid pay

The federal floor is a $7.25 minimum wage and time-and-a-half after 40 hours in a week for most workers — and Vermont’s own wage law may promise more. If your pay itself is short, see the unpaid wages section on this page, and know that the U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division also takes complaints for free.

U.S. Department of Labor — Wage and Hour Division →
Retaliation

Punished for speaking up?

It is illegal for an employer to fire, demote, or cut the hours of a worker for filing a discrimination charge, reporting a safety problem, or asking about unpaid wages. If that happens, tell the agency you filed with right away — retaliation is its own violation, with its own deadline.

What to do next:

  1. Write down what happened, with dates, names, and witnesses, while it is fresh — and keep pay stubs, schedules, and messages somewhere your employer cannot delete them.
  2. Check the deadline above and file your charge before it runs out, even if you are still gathering documents.
  3. If you lost the job, file for unemployment the same week — then keep up the weekly certifications.
  4. Do not sign a severance agreement or a “resignation” letter under pressure. You can ask for time to have a lawyer or legal aid look at it first.
  5. If money is tight, ask legal aid about employment cases — many discrimination lawyers also offer a free first consultation.

Child custody & visitation in Vermont

When parents live apart, a court order is what turns an arrangement into something enforceable — who the child lives with, when each parent has them, and who decides about school and medical care. You do not need a lawyer to start, and in Vermont the courts publish the path. Here it is.

Where to start

Parental Rights and Responsibilities and Parent-Child Contact

This is the official court starting point for custody cases in Vermont — the process, the forms, and the terms the court uses.

Start with the court →
The words matter

What Vermont calls it

In Vermont, the courts call it parental rights and responsibilities, and parent-child contact (visitation). Whatever the label, the judge is deciding the same two things: who makes the big decisions for the child, and where the child lives and spends time.

Forms & parenting plans

Free forms and the parenting plan

The court offers free forms — including, in most states, a parenting plan you fill in with the schedule, holidays, and decision-making you are asking for. A complete, realistic plan is the single most persuasive thing you can file.

Safety

If a child is in danger

If a child is in immediate danger, call 911. Courts can issue emergency and temporary custody orders quickly when a child’s safety is at risk — ask the clerk how to request one. Many states also require or offer mediation before a custody hearing; it is cheaper and faster than a courtroom fight, and it does not require you to give up your rights.

What to do next:

  1. Gather the basics: the child’s birth certificate, any existing court orders, and school and medical information.
  2. Write down the schedule you are asking for — school weeks, weekends, holidays, summers — and how it serves the child. Judges decide on the child’s best interests, not on which parent argues best.
  3. File in the court for the county where the child lives, using the court’s own forms and terms above.
  4. Do not withhold the child, move away, or skip a court date out of frustration — each one damages your case.
  5. If you cannot afford a lawyer, ask the court about legal aid and filing-fee waivers — custody help is one of the most common things legal aid offices handle.

Voting rights in Vermont

Voting rules are set by each state, and the two that trip people up are ID and registration deadlines. Here is exactly what Vermont’s own elections officials say — plus what to do if something goes wrong at the polls.

Start here

Voter Registration

Vermont’s official voter page: check your registration, register or update your information, and find where and when to vote. Not sure of your state? Vote.gov routes every voter to the right state.

Go to Vermont’s official voter page →
At the polls

What ID do you need in Vermont?

First-time voters registering by mail or online must include a photocopy of an acceptable form of ID — a valid photo ID such as a driver’s license or passport, a current utility bill, a current bank statement, or another government document.

Deadlines

Registration deadlines in Vermont

There is no deadline — eligible persons may register to vote on any day up to and including the day of the election.

Problems at the polls

If you are turned away

Do not just leave. If officials say you are not on the list or your ID is not accepted, ask for a provisional (or affidavit) ballot — federal law gives you that right, and your vote counts once your registration is confirmed. Then call your county elections office or the official voter page above and report what happened. If you believe your rights were violated, you can also call the national, nonpartisan Election Protection hotline at 866-OUR-VOTE (866-687-8683).

What to do next:

  1. Check your registration on the official page above now — not the week of the election — and fix any problem while there is time.
  2. Check the ID rule for Vermont above and get the document in hand early; several states give free voter ID cards to people who ask.
  3. Find your polling place and its hours, or vote early or by mail if your state offers it and your schedule is tight.
  4. If anything goes wrong on the day, ask for a provisional ballot, write down the names of the officials involved, and report it.

Utility shutoffs in Vermont

A shutoff notice is not the end of the story. Most states restrict when a utility can turn off your heat or power — especially in winter — and there is a federally funded program in every state that helps pay the bill. Here is Vermont’s rule, the help, and where to complain.

Winter protection

Vermont’s shutoff rule

Winter period, November 1–March 31: gas and electric utilities must meet extra contact and notice steps before any residential disconnection, and service to households with any member aged 62 or older must not be disconnected if outdoor temperatures are forecast to fall below 32°F during the 48-hour period beginning on the morning of the anticipated disconnection (the household must give advance written notice of eligibility) (PUC Rule 3.300).

Help paying

Energy assistance: Seasonal Fuel Assistance (Vermont's LIHEAP Fuel Assistance), Dept. for Children and Families

The federal energy assistance program (LIHEAP, under its Vermont name above) helps low-income households pay heating and cooling bills and can cover crisis reconnections. Apply as early as you can — in many states, being approved for assistance is itself what switches the winter protection on, and the money runs out each season.

Apply for energy assistance →
Complaints

If the utility will not work with you: Vermont Public Utility Commission

The Vermont Public Utility Commission regulates utilities in Vermont and takes consumer complaints. Call them at 1-800-622-4496. Filing a complaint often pauses a shutoff while the regulator reviews it — file before the shutoff date, not after.

File a utility complaint →
Right now

Got a shutoff notice?

Call the utility before the date on the notice and ask for a payment plan — get the agreement in writing, with the amount and dates. If anyone in the home is seriously ill or depends on medical equipment, say so on that call and ask about a medical certificate or medical hold. Dialing 211 connects you to local help (charities, churches, and emergency funds) in most of the country.

What to do next:

  1. Find the shutoff date on your notice — everything else is scheduled backwards from that date.
  2. Call the utility and set up a payment plan; in several states the plan itself is your legal protection. Write down the name of the person you spoke with.
  3. Apply for energy assistance above right away — approval can pause or prevent the shutoff.
  4. If the utility will not budge, file the complaint above before the shutoff date.
  5. Never ignore a notice because you cannot pay in full — every protection on this page starts with you contacting someone.

Financial scams in Vermont

Scammers are professional liars, and they are good at it — Americans lose billions of dollars to fraud every year, and most of it is never reported. Falling for one is not a character flaw; these crews run scripts tested on thousands of people. The good news: almost every financial scam shares the same handful of tells, and once you know them, the spell breaks.

The golden rule: anyone who pressures you to pay right now with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, a cash app, or cash is running a scam — full stop. No real agency, bank, utility, or business demands payment that way. The second someone insists on secrecy (“don’t tell your family or your bank”), hang up. Urgency plus secrecy plus a strange payment method is the whole game.

Know the signs

Government impostors

A call, text, or email claims to be the IRS, Social Security, Medicare, or the sheriff: you owe money, your benefits will stop, or there is a warrant for your arrest. Real agencies almost always contact you by mail first, never threaten arrest over the phone, and never ask for gift cards or wire transfers. Hang up and call the agency yourself at the number on its official website or your paperwork.

Know the signs

The family emergency — now with cloned voices

“Grandma, I’m in jail, I need bail money — please don’t tell Mom.” Scammers can now clone a loved one’s voice from a few seconds of online audio, so it can sound exactly like them. Defenses that work: agree on a family code word in advance, and always hang up and call your loved one back on the number you already have. Real trouble survives a callback; a scam can’t.

Know the signs

Romance and investment scams

An online sweetheart who can never meet — and eventually needs money — is a script, not a relationship. So is the friendly stranger pitching can’t-miss crypto or investment returns, sometimes showing you a fake account that looks like it is growing (“pig butchering”). Guaranteed high returns with no risk do not exist. If you have to pay fees or taxes before you can withdraw your own money, it was never real.

Know the signs

Fake checks, phishing, and tech support

A check that overpays you — for a car, a job, a prize — with a request to send part back is counterfeit; it bounces days later and you owe the bank. Texts and emails about a “locked account” or a “missed delivery” are phishing for your logins — never use their link; type the company’s address yourself. And a pop-up warning that your computer has a virus, with a number to call, is fake: real companies never do that.

Free help lines

Anyone, any age, can call the AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline: 1-877-908-3360 (Mon–Fri, 8 a.m.–8 p.m. ET) — free, member or not, for advice on a suspicious call or support after a loss.

Already paid or shared information? Act in this order:

  1. Call your bank or card issuer right now — use the number on your card or statement, not one the scammer gave you. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment. Wires and transfers have short rescue windows; hours matter.
  2. Paid by gift card? Call the company on the back of the card immediately and say it was a scam — if the money has not been drained yet, they can sometimes freeze it. Keep the cards and receipts.
  3. Shared personal information (Social Security number, passwords, account logins)? Go to IdentityTheft.gov for a step-by-step recovery plan, and place a free credit freeze with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion so no one can open accounts in your name.
  4. Change the passwords on any account you touched or mentioned, starting with email and banking, and turn on two-factor authentication.
  5. Report it — to Vermont’s consumer office and the FTC (links above) — and tell your family. Silence is the scammer’s best protection; a report is how the next victim gets warned.

What to bring to court in Vermont

Walking in prepared makes the day go smoother and helps the judge see your case clearly. Use this as a packing list — a starting point, not legal advice. Your court or your lawyer may ask for more.

  1. Your photo ID and your case number. Courts run on case numbers — bring the one from your court papers.
  2. Copies of every document you filed, plus two extra sets. One set for the judge, one for the other side, one for yourself.
  3. Evidence that backs your claims, in order. Dated messages, receipts, photos, statements — a paper trail beats memory. Bring originals where you have them.
  4. A one-page timeline. What happened, in date order, and who was there. Judges love timelines.
  5. Financial records if money is at issue. Pay stubs, bank statements, bills, tax returns — bring more than you think you’ll need.

Tenant rights in Vermont

Renting comes with rights most people never hear about until something goes wrong. Here are the key ones in Vermont.

Before you sign a lease

A lease is a contract. These checks take a few minutes before you sign and can protect your money, your home, and your security deposit later. Lease rules vary by state and sometimes by city, so use the Vermont tenant resources and official link in this section to check the exact rule.

  1. Read it before you sign. Read every page, including addendums and rules. Never sign a lease with blank spaces: cross out a blank that does not apply or write “N/A,” and have both sides initial any handwritten change.
  2. Get every promise in writing. A promise about repairs, appliances, parking, storage, or the move-in date is hard to enforce if it is only spoken. If it matters, put it in the lease or in a signed addendum.
  3. Question clauses that take away basic protections. In many states, clauses like these are illegal or unenforceable: giving up your right to an itemized security-deposit statement; letting the landlord enter at any time without notice; giving up notice or your right to go to court before an eviction; renewing the lease automatically with a large penalty that is not spelled out clearly up front; or making you pay for all repairs, including major systems. Check Vermont’s rules with the tenant resource in this section, and ask for a problem clause to be removed or changed before you sign.
  4. Know the money up front. Add up the full move-in cost: first month’s rent, the security deposit, and every fee. The lease should say which charges are a refundable deposit and which are nonrefundable fees, when rent is due, the exact late fee, and how rent must be paid.
  5. Document the home on move-in day. Photograph or video every room and every flaw, including walls, floors, windows, and appliances. Write existing damage on a move-in checklist, date it, and keep a copy; if the landlord gives you a form, return it on time and keep your dated copy. That record helps protect your deposit when you move out.
  6. Get your complete copy. Leave with a signed copy of the lease and every addendum, plus the landlord’s legal name and a mailing address for repair requests and legal notices. Do not rely on a promise to send your copy later.
  7. Spell out who pays for what. Check who is responsible for utilities, lawn care, snow removal, and supplying and repairing appliances. If the lease does not say, ask before you sign and get the answer written in.

Motorcycle lane splitting in Vermont

Lane splitting is a motorcycle riding between lanes of traffic. Lane filtering is the narrower version — moving between stopped or very slow cars, usually to the front at a light. Only six states allow either one, and every one of them puts strict speed limits on it. Two motorcycles riding side by side in one lane is different — that is lane sharing, and it is legal almost everywhere.

Not allowed

The rule in Vermont

Lane splitting and lane filtering are not allowed in Vermont. Vermont law (Vt. Stat. Ann. tit. 23, § 1038) gives motorcycles full use of a lane and bars a rider from passing another vehicle in the same lane or riding between lanes of traffic. A rider who does it can be ticketed for improper passing or lane use.

If you drive a car

Know the numbers — they protect you

Most drivers don’t know the numbers — and the numbers are what protect you. In most states, fault in a crash is shared out between everyone involved, so a rider who splits outside the legal limits can carry a share of the blame — and that matters for insurance claims and tickets. A few states — Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, and Virginia — use a stricter rule where even a small share of fault can block a claim entirely. And the strongest record of what happened is a dash cam: it shows the rider’s speed and position better than any argument afterward.

What to do:

  1. Riders: stay in your lane. Splitting or filtering can get you ticketed, and it can count against you if there is a crash.
  2. Drivers: before changing lanes, check your mirrors and your blind spot — a motorcycle is small and easy to miss, legal or not.
  3. Drivers: if a rider hits you while splitting illegally, save any dash cam footage and note the time and place — it is the clearest record of what the rider was doing.
  4. If there is a crash: get the police report and talk to a lawyer before giving a recorded statement to an insurer — fault is often shared, and the details matter.

This is general information, not legal advice. Traffic laws change, and the exact wording of your state’s statute controls.

Special education & student rights in Vermont

If your child has a disability that affects learning, two federal laws protect them at school. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) gives eligible children an Individualized Education Program (IEP) — specially designed instruction, written down, with goals the school must work toward. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act covers children who have a disability that substantially limits a major life activity but do not need specially designed instruction — they get a 504 plan of accommodations instead, like extra time, preferential seating, or a quiet room for tests. You do not have to know which one fits before you ask: ask for an evaluation in writing, and the school must consider it.

IEP

How an IEP works — and the federal clocks

Ask for an evaluation in writing, addressed to the principal or the district’s special education director, and keep a copy with the date. The school must get your written consent before it evaluates. Under the federal default, the evaluation is completed within 60 days of your consent — your state may set its own timeline, so ask what Vermont’s is — and once your child is found eligible, the school must hold the IEP meeting within 30 days. The IEP is reviewed at least once a year, and your child must be reevaluated at least every 3 years. You are a member of the IEP team: you can bring someone with you, you can disagree, and you can ask for another meeting at any time.

504 plan

If an IEP is not the fit: a Section 504 plan

A 504 plan does not come with specially designed instruction, but it does require the school to remove barriers: accommodations in the regular classroom, changes to how work or tests are given, and access to school activities. Ask the school who its Section 504 coordinator is and put your request in writing, with a note from your child’s doctor if you have one. If the school refuses an evaluation or a plan, ask for that refusal in writing — a written refusal (called prior written notice) must explain why, and it starts your right to challenge it.

Discipline

School discipline: extra protections apply

A student with an IEP or 504 plan cannot simply be suspended or expelled like any other student. If the school wants a disciplinary change of placement lasting more than 10 school days in a row, the team — including you — must hold a manifestation determination review: was the behavior caused by, or directly related to, the disability, or did the school fail to carry out the IEP? If the answer to either is yes, the student generally must return to the placement, unless the case involves weapons, drugs, or serious bodily injury, which have their own rules. If your child is suspended, ask in writing on day one whether a manifestation review is required, and do not let the days run while you wait.

Disagreements

If you and the school disagree

Work up the ladder in this order, and keep everything in writing: first, ask the IEP team to meet again and say plainly what you want changed. Second, ask about mediation — a free, voluntary meeting with a neutral mediator that your state provides. Third, file a state complaint with Vermont’s department of education (your PTI, linked above, gives you the current form and address) if the school broke a rule or missed a deadline. Fourth, a due process hearing, where a hearing officer decides — this is the formal route, and deadlines apply, so ask your PTI or free legal help right away rather than waiting. You can also file a complaint with the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights if you believe the school discriminated because of disability.

What to do:

  1. Put every request in writing — evaluation, meeting, change to the plan — and keep a dated copy. Spoken requests are easy for a school to lose.
  2. Call your PTI before the meeting, not after. It is free, and a short prep call changes how the meeting goes.
  3. Do not sign an IEP you disagree with on the spot. You can take it home, ask the PTI to review it, and reply in writing about what you accept and what you dispute.
  4. Track the clocks: 60 days (federal default) for the evaluation, 30 days to the IEP meeting after eligibility, a review every year, a reevaluation every 3 years — and ask what Vermont’s own timelines are, since states may differ.
  5. If discipline starts, act on day one: ask in writing whether a manifestation determination review is required, and keep a log of every school day missed.

Public benefits & appeals in Vermont

A letter that cuts, denies, or reduces your benefits is not the final word. SNAP (food assistance), Medicaid, SSI, and SSDI all give you a right to appeal — usually called a fair hearing for SNAP and Medicaid, and a reconsideration and hearing for Social Security. The deadlines are the whole game: miss one and you may have to start over. Read every notice the day it arrives, circle the deadline and the date the change takes effect, and file before that effective date if you can — that is often what keeps your benefits flowing while you appeal.

SNAP

SNAP: ask for a fair hearing within 90 days

If Vermont denies your SNAP application, cuts your benefit, or says you were overpaid, you can ask for a fair hearing within 90 days of the agency’s action. At the hearing you can bring evidence, bring witnesses, and have someone speak for you, including free legal help. If you ask before the cut takes effect, your benefits usually stay at the old level while the appeal is pending — but if the agency wins, you may owe back the extra amount, so ask about that risk when you file.

Medicaid

Medicaid: fair hearing — the notice controls the deadline

Federal rules give you up to 90 days from the date the Medicaid notice is mailed to ask for a fair hearing, but some states set shorter deadlines — as few as 30 days. The deadline printed on your notice is the one that counts. If you are already getting a service and you ask before the effective date on the notice, your coverage or service usually continues while the appeal is pending. If you are in a Medicaid managed care plan, you usually appeal to the plan first, and if the plan says no, you can then ask for a state fair hearing.

Social Security

SSI / SSDI: 60 days at every stage

Social Security gives you 60 days to appeal at each stage, and it assumes you got the notice 5 days after the date printed on it. The stages, in order, are: reconsideration (a fresh reviewer looks at your file), a hearing before an administrative law judge, Appeals Council review, and then federal court. Appeal online or with the paper forms — ask for reconsideration with Form SSA-561 and for a hearing with Form HA-501. If Social Security says your disability has ended, ask in writing within 10 days of that notice if you want your SSI payments to continue during the appeal.

Free legal help with a benefits appeal

You do not need a lawyer for a fair hearing, but free legal aid offices handle these appeals every day and often take them at no cost — they know the hearing officers, the forms, and the evidence that wins. See the “Free or low-cost legal help” section on this page and call as soon as the notice arrives, because the deadline does not wait for a callback.

Free or low-cost legal help on this page →

What to do:

  1. Read the notice the day it arrives. Circle the effective date and the appeal deadline. If the notice and anything else disagree, trust the notice and call the agency the same day.
  2. File before the effective date if benefits you already get are being cut. That is usually what keeps them flowing while the appeal is pending.
  3. File the way the notice says — online portal, mail, fax, or in person — and keep a copy of everything plus proof you sent it (a confirmation number, a receipt, or a photo of the envelope).
  4. Gather your proof: pay stubs, bank statements, medical records, and every letter the agency sent you. Write down what changed and when.
  5. If the deadline has already passed, still ask in writing for a late filing and explain why you were late. Do not assume it is over until the agency answers.

This page does not list one Vermont appeal deadline because the deadline depends on the program and on the date printed on your notice — the notice controls. This is general information, not legal advice. Benefit rules change, and the agency’s written notice and your state’s current rules control.

What to do next:

  1. If a collector contacts you, ask for written validation of the debt before you pay anything.
  2. Collectors can’t harass you — keep a log of every call with dates and times.
  3. Call the CFPB number above, or find a nonprofit credit counselor for a free review of your options.
