Tenant rights in Maryland

Renting comes with rights most people never hear about until something goes wrong. Here are the key ones in Maryland.

Eviction

Notice before eviction

30 days to end month-to-month; 10-day notice for nonpayment

Security deposit

Deposit limits & return

Max 1 month's rent (2 months if utility agreement in writing); returned within 45 days with itemized list

Repairs

When the landlord won't fix things

No repair-and-deduct statute; rent escrow through district court allowed

Gun laws in Maryland

Gun laws vary a lot from state to state — what's perfectly legal in one state can be a crime in another. Here's the big picture for this state. Laws change often, so double-check anything before you act.

Concealed carry

Carrying a concealed handgun

Permit required (Wear and Carry Permit), shall-issue post-Bruen, 21+

Open carry

Carrying openly

Prohibited (permit holders must carry concealed)

Buying a gun

Background checks & waiting periods

Private sales: Required (universal; all transfers through licensed dealer) Waiting period: 7 days.

Red flag law

Extreme risk protection

Yes — (Extreme Risk Protective Orders, 2018). Courts can temporarily order firearms removed from someone found to be a danger.

Out-of-state permits

Reciprocity

None; does not recognize any out-of-state permits

Filming the police: your rights in Maryland

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

All-party consent

State law generally requires everyone recorded to consent — but on-duty officers in public have no reasonable expectation of privacy, so courts have held that recording them is protected. (Md. Code Ann., Cts. & Jud. Proc. § 10-402)

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Maryland has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

Maryland is all-party consent; recording audio without every party's consent is a felony. But case law holds the wiretap law does not cover on-duty officers in public: in Maryland v. Graber (Harford Co. Cir. Ct. 2010), felony wiretap charges against a motorcyclist who recorded a trooper at a traffic stop were dismissed because the officer had no reasonable expectation of privacy.
