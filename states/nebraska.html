Car accident injuries in Nebraska

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for Nebraska: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

Nebraska’s system: Nebraska is an at-fault state: the driver who caused the crash — and their insurance — is responsible for your injuries.

Deadline: In Nebraska you generally have 4 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Whistleblower protection in Nebraska

Fired, demoted, or punished for reporting something illegal or unsafe at work? The law may protect you — but deadlines can be as short as 30 days, so don’t wait.

Workplace safety: Federal law protects workers who report safety or health hazards. Complaints go to OSHA.

Financial fraud: The SEC runs a whistleblower program for securities fraud, with possible awards. The IRS has one for major tax fraud too.

Nebraska: Workers can start a complaint through the Nebraska Equal Opportunity Commission's "Start the Complaint Process" form; NEOC handles whistleblower and other retaliation cases, with most charges due within 300 days of the harm.

Bail & bail bondsmen in Nebraska

Someone you love is sitting in jail and you need them out. Here's how bail works in Nebraska.

Nebraska specifics: No commercial bail bondsmen — courts handle release directly, usually with a 10% cash deposit paid to the court clerk.

Be skeptical: commercial bondsmen barely operate in Nebraska, so treat anyone offering to post bail for a fee with suspicion — when in doubt, call the jail or courthouse directly.

Filming the police: your rights in Nebraska

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (Neb. Rev. Stat. § 86-290(2)(c))

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Nebraska has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

One-party consent state: a party to a conversation may record it without telling the others. A 2024 bill (LB1185) that would have required recorders to stay 10 feet from officers was 'indefinitely postponed' and never became law, so no buffer zone exists.

Domestic abuse & protection orders in Nebraska

If someone is hurting you or your kids, or threatening to, Nebraska courts can order them to stay away. It’s called a Domestic Abuse Protection Order.

How to get one: go to your county courthouse and ask for the protection-order paperwork — filing is free. A judge reviews it, usually the same day, and can grant a temporary order immediately. A hearing is scheduled within a couple of weeks where both sides can speak.

If you are in danger right now, call 911. For free confidential help anytime, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788.
