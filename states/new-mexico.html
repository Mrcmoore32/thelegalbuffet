Car repossession in New Mexico

If you're behind on car payments, know your rights before the tow truck shows up — and after.

Your rights

What they can't do

No breaking into a locked garage, no physical force, no threats — that's "breach of the peace" and it's illegal everywhere. They must return your personal belongings from the car.

Get it back

Cure and redemption

No state cure right — only what your loan contract allows. Pay the full balance plus costs any time before the car is sold.

Money owed

Deficiency balance

The lender can sue for the remaining balance after a fair sale.

Notice

What they must tell you

No advance warning required. Notice before the sale is required. (Only licensed repo companies may repossess — verify with the Financial Institutions Division.)

What to do next:

  1. If your car was taken, call the lender immediately and ask for the exact payoff or cure amount in writing.
  2. Remove valuables from your car now — don't wait for repossession.
  3. If the repo agent used force, threats, or broke into a locked space, document everything and contact free legal help in New Mexico.

What to bring to court in New Mexico

Walking in prepared makes the day go smoother and helps the judge see your case clearly. Use this as a packing list — a starting point, not legal advice. Your court or your lawyer may ask for more.

  1. Your photo ID and your case number. Courts run on case numbers — bring the one from your court papers.
  2. Copies of every document you filed, plus two extra sets. One set for the judge, one for the other side, one for yourself.
  3. Evidence that backs your claims, in order. Dated messages, receipts, photos, statements — a paper trail beats memory. Bring originals where you have them.
  4. A one-page timeline. What happened, in date order, and who was there. Judges love timelines.
  5. Financial records if money is at issue. Pay stubs, bank statements, bills, tax returns — bring more than you think you’ll need.
  6. A pen and paper. To write down the judge’s orders, next dates, and anything you’re told to do.

Before the day: call the courthouse (or check its website) to confirm the courtroom, the time, and anything you’re not sure about. Arrive 30 minutes early — you’ll go through security and need time to find the room. Leave anything distracting at home and silence your phone before you walk in.

Car accident injuries in New Mexico

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for New Mexico: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

New Mexico’s system: New Mexico is an at-fault state: the driver who caused the crash — and their insurance — is responsible for your injuries.

Deadline: In New Mexico you generally have 3 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Bail & bail bondsmen in New Mexico

Someone you love is sitting in jail and you need them out. Here's how bail works in New Mexico.

Two ways to post bail: pay the full amount to the court yourself (you get it back when the case ends), or hire a bail bondsman — they post it for a fee, usually around 10% of the bail amount, which you do not get back.

New Mexico specifics: Commercial bail bondsmen are licensed by the New Mexico Office of Superintendent of Insurance under the Bail Bondsmen Licensing Law.

Protect yourself: only use a licensed bondsman, get every fee in writing before paying, and never hand over cash without a receipt and paperwork. Unlicensed operators are a real scam risk when families are desperate.

Filming the police: your rights in New Mexico

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (N.M. Stat. Ann. § 30-12-1)

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

New Mexico has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

The 10th Circuit (Irizarry v. Yehia, 2022), which covers New Mexico, held the First Amendment protects filming on-duty police in public and that the right was clearly established by 2019. Simply recording from a safe distance is not obstructing an officer (N.M. Stat. Ann. § 30-22-1), but physically interfering still can be.

What to do next:

  1. Keep a safe distance and don’t interfere — that’s the line the law draws.
  2. Know your state’s audio-recording rule stated above before you hit record.
  3. If you believe your rights were violated, write down exactly what happened and report it: how to report a civil rights violation.

Defamation in New Mexico

Someone posted or said something false about you? In New Mexico, defamation means a false statement of fact, shared with others, that harmed your reputation. Truth is a complete defense, and pure opinions (“I think he’s a jerk”) generally don’t count.

Deadline to sue: 3 years from when the statement was made (Single 3-year limit for libel and slander under NMSA 37-1-8.). Miss it and you lose the right to file.

Anti-SLAPP: New Mexico has an anti-SLAPP law — it lets people get baseless lawsuits over speech thrown out quickly, and the person who filed can be ordered to pay the other side’s legal costs.

What to do next:

  1. Save everything. Screenshots with dates, links to the posts, and names of anyone who saw them. Don’t rely on the post staying up.
  2. Don’t fire back publicly. Responding online can hurt your case later.
  3. Talk to a lawyer who handles defamation. Many offer a free first consultation or take strong cases on contingency — you don’t pay unless you win.

Find a lawyer through the referral section on this page →

Domestic abuse & protection orders in New Mexico

If someone is hurting you or your kids, or threatening to, New Mexico courts can order them to stay away. It’s called an Order of Protection.

How to get one: go to your county courthouse and ask for the protection-order paperwork — filing is free. A judge reviews it, usually the same day, and can grant a temporary order immediately. A hearing is scheduled within a couple of weeks where both sides can speak.

If you are in danger right now, call 911. For free confidential help anytime, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788.
