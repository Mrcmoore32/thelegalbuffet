What to bring to court in Oregon

Walking in prepared makes the day go smoother and helps the judge see your case clearly. Use this as a packing list — a starting point, not legal advice. Your court or your lawyer may ask for more.

  1. Your photo ID and your case number. Courts run on case numbers — bring the one from your court papers.
  2. Copies of every document you filed, plus two extra sets. One set for the judge, one for the other side, one for yourself.
  3. Evidence that backs your claims, in order. Dated messages, receipts, photos, statements — a paper trail beats memory. Bring originals where you have them.
  4. A one-page timeline. What happened, in date order, and who was there. Judges love timelines.
  5. Financial records if money is at issue. Pay stubs, bank statements, bills, tax returns — bring more than you think you’ll need.
  6. A pen and paper. To write down the judge’s orders, next dates, and anything you’re told to do.

Before the day: call the courthouse (or check its website) to confirm the courtroom, the time, and anything you’re not sure about. Arrive 30 minutes early — you’ll go through security and need time to find the room. Leave anything distracting at home and silence your phone before you walk in.

Lemon law in Oregon

Bought a new car that's constantly in the shop? Oregon's lemon law may get you a refund or a replacement.

Coverage

Which vehicles qualify

new motor vehicles (and new travel trailers) purchased or leased in Oregon; not used vehicles

Threshold

When it's legally a lemon

3+ repair attempts, 30+ calendar days out of service, or 1 repair attempt on a defect likely to cause death or serious injury, within 2 years or 24,000 miles

Remedy

What you get

replacement with a new motor vehicle or full refund of purchase or lease price (less a use allowance)

What to do next:

  1. Keep every repair order, receipt, and record of days your car was in the shop.
  2. Notify the manufacturer in writing about the defect.
  3. Give them the final repair opportunity the law requires, then file your claim with your documentation in hand.

Car accident injuries in Oregon

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for Oregon: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

Oregon’s system: Oregon is an at-fault state: the driver who caused the crash — and their insurance — is responsible for your injuries.

Deadline: In Oregon you generally have 2 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Whistleblower protection in Oregon

Fired, demoted, or punished for reporting something illegal or unsafe at work? The law may protect you — but deadlines can be as short as 30 days, so don’t wait.

Workplace safety: Federal law protects workers who report safety or health hazards. Complaints go to OSHA.

Financial fraud: The SEC runs a whistleblower program for securities fraud, with possible awards. The IRS has one for major tax fraud too.

Oregon: Oregon workers can file a retaliation complaint with the Bureau of Labor and Industries (BOLI) within one year of the retaliation.

Bail & bail bondsmen in Oregon

Someone you love is sitting in jail and you need them out. Here's how bail works in Oregon.

Oregon specifics: Oregon doesn't use commercial bail bondsmen — courts use a security-release system where the defendant posts 10% of the security amount with the court.

Be skeptical: commercial bondsmen barely operate in Oregon, so treat anyone offering to post bail for a fee with suspicion — when in doubt, call the jail or courthouse directly.

Filming the police: your rights in Oregon

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

Mixed recording rules

hybrid (one-party for phone/radio calls; all participants must be informed for in-person conversations) (ORS 165.540(1)(a), (1)(c); ORS 165.540(5)(b))

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Oregon has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

Oregon law expressly allows recording an on-duty officer performing official duties, openly and where you may lawfully be (ORS 165.540(5)(b)); the all-parties-must-be-informed rule for other conversations was upheld by the 9th Circuit en banc in Project Veritas v. Schmidt (2025). The 9th Circuit recognized a right to film police in public in Fordyce v. City of Seattle (1995).

What to do next:

  1. Keep a safe distance and don’t interfere — that’s the line the law draws.
  2. Know your state’s audio-recording rule stated above before you hit record.
  3. If you believe your rights were violated, write down exactly what happened and report it: how to report a civil rights violation.

Defamation in Oregon

Someone posted or said something false about you? In Oregon, defamation means a false statement of fact, shared with others, that harmed your reputation. Truth is a complete defense, and pure opinions (“I think he’s a jerk”) generally don’t count.

Deadline to sue: 1 year from when the statement was made (Single 1-year limit for libel and slander under ORS 12.120(2).). Miss it and you lose the right to file.

Anti-SLAPP: Oregon has an anti-SLAPP law — it lets people get baseless lawsuits over speech thrown out quickly, and the person who filed can be ordered to pay the other side’s legal costs.

What to do next:

  1. Save everything. Screenshots with dates, links to the posts, and names of anyone who saw them. Don’t rely on the post staying up.
  2. Don’t fire back publicly. Responding online can hurt your case later.
  3. Talk to a lawyer who handles defamation. Many offer a free first consultation or take strong cases on contingency — you don’t pay unless you win.

Domestic abuse & protection orders in Oregon

If someone is hurting you or your kids, or threatening to, Oregon courts can order them to stay away. It’s called a restraining order under the Family Abuse Prevention Act.

How to get one: go to your county courthouse and ask for the protection-order paperwork — filing is free. A judge reviews it, usually the same day, and can grant a temporary order immediately. A hearing is scheduled within a couple of weeks where both sides can speak.

If you are in danger right now, call 911. For free confidential help anytime, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788.

Abortion laws in Oregon

Abortion is legal in Oregon with no state-imposed gestational limit; the Oregon Health Authority states there are no restrictions based on how far along a pregnancy is and no required waiting period.

These laws change fast — court rulings and new legislation can shift the rules with little notice. Always confirm the current law through the official source below before making decisions.
