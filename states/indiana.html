Spousal support and dividing retirement accounts (QDROs)

Two money questions decide more divorces than anything else: will one spouse pay support to the other, and who gets what share of the retirement savings? Here is how Indiana answers both — plus the extra court order, the QDRO, that most people have never heard of until it is almost too late. One federal tax note first: for divorces finalized after December 31, 2018, spousal support is not tax-deductible for the payer and not taxable income for the receiver.

Spousal support

How Indiana decides support — and how long it lasts

Indiana calls it "spousal maintenance," and it is far narrower than alimony in most states — there is no formula and no general support just because one spouse earns less. Under IC 31-15-7-2 the court may order it in only three situations: (1) a spouse is physically or mentally incapacitated to the extent their ability to support themselves is materially affected; (2) a spouse lacks sufficient property and is the custodian of a child whose physical or mental incapacity requires them to forgo employment; or (3) short-term rehabilitative maintenance, after weighing education levels, career interruptions for homemaking or child care, earning capacity, and the time and cost of training. Incapacity maintenance lasts during the period of incapacity, subject to further court order; caregiver maintenance lasts for the amount and period the court considers appropriate; rehabilitative maintenance can never exceed 3 years from the date of the final decree. Under IC 31-15-7-3, an order may be modified or revoked only on changed circumstances so substantial and continuing as to make the terms unreasonable.

Dividing retirement

Retirement accounts and pensions in a Indiana divorce

Indiana puts all property owned by either spouse — whether acquired before or during the marriage — into one pot for division, starting from a presumption that an equal division is just and reasonable (IC 31-15-7-4 and -5), and retirement accounts are part of that property.

Federal rules — all 50 states

The QDRO: the separate order that actually splits a workplace plan

For most workplace plans — 401(k)s and private pensions covered by the federal ERISA law — the divorce decree by itself does not split the account. A separate court order called a QDRO (Qualified Domestic Relations Order) does that. The usual steps: (1) the settlement or judgment states exactly what share each spouse gets; (2) the QDRO is drafted — many plans publish a model form, so ask the plan administrator for theirs and have the plan pre-approve your draft before the judge signs; (3) the judge signs it; (4) a certified copy goes to the plan administrator, who approves it and sets up the former spouse’s separate share. Start before the divorce is final and finish right after: waiting can cost the benefit entirely if the plan member retires, dies, or remarries first. Government plans do not use QDROs — federal civilian and military retired pay, railroad retirement, and most state and local plans each have their own kind of court order and their own forms (the Indiana card above covers the state plan where it applies). Tax note: money paid from a plan to a former spouse under a QDRO can be rolled into an IRA to defer the tax, and a QDRO payout is exempt from the usual 10% early-withdrawal penalty.

Jury Duty in Indiana

A jury summons is a court order, not an invitation — but millions of people serve every year without a lawyer and without trouble. This section explains how jury service works in Indiana: what it pays, what your employer can and cannot do, how to ask for an excuse or a different date, and what happens if you ignore the envelope.

Jury duty

How jury service works in Indiana

County circuit and superior courts summon jurors chosen at random from a pool the state compiles from Bureau of Motor Vehicles records, Department of Revenue records, and local tax information. Terms vary by county; in Monroe County, a person who receives a jury notice must be available for one month. Jurors who report for selection but are not seated are paid $30 per day; jurors seated on a jury are paid $80 per day for the first five days of a trial and $90 per day from the sixth day until discharge, plus mileage (rates in effect since July 1, 2023, per county court pages).

Work, excuses & moving your date

Your job, asking to be excused, and postponing

If an employee notifies the employer within a reasonable time after receiving the summons and before appearing, the employer may not subject the employee to any adverse employment action because of jury service (IC 33-28-5-24.3). The employee also may not be required or asked to use annual leave, vacation leave, or sick leave for time spent on jury service. A person 75 years of age or older may be exempted from jury service by notifying the jury administrator that they wish to be exempted (IC 33-28-5-18). Any summoned person may defer their initial appearance one time for hardship, extreme inconvenience, or necessity by contacting the jury administrator by phone, email, in writing, or in person and choosing a new date within one year.

What to do:

  1. Answer the summons. Follow the instructions on it — most courts let you respond, confirm your date, or check reporting status online or by phone, and many require a response before you appear.
  2. Can’t make that date? Ask — don’t skip. Use the jury office contact on your summons to request a postponement or an excuse before the date arrives. Courts move dates routinely; silence is what gets people in trouble.
  3. Tell your employer right away. Show them the summons and keep a copy for yourself — the work rules for Indiana are in the card above.
  4. Skipping has real consequences in Indiana: A person who is summoned for jury service and fails to appear or complete jury service as directed is subject to criminal contempt (IC 33-28-5-24).
  5. Beware the jury-duty phone scam. Courts and sheriffs never call demanding gift cards, wire transfers, or payment for “missed jury duty,” and they never threaten arrest over the phone. If you get that call, hang up — then call the court yourself at the number on its official website.

Jury service is run by county courts, so reporting times, room locations, and some pay details vary by county — your summons and your county jury office have the final word. This is general information, not legal advice.

Trespassing in Indiana

Trespassing cuts both ways, so this section does too: what to do about the person who will not stay off your property, and what it means when you are the one told to leave or banned from a place. Most of it turns on notice — being told, a sign, a fence, or paint — though some offenses need no warning at all. Here is how Indiana draws the line, and what the basic offense costs.

Your property

Someone on your property who won’t leave?

Indiana defines how entry is 'denied' (IC 35-43-2-2(c)): personal communication, oral or written; posting or exhibiting a notice at the main entrance in a manner prescribed by law or likely to come to the public's attention; a hearing authority or court order; or purple paint. Purple marks (subsection (d)) must be readily visible to anyone approaching: on a tree, a vertical line at least 8 inches long with the bottom 3 to 5 feet from the ground, no more than 100 feet from the nearest marked tree; on a post, a mark covering at least the top 2 inches, no more than 36 feet from the nearest marked post. A denial of entry or an order to leave may be delivered by a law enforcement officer acting on the owner's behalf — but there is no formal warning document or ban period in the statute.

Accused

Told you’re trespassing — or banned from a place?

Criminal trespass is a Class A misdemeanor — including refusing to leave real property after being asked to leave by the owner, the owner's agent, or an officer acting for them (subsection (b)(2)). It is a Level 6 felony if committed on a scientific research facility, a public-utility facility, school property, or a school bus, or if the person has a prior unrelated trespass conviction concerning the same property. Whatever the merits, leave when you are told to: arguing your case on the spot can turn a warning into an arrest. Write down who told you, when, and where, and do not go back until it is resolved. If you believe the warning was wrong — you had permission, the place was public, or nobody ever actually told you — that is a defense to raise afterward, in writing or in court, not in the doorway.

What to do:

  1. Owners: say it clearly, then put it in writing. A dated note or letter saying the person is not allowed on the property, with a copy kept, is what most trespass charges stand on — in most states, the warning comes first.
  2. Posting land? Follow the exact rules in the first card. Sign wording, paint color, and spacing are the first things challenged when a posting is sloppy.
  3. If they won’t leave, or they keep coming back: call the police non-emergency line — 911 if anyone is threatened or it is happening right now. Don’t physically remove them yourself.
  4. Document every incident. Dates, times, photos or video, witnesses. That record backs up criminal charges and a civil claim for any damage they did — the small claims section covers suing without a lawyer, and if the trespasser is a neighbor, the neighbor disputes section covers that fight.
  5. If you were the one warned or banned: leave immediately, write down who warned you, when, and where, and stay away until it is sorted out. Returning after a warning is usually the crime itself — challenge it afterward, in writing or in court.

This is general information, not legal advice. Trespass law turns on the exact facts — who owns the place, what notice was given, and whether anyone was told to leave — and local ordinances can add rules on top of the state statute.

Car booted or towed in Indiana

A boot — a clamp locked over your wheel — means your car is not moving until money changes hands. Cars get booted two ways: a city clamps the cars of people who pile up unpaid parking tickets, and a private parking-lot operator clamps a car it says parked without permission or payment. Who put it there decides which rules protect you, and in Indiana the two rulebooks are very different. Start with the notice left on your car: it names who booted you, what they say you owe, and the number to call.

City boot

Booted for unpaid parking tickets?

Municipal — Indiana state law creates no government booting program; unpaid-ticket and impoundment enforcement is handled by cities under local ordinances. Move today: many city programs tow a booted car that is not settled promptly — sometimes within 24 to 72 hours — and daily storage charges pile up after that. When you call, ask two questions: is there a payment plan, and how do I contest the tickets?

Private lot

Booted in a parking lot?

Indiana has no state booting law. Local rules can cut the other way: Indianapolis expressly prohibits the practice — its ordinance states that booting a vehicle to hold it for towing is prohibited. With no state rulebook, your leverage is the lot’s own posted terms and any city ordinance: if nothing was posted, or the charge does not match what was posted, complain to the attorney general’s consumer-protection office and consider small claims court for the fee. Keep every receipt and photo.

What to do:

  1. Photograph everything first. The boot, the notice on the windshield, any signs at the lot entrance, and your parking receipt or payment-app screen if you had paid. Photos taken now are your evidence later.
  2. Read the notice — it tells you who booted you. A city boot means unpaid tickets; a lot boot means a private operator. The notice carries the amount and the phone number, and the two problems have different rulebooks, so do not pay the wrong office.
  3. Do not drive on the boot, and do not pry or cut it off. You can wreck your wheel and suspension, and damaging or removing a boot adds fines or criminal charges in many places. Some modern boots are self-releasing once you pay electronically — the notice will say if yours is.
  4. Get it released today. City boot: pay the tickets plus the boot fee, or get on a payment plan if the city offers one — waiting risks a tow and storage fees. Lot boot: pay what is demanded if you need the car, and keep the receipt.
  5. Then dispute it, in writing. Wrong tickets, a meter you had paid, no posted sign, a charge over the legal cap — each has a route: the hearing or appeal named on the notice, a complaint through the Consumer protection section of this page, and small claims court for the fee if it comes to that.

This is general information, not legal advice. Booting rules are mostly local and change often — the notice on your windshield, the city parking office, and the lot’s posted terms control the exact amounts and deadlines in your case.

Contractor disputes in Indiana

You paid for the work, and the work isn’t done — the contractor stopped showing up, walked off with your deposit, or left you with a job done badly. This is one of the most common consumer problems there is, and you have more moves than most people realize. Work them in order: paper trail first, written demand second, then the state — licensing board, recovery fund, attorney general — and court if it comes to that. And do not hand over more money to lure a contractor back to the job. That is how people lose twice.

Licensing

Indiana does not license home contractors at the state level

Indiana has no state-level license or registration for general or home-improvement contractors — some cities and counties license locally, so check with yours. The Attorney General’s Consumer Protection Division (next card) is the state complaint route. Because there is no state license to complain about, your leverage is the written demand, the attorney general’s consumer complaint (next card), and court — and checking any license or registration a contractor claims before you hire, through your city or county.

Consumer complaint

Also complain to the Attorney General

Licensed or not, file a consumer complaint with Indiana’s attorney general / consumer protection office — it is the same office linked in the Consumer protection section of this page. They cannot act as your private lawyer, but complaints build the pattern that triggers investigations, and some offices mediate. Attach the same paper trail.

File a consumer complaint in Indiana →

What to do:

  1. Document everything, starting today. Save the contract and any change orders, cancelled checks and receipts, photos and video of the work as it stands, and every text and email. Write down the dates the contractor showed up — and the dates they didn’t.
  2. Stop paying. Do not pay the balance or any more draws until this is resolved, and do not pay extra to get the contractor back on the job. If part of the work was done right, keep notes on what it was worth — you may still owe for that part, and a lawyer can advise you if the amounts are large.
  3. Send a written demand with a deadline. Say what was agreed, what you paid, what is unfinished or wrong, and what you want — the work finished by a date certain, or your money back for the work not done. There is a free fill-in template: the contractor demand letter. Send it certified mail with return receipt requested, and keep your copy — boards, funds, and courts all ask for it.
  4. Complain to the state — the licensing authority and the attorney general, using the cards above. Both are free, and neither stops you from also going to court.
  5. If the money fits small claims, sue there. Most contractor cases are about a dollar amount you can prove — what you paid minus the value of work actually done, plus what it costs to finish or fix. See the Small claims section on this page for Indiana’s limit, fees, and forms.

This is general information, not legal advice. Contractor licensing and complaint rules differ by state and change, and the exact wording of your contract controls a lot — for large amounts, talk to a lawyer in Indiana.

Car repossession in Indiana

If you're behind on car payments, know your rights before the tow truck shows up — and after.

Your rights

What they can't do

No breaking into a locked garage, no physical force, no threats — that's "breach of the peace" and it's illegal everywhere. They must return your personal belongings from the car.

Get it back

Cure and redemption

Yes — for most vehicle purchases, the lender must give you 20 days' notice and a chance to catch up before repossessing. Pay the full balance plus costs any time before the car is sold.

Money owed

Deficiency balance

The lender can sue for the remaining balance after a fair sale.

Notice

What they must tell you

20-day right-to-cure notice required before repossession. Notice before the sale also required.

What to do next:

  1. If your car was taken, call the lender immediately and ask for the exact payoff or cure amount in writing.
  2. Remove valuables from your car now — don't wait for repossession.
  3. If the repo agent used force, threats, or broke into a locked space, document everything and contact free legal help in Indiana.

Health insurance & medical bills in Indiana

A denied claim or a shocking hospital bill is not the end of the story. Health insurance denials can be appealed twice — first to the insurer itself, then to an independent reviewer whose decision the insurer must accept — and federal law now bans most surprise out-of-network bills outright. Separately, if you cannot afford a hospital bill, nonprofit hospitals are required to have a financial assistance program, and many people who qualify never apply. This section is about getting a denial or a bill fixed. It is not about suing a doctor or hospital for harm from care itself — for that, see the Medical malpractice section on this page.

Step 1

Internal appeal: 180 days from the denial

When your plan denies a claim or refuses to pay for care, you have 180 days from the date you got the denial notice to file an internal appeal — ask the insurer to review its own decision with a different reviewer. The plan must decide within 30 days if the service has not happened yet, or 60 days if it already has; urgent cases must be decided within 72 hours. Your strongest move is a letter from your doctor explaining, in plain terms, why the care is medically necessary — attach it, keep a copy of everything, and write down the date you filed and every name you spoke to.

Step 2

External review: an independent reviewer, and the decision binds the insurer

If the internal appeal says no, you can ask for external review within 4 months of the final internal denial. An independent reviewer with no ties to your insurer looks at the medical evidence and decides — and the insurer is legally required to accept that decision. Standard reviews are decided within 45 days; urgent ones within 72 hours. If your plan uses the federal process, it is free: call 1-888-866-6205 to request a form, or use the HealthCare.gov external review page, which also lists state programs.

HealthCare.gov: external review →
Surprise bills

Surprise bill? The No Surprises Act may make it illegal

Since January 1, 2022, federal law bans balance billing in three situations: emergency care, non-emergency care from an out-of-network provider at an in-network hospital or surgery center (the anesthesiologist you never chose, for example), and air ambulance rides. In those cases you generally owe only your normal in-network cost-sharing — the provider and the plan fight over the rest, not you. Ground ambulance rides are not covered by the federal law, though some state laws cover them. If you get a bill that looks like a banned surprise bill, do not just pay it: dispute it in writing with the provider and the plan, and complain to the federal No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059.

No insurance

Uninsured or paying yourself: get the estimate, then dispute the gap

If you have no insurance or are paying out of pocket, you have the right to a good faith estimate before scheduled care. If the final bill is at least $400 more than that estimate, you may dispute it through the federal patient-provider dispute process within 120 days of the first bill. Keep the estimate and the bill side by side — the gap is the whole dispute.

Charity care

Hospital financial assistance (charity care): ask before you pay

Nonprofit hospitals — most community hospitals — are required by federal tax law to have a written financial assistance policy, publish it, and cap what qualifying patients are charged at the amounts generally billed to insured patients. Each hospital sets its own income limits in its policy, so read that policy, not a rumor about it. Call the hospital’s billing or financial counseling office and ask, by name, for the financial assistance application. Nonprofit hospitals must accept applications for at least 240 days from your first bill after discharge, and a complete application in that window pauses collection efforts while it is decided. If you are denied, ask for the reason in writing.

In Indiana: your state insurance department and your denial letter

Your denial letter or Explanation of Benefits must tell you how to appeal and, in many states, names the organization that handles your external review. Your state insurance department also takes complaints about state-regulated plans and can point you to the right review program. This page does not list one Indiana phone number or form because the right office depends on your plan — a plan you bought yourself, a plan through a small employer, and a large employer’s self-funded plan follow different tracks — so start with the contact information on your denial letter and the HealthCare.gov external review page linked above, which lists state programs.

What to do:

  1. Read the denial or bill the day it arrives. Circle the reason given, the amount, and the appeal deadline — 180 days for an internal appeal, 4 months after that for external review.
  2. Ask for an itemized bill and compare it line by line with your Explanation of Benefits. Wrong codes, duplicate lines, and services you never got are common — and a billing-office correction is faster than an appeal.
  3. File the internal appeal in writing, with your doctor’s letter attached, and keep copies plus proof you sent it.
  4. If the internal appeal fails, file for external review — do not stop there. Independent reviewers overturn denials often enough that skipping this step leaves money on the table.
  5. For a hospital bill you cannot afford, ask for the financial assistance application before you agree to a payment plan, and get any payment-plan or hold-on-collections promise in writing.
  6. Never ignore a bill while you dispute it: tell the provider and any collector in writing that you dispute it, and ask them to pause collections while the dispute is pending.

Medical malpractice in Indiana

A bad outcome is not always malpractice. To have a case, you usually must show the doctor or hospital fell below the accepted standard of care — what a careful provider would have done — and that the mistake caused your injury. These cases turn on strict deadlines and expert review, so the clock matters here more than almost anywhere else.

Take action

Complain to the Medical Licensing Board of Indiana

Filing a complaint with the state medical board is free. The board can investigate and discipline a doctor, but it cannot award you money — that is what a lawsuit is for. Filing a complaint does not pause your lawsuit deadline.

Visit the board — complaints and license lookup →
Deadline

Indiana's deadline to sue

You generally have 2 years from the date it happened — Indiana counts from the occurrence, with only a limited exception when an injury could not reasonably be discovered in time. Miss the deadline and your case is usually over, no matter how strong it is — talk to a lawyer well before it runs out.

Free help

Talk to a malpractice lawyer

Most malpractice lawyers give a free first consultation and work on contingency — you pay nothing up front, and they take a percentage only if you win. Many states also require an expert to certify the case before you can file; a lawyer handles that.

Your records

Get your medical records

You have the right to copies of your own medical records. Ask every provider involved, in writing, and keep the copies somewhere safe — records are the backbone of any malpractice case.

What to do next:

  1. Request complete copies of your medical records from every provider involved.
  2. Write down your timeline — dates, symptoms, what you were told — while it is fresh.
  3. Do not sign anything from the hospital or its insurer, and do not accept a quick settlement, before a lawyer looks at it.
  4. Get a free consultation with a malpractice attorney well before Indiana's deadline above.
  5. If you want the doctor investigated, file the free board complaint too — just remember it does not stop your lawsuit clock.

Know your rights

Your first court appearance

At your first appearance (arraignment), the judge reads the charges and asks how you plead. Pleading not guilty keeps every option open — it does not lock you into a trial. This is also where you ask the court to appoint a public defender if you cannot afford a lawyer. Do not plead guilty just to get it over with.

On probation? How supervision, violations, and early endings work

The conditions almost everyone gets

Almost every probation order has the same core conditions: report to your probation officer as directed and keep every appointment, obey all laws, tell your officer before you change your address or job, and get permission before leaving the state (often the county). Your own probation order controls — it can add treatment, drug testing, community service, no-contact rules, and payments. When in doubt, ask your officer first and get the answer in writing.

If your officer says you violated

The pattern in most states: your officer files a violation report or petition with the sentencing court; the court issues a summons or a warrant; and you get a hearing before the judge. At the hearing you can have a lawyer and present your side. The judge can continue your probation as-is, add or change conditions, extend the term, or revoke probation and impose the sentence that was hanging over the case. Where your state's card below describes its process, that description comes from the state's own official sources.

Moving to another state

All 50 states belong to the Interstate Compact for Adult Offender Supervision (ICAOS). If you need to move, you apply through your probation officer: the state that sentenced you has the final say, the state you want to move to must accept you, and there is no right to a transfer. Do not move first and ask later — with limited exceptions you cannot travel to the new state while the application is pending, the sending state may charge a transfer application fee, and moving without approval can itself be a violation.

Want it over sooner? Early termination

Courts in many states can end probation early for people who have finished their conditions and stayed in compliance — the request goes to the sentencing court, and your officer's compliance report usually carries the most weight. Where your state's card below describes an official early-termination or earned-discharge process, follow it. If it does not, ask your officer how early-termination requests are made in your court — and finish treatment, community service, and restitution first, because open conditions are the most common reason requests stall.

Guardianship, conservatorship & long-term care in Indiana

When an adult can no longer make safe decisions about their own health, home, or money — after dementia, a brain injury, or a serious disability — someone has to be legally able to decide for them. Guardianship and conservatorship are the court’s answer, but they are also the most restrictive answer: a court order takes away an adult’s right to decide. That is why courts are supposed to try the least restrictive option first, and why this section starts with the alternatives. The Wills & probate section on this page covers what happens after death; this section covers what happens while the person is still alive and needs help deciding.

What it means

Guardianship vs. conservatorship: who decides what

A guardian is a person a court appoints to make personal decisions for an adult who cannot safely make them — where they live, what medical care they get, and day-to-day care. A conservator is a person a court appoints to handle money and property — paying bills, managing accounts, and protecting assets. In many states those are two separate jobs; in others, one appointment covers both, or the state uses only one of the words. The exact titles and powers in Indiana come from Indiana law and from the judge’s order itself, so read the order, not just the label: a good order is narrow, naming only the decisions the person truly cannot make, and leaving the rest with the person.

Step 1

Who can petition, and how the court process works

Usually a family member, close friend, or concerned professional files a petition in the probate court of the county where the adult lives, explaining what decisions the person can no longer make safely and what evidence supports that. The adult must be given notice and a chance to be heard, and in most states the court orders an independent evaluation — often by a doctor or a court visitor — and appoints a lawyer for the adult if they do not have one. At a hearing, the judge decides whether the person lacks capacity for specific decisions and, if so, who should serve and how broad the powers should be. Bring medical records, a list of the person’s existing helpers (family, home aides, a power of attorney), and any written wishes the person expressed while they could still decide.

Contested?

If family members disagree, or the adult objects

Guardianships are contested more often than people expect — siblings disagree about who should serve, or the adult themselves says they do not need a guardian. Anyone with an interest can object, present their own evidence, and ask the judge to choose a different person or a narrower order. If you object, say so in writing by the court’s deadline and show up: an unopposed petition is usually granted. After appointment, the guardian or conservator must report to the court — typically an annual report on the person’s condition and, for money, an accounting of every dollar in and out. If a guardian misuses money, ignores the person’s wishes, or is no longer needed, anyone can ask the court to review, limit, replace, or end the guardianship.

Try first

Less-restrictive alternatives: try these before court

Courts are supposed to prefer the least restrictive option that keeps the person safe, and these often avoid guardianship entirely: a durable power of attorney lets a trusted person handle money and sign documents (durable means it stays valid if the person later loses capacity); a health care directive or proxy names who makes medical decisions; supported decision-making — now recognized in a growing number of states — is a written agreement where the person keeps their legal rights and names supporters who help them understand options and communicate choices, rather than deciding for them; and practical tools like joint accounts, automatic bill pay, and a Social Security representative payee can solve a single problem without a court case. If the person can still understand and communicate with support, an alternative usually fits better than a guardianship — and it is far cheaper and faster.

Nursing home

Nursing-home resident rights and discharge protections

A nursing-home resident keeps their rights: to be treated with dignity, to take part in their own care plan, to have visitors, to manage their own money or choose someone who will, and to complain — to the facility, to the state, or to the ombudsman — without retaliation. A facility generally cannot just throw a resident out: federal rules require written notice of a discharge or transfer, usually 30 days in advance, stating the reason and the right to appeal, with shorter notice only in emergencies such as a medical crisis or an immediate safety risk. If you get a discharge notice, do not move the resident out quietly and do not miss the appeal deadline on the notice — call the ombudsman the same day (below). Before choosing a facility, compare inspection results, staffing, and star ratings on Medicare’s Care Compare.

Indiana’s small-estate shortcut

Estates of $100,000 or less (for deaths after June 30, 2022) can use a small-estate affidavit after a 45-day wait (Ind. Code §29-1-8-1).

Making a will

Are handwritten wills valid in Indiana?

No. Indiana does not recognize unwitnessed handwritten wills. A valid will must be in writing, signed, and witnessed as the law requires (Ind. Code §29-1-5-3). A handwritten note left in a drawer will not control what happens to your property.

First steps

When someone dies: the first week

Order several certified copies of the death certificate — banks, insurers, and agencies each want one. Find the original will (check the home, a safe deposit box, and the court or lawyer who prepared it). Secure the home and vehicles. Do not pay the person’s debts from your own money — debts are paid from the estate, in an order the law sets. Notify Social Security and the person’s employer and insurers.

Avoiding probate: trusts, TOD deeds, and beneficiary names

Probate is not always a disaster — the small-estate shortcuts elsewhere in this section may be all your family needs. But three tools can move your biggest assets outside probate entirely, and they are cheaper than most people expect. What they cannot do is replace a will: a will is still the safety net for everything the tools miss.

Tool 1 — every state

A living trust only works if it is funded

A revocable living trust lets a successor trustee hand your property to your family without probate — but only for assets you actually transferred into the trust while you were alive. Signing the trust paper and leaving the house, accounts, and titles in your own name accomplishes nothing; lawyers call the transfer step “funding,” and it is the step people forget. A pour-over will catches leftovers at death and pours them into the trust — but anything that arrives that way still passes through probate first. A trust costs more to set up than a will; what it buys is privacy and skipping the court process for the assets inside it.

Tool 2 — every state

Beneficiary designations beat your will

Retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death (POD) bank accounts, and transfer-on-death (TOD) brokerage accounts go to whoever is named on the account — not to whoever your will names. If the two disagree, the account paperwork wins. That makes designations the cheapest probate-avoidance there is, and the easiest to get wrong: review them after every marriage, divorce, birth, or death in the family, and name a backup (contingent) beneficiary too.

In Indiana: Indiana's Transfer on Death Property Act (IC 32-17-14) is broader than most states: it uses one statutory framework for TOD transfers of real estate, vehicles, watercraft, securities, and other property, using a 'transfer on death direction' to name beneficiaries, so the same chapter that authorizes the deed also covers TOD designations beyond real estate.

Tool 3 — in Indiana

Transfer-on-death deeds in Indiana

Indiana authorizes a transfer-on-death deed under Indiana Code 32-17-14, the state's Transfer on Death Property Act chapter covering real estate. The statute calls it a 'transfer on death deed' (beneficiary deed). It must be recorded with the county recorder before the owner's death to transfer title outside probate, and the owner can revoke it by recording a revocation or a new deed. Indiana law sets the deed's required contents; there is no official state-published fill-in form.

Tool 4 — with a catch

Joint ownership: easy now, tradeoffs later

Property owned jointly with right of survivorship passes to the surviving owner automatically, with no probate. The honest tradeoff: while you are alive, the property is exposed to the other owner’s debts, lawsuits, and divorce, you generally cannot sell or change the arrangement without their signature, and for property that has grown in value the survivor may lose part of the tax basis step-up a probate transfer would have given. It is a real tool — just not a free one.

Workers’ compensation in Indiana

If you are hurt at work or made sick by your job, workers’ compensation is the system that pays for your medical care and replaces part of your lost wages — and you do not have to prove your employer did anything wrong to use it. But the system runs on deadlines and paperwork, and the first steps matter more than people expect: report the injury in writing right away, get medical care and tell the provider it is a work injury, and keep a copy of everything. This section walks through the claim from the day of the injury to a denial or a return to work. It is the dedicated guide; the Workplace rights section on this page only mentions workers’ compensation in passing.

Step 1

Report the injury in writing, the same day if you can

Tell your supervisor or employer about the injury or illness as soon as possible, in writing — an email or a dated note counts, and keep a copy for yourself. Say what happened, when, where, and what part of your body was hurt. Deadlines for reporting vary by state, and in some states they are short (days or weeks), so do not wait to see if it gets better on its own. A late report is one of the most common reasons claims get denied. If your injury or illness developed over time — a bad back, a breathing problem, a repetitive-strain injury — report it as soon as a doctor connects it to your work.

Step 2

Get medical care, and say it is a work injury

Get care promptly, and tell every provider the injury or illness is work-related so the visit is billed to workers’ compensation, not to you or your health plan. In some states your employer or its insurer picks the first doctor; in others you pick. Follow the treatment plan and go to every appointment — missed care is used against claims. Keep every bill, work note, and restriction slip (for example, “no lifting over 10 pounds”), and give copies to your employer.

Step 3

Your employer reports it, the insurer decides

Your employer must report the injury to its workers’ compensation insurer, which opens a claim and decides whether to accept or deny it. Ask your employer for the insurer’s name, the claim number, and the adjuster’s contact information, and write them down. If your employer will not report it, or has no insurance, contact the Indiana agency below directly — that is what it is there for. Never pay for work-injury care out of pocket without telling the agency and the insurer first.

Benefits

What workers’ compensation pays: medical care and part of your wages

An accepted claim generally pays for reasonable and necessary medical treatment for the work injury, with no copay or deductible to you. If the injury keeps you from working, wage-replacement benefits replace part of your pay — in many states about two-thirds of your average weekly wage, up to a state maximum, after a short waiting period (a few days, which are often paid back if you are out longer). Exact rates, waiting periods, and how long benefits last are set by Indiana law, so check the agency page below rather than trusting a single national number. Permanent injury may also bring a separate scheduled payment.

Denied?

If the claim is denied, appeal — and watch the deadline

A denial letter must explain why, and it starts a clock: you can challenge it by filing an appeal or a claim petition with the Indiana agency, which leads to a hearing before a workers’ compensation judge or board. Appeal deadlines vary by state and are strict — read the letter the day it arrives, file even if your evidence is not complete yet, and bring your medical records, your written injury report, and any witness names to the hearing. Many workers’ compensation lawyers take these cases on contingency (paid only if you win), and the agency below can explain the process for free. If you are fired, demoted, or punished after filing, tell the agency promptly and get legal help — many states prohibit retaliation for filing a claim.

Return to work

Returning to work: light duty, restrictions, and honest communication

When your doctor clears you with restrictions, your employer may offer light-duty work that fits them. Accepting suitable light duty usually keeps benefits flowing and protects your job; refusing work that fits your restrictions can cut off wage benefits, so talk to your doctor and the agency before refusing anything. If no suitable work exists, say so in writing and keep certifying your restrictions. Never work outside your restrictions to “prove” you are fine — re-injury hurts your health and your claim.

Veterans and active-duty service members have three separate legal toolkits that most people never hear about in one place: a way to challenge a VA benefits decision, a way to upgrade a less-than-honorable discharge, and a federal law that pauses or caps civilian debts and lawsuits while you serve. VA decision

VA denied your claim? Pick the lane that matches what is wrong

You have three decision-review options, and the right one depends on your evidence. Higher-Level Review: a senior reviewer looks at the exact same file for an error — you cannot add new evidence, but you can request a one-time informal conference to point out the mistake. Supplemental Claim: you bring new and relevant evidence the VA did not have before, such as a new medical opinion or a buddy statement. Board Appeal: a Veterans Law Judge decides — on the existing record (Direct Review), with new evidence added within 90 days (Evidence Submission), or after a hearing where you testify (Hearing docket). You can choose a different option for each issue, but you cannot use two options for the same issue at the same time, and you cannot request a second Higher-Level Review or a second Board Appeal in a row on the same issue.

Deadline

The one-year VA clock — counted from the date on the letter

For most benefits, you have 1 year from the date printed on your VA decision letter — not the date you received it — to request a Higher-Level Review or a Board Appeal. A Supplemental Claim can be filed at any time, but filing it within that same year keeps your claim continuously pursued, which protects your original effective date and your back pay; filed later, the effective date generally moves to the new filing date. Exception: in a contested claim, where two people claim a benefit only one can receive, a Board Appeal is due within 60 days. Some benefit types have shorter limits stated on the letter itself, so read your letter first. SCRA

On active duty? The SCRA caps interest at 6% and pauses lawsuits

The Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (federal law, 50 U.S.C. §§ 3901–4043) protects active-duty members, reservists and Guard members on federal orders, and commissioned Public Health Service and NOAA officers, plus many protections for their dependents. The three most-used protections: (1) 6% interest cap on debts from before you entered service — credit cards, car loans, mortgages — but you must request it in writing from each creditor with a copy of your orders; the interest above 6% is forgiven, not deferred. (2) Lease termination without penalty when you receive orders for a permanent change of station, a deployment of 90 days or more, or separation/retirement — give written notice with your orders. (3) Default-judgment protection: a court cannot enter a default judgment against you while service keeps you from responding; it must appoint an attorney for you and can stay the case. Landlords also generally cannot evict you without a court order, and a pre-service mortgage cannot be foreclosed without a court order. None of this is automatic — you have to ask, in writing, with your orders attached.

Unemployment benefits in Indiana

Losing a job is expensive enough without losing benefits to a missed week or a missed deadline. Unemployment benefits replace part of your pay while you look for new work, but they are not automatic: you must file promptly, prove you are able to work and looking for work, and certify every week to keep the money coming. This is the dedicated guide — the Workplace rights section on this page only gives unemployment one paragraph. The rules below are the ones that hold in most states; where a number varies by state, this section says so and sends you to the Indiana agency for the exact figure, because trusting a national number is how people miss a deadline.

Step 1

File in your first week — benefits generally are not retroactive

File your claim in the first week you are out of work, even if you are still waiting on a final paycheck or severance paperwork. In most states you will not be paid for weeks before the week you filed, no matter how good the reason you waited. You will need your Social Security number, your employer’s name and address, your dates of employment, and your recent earnings. File in the state where you worked, not necessarily the state where you live now. If you were laid off, fired, or quit, answer honestly about why — the agency checks with your employer, and a mismatch delays everything.

Step 2

Certify every week, and keep looking for work

Once your claim is open, you must certify — usually online or by phone, every week or every two weeks depending on the state — that you were able to work, available for work, and looking for work, and you must report any earnings. Miss a certification and that week is not paid, even if you were eligible. Keep a written log of every job you apply for, with dates and employer names: the agency can ask for it, and a thin log is a common reason benefits stop. If you are sick, in school, or unavailable for a week, report it rather than certifying something untrue.

Denied?

If you are denied, appeal the day you get the notice

A denial (often called a determination) can be appealed to a hearing, usually before an administrative law judge, where you can present evidence and witnesses. The deadline varies by state — commonly about 10 to 30 days — and the clock usually runs from the mailing date printed on the notice, not the date you opened it, so read every notice the day it arrives and file your appeal that same day even if your evidence is not ready yet; you can add it before the hearing. If you miss the deadline, you may still file late with a good-cause reason, such as serious illness or never receiving the notice, but “I did not understand” is rarely enough. Keep certifying while your appeal is pending — if you win, you can only be paid for weeks you certified.

Overpayment

Overpayment notice? Do not ignore it — appeal it or ask about a waiver

An overpayment notice says you were paid benefits you were not entitled to, and it states an amount and a reason. In most cases overpayments must be repaid even if you already spent the money, and the agency can take it from future benefits or tax refunds if you do nothing. But you have options, and both have deadlines: appeal if the decision is wrong (for example, earnings you did report), and ask about a waiver if you were not at fault and repayment would cause real hardship — many states waive non-fault overpayments on hardship grounds, but the rules and the form are state-specific, so use the agency link below and do not assume a waiver is automatic. Never ignore the notice while you decide: an unanswered overpayment grows penalties and collection action.

Part-time work

Part-time work: report every dollar, and partial benefits may still be possible

Taking part-time or temporary work does not automatically end your benefits — but you must report all earnings for the week you earned them, not the week you were paid, and failing to report earnings is the fastest route to an overpayment and a fraud penalty. In most states part of your earnings is disregarded and the rest reduces your weekly benefit dollar-for-dollar or by a formula; earn too much in a week and that week pays nothing, but the claim itself usually stays open. The exact disregard and formula are set by Indiana law, so check the agency page below before you turn down a shift out of fear of losing everything, or accept one without reporting it.

Federal law — FMLA

The federal floor: 12 weeks of job-protected leave

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) gives eligible workers up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave a year — for a new child (birth, adoption, or foster placement), your own serious health condition, or to care for a spouse, child, or parent with a serious health condition — with your health coverage continued and your job (or an equivalent one) waiting when you return. If your employer denies leave you qualify for, punishes you for taking it, or refuses to restore your job, you can complain to the U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division at 1-866-487-9243.

Severance packages and releases: read before you sign

When a job ends, some employers offer a severance package: money (and sometimes continued benefits) paid after you leave. Almost always, the money comes with a paper to sign — a release giving up your right to sue the employer. That signature is the real transaction, and it is worth understanding before you trade. Here is the national picture, then exactly where Indiana stands.

Every state

Severance is almost never owed to you — it is a trade

No federal law requires an employer to pay severance. The U.S. Department of Labor states that severance pay is a matter of agreement between an employer and an employee (or the employee’s representative) — it comes from a company policy, a union contract, your employment contract, or negotiation, not from a general legal duty. The rare exceptions are narrow state rules for big plant closings and mass layoffs, covered in the Indiana card below. Because the money is voluntary, the employer is buying something with it: your signature on a release. That is also why the amount and terms can often be negotiated — before you sign, not after.

Every state

What the release takes — and what to ask for

A typical agreement asks you to give up claims for wrongful termination, discrimination, unpaid wages and overtime, and sometimes more — and may add confidentiality, a promise not to disparage the company, and limits on working for competitors. Before signing, read for: what claims you release, any non-compete or non-solicit clause, what happens to your unused vacation and final pay, the last-day and pay-through dates, and when the money is actually paid. Common things employees ask for in exchange: more weeks of pay, a later separation date, a neutral reference or agreed reference letter, help with health-coverage costs, and removal of a non-compete. A one-hour review by an employment lawyer is cheap insurance on a document that ends your claims.

Every state

Age 40 or older? Federal law gives you time

If you are 40 or older and the agreement asks you to waive age-discrimination claims, the federal Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (part of the ADEA) sets strict conditions: the waiver must be written in understandable language, must specifically mention the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, cannot waive claims that arise after you sign, must give you something beyond what you were already owed, and must advise you in writing to consult a lawyer. You must get at least 21 days to consider it — 45 days if it is part of a group layoff or exit-incentive program — and after signing you get 7 days to revoke. In a group program the employer must also give you written information about who was selected and who was not, by job title and age. An agreement that skips these steps may not hold up as a waiver of your age claims at all.

Every state

A big layoff or shutdown? The 60-day federal notice rule

The federal WARN Act requires employers with 100 or more employees to give at least 60 calendar days’ written notice before a plant closing or mass layoff affecting 50 or more workers at a single site. It is a notice law, not a severance law — but an employer that skips the notice can owe back pay and benefits for the violation period, up to 60 days, and some states add their own notice laws (and a couple add real severance). Adoption, paternity & parentage in Indiana

Two different questions live in this section, and both come down to one thing: who counts, legally, as a child’s parent. Adoption creates a legal parent-child relationship where none existed before. Parentage (often called paternity when the father is the parent being established) proves that relationship when a child is born to unmarried parents. Parentage matters beyond the birth certificate: until parentage is legally established, a court generally cannot order custody, visitation, or child support. The Child custody and Child support sections on this page cover what happens after parentage exists; this section covers how you get there, and how adoption works from the other side.

Adoption types

The four paths to adoption

Agency adoption goes through a licensed public or private agency, which matches children with families and completes the required home study. Private (independent) adoption is arranged directly between birth parents and adoptive parents, usually with lawyers on both sides and a court-approved home study. Stepparent adoption is when a spouse adopts their partner’s child — often the simplest path, but it still requires the other birth parent’s rights to be terminated or surrendered, unless that parent is deceased or their rights were already terminated. Foster-care adoption is adopting a child already in state care, usually after parental rights have been terminated; children adopted from foster care who meet the state’s definition of special needs may qualify for federal or state adoption assistance to help with costs. Every path ends the same way: a court hearing that finalizes the adoption and issues a new birth certificate naming the adoptive parents.

Adoption steps

How adoption works, step by step

First, the birth parents’ legal rights must end — by voluntary surrender or consent, or by a court order terminating rights after a hearing. Second, the adoptive family completes a home study: interviews, background checks, a home visit, and references, done by a licensed agency or social worker. Third, the child is placed with the family for a supervision period set by state law. Fourth, the court holds a finalization hearing and signs the adoption decree. Two warnings: never pay for a child or promise payment in exchange for consent — baby-selling is illegal in every state, and legitimate adoption costs are fees for agencies, lawyers, and the home study, not payments to birth parents — and never skip the home study or the court hearing, because an adoption that is not finalized by a court is not an adoption.

Parentage

Establishing paternity and parentage: three ways

When parents are married, the spouse is usually presumed to be the legal parent automatically. When they are not, parentage must be established one of three ways. Voluntary acknowledgment: both parents sign a Voluntary Acknowledgment of Paternity (VAP) — hospitals are required to offer this form at birth, and it can also be signed later. Signing is a legal act with real consequences, including child support, so do not sign it casually or under pressure; most states give a short window to rescind it. Court order: either parent, or the child-support agency, can file a parentage case and ask a judge to decide. Genetic testing: the court can order DNA testing, and the child-support agency can usually arrange testing without a full court case. If the test excludes a man, he is not the legal father; if it matches, the court declares parentage.

Why it matters

Why parentage comes first: no parentage, no custody or support order

Parentage is the key that unlocks everything else. Without it, a father generally cannot get a court order for custody or visitation, and a mother generally cannot get a court order for child support from him — the court has no legal parent to order. Parentage also affects the child’s rights: inheritance, health-insurance coverage through a parent, Social Security benefits on a parent’s record, and access to family medical history. If you are unsure whether parentage is established for your child, check whether a VAP was signed or a court order exists; a name on the birth certificate alone does not always settle the legal question, and the rules vary by state.

Fit parents get the benefit of the doubt — in every state

Financial scams in Indiana

Scammers are professional liars, and they are good at it — Americans lose billions of dollars to fraud every year, and most of it is never reported. Falling for one is not a character flaw; these crews run scripts tested on thousands of people. The good news: almost every financial scam shares the same handful of tells, and once you know them, the spell breaks.

The golden rule: anyone who pressures you to pay right now with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, a cash app, or cash is running a scam — full stop. No real agency, bank, utility, or business demands payment that way. The second someone insists on secrecy (“don’t tell your family or your bank”), hang up. Urgency plus secrecy plus a strange payment method is the whole game.

Know the signs

Government impostors

A call, text, or email claims to be the IRS, Social Security, Medicare, or the sheriff: you owe money, your benefits will stop, or there is a warrant for your arrest. Real agencies almost always contact you by mail first, never threaten arrest over the phone, and never ask for gift cards or wire transfers. Hang up and call the agency yourself at the number on its official website or your paperwork.

Know the signs

The family emergency — now with cloned voices

“Grandma, I’m in jail, I need bail money — please don’t tell Mom.” Scammers can now clone a loved one’s voice from a few seconds of online audio, so it can sound exactly like them. Defenses that work: agree on a family code word in advance, and always hang up and call your loved one back on the number you already have. Real trouble survives a callback; a scam can’t.

Know the signs

Romance and investment scams

An online sweetheart who can never meet — and eventually needs money — is a script, not a relationship. So is the friendly stranger pitching can’t-miss crypto or investment returns, sometimes showing you a fake account that looks like it is growing (“pig butchering”). Guaranteed high returns with no risk do not exist. If you have to pay fees or taxes before you can withdraw your own money, it was never real.

Know the signs

Fake checks, phishing, and tech support

A check that overpays you — for a car, a job, a prize — with a request to send part back is counterfeit; it bounces days later and you owe the bank. Texts and emails about a “locked account” or a “missed delivery” are phishing for your logins — never use their link; type the company’s address yourself. And a pop-up warning that your computer has a virus, with a number to call, is fake: real companies never do that.

Eviction records and rental screening: clean up your record

An eviction case can follow you for years — even one you won, and even one that was dismissed — because tenant-screening companies scrape court filings and sell them to landlords. Federal law gives you rights against bad screening reports, and a growing number of states let you seal, expunge, or limit the court record itself. Here is the federal picture, then exactly where Indiana stands.

Every state

A filing is not a judgment

When a landlord files an eviction case, that filing exists as a public court record from day one — before any judge hears anything, and even if the case is later dismissed, settled, or decided in your favor. Screening companies collect those filings in bulk, so a case that went nowhere can still show up as an “eviction” on a report and cost you an apartment. That is why cleaning up the report (below) and the court record itself (the Indiana card) are two separate jobs — most people need both.

Every state

Your federal rights when a landlord screens you

A tenant-screening report is a consumer report under the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA). If a landlord turns you down — or charges you more — based on one, the landlord must give you an adverse-action notice naming the screening company that made the report. You then have the right to a free copy of that report from the company if you ask within 60 days, and to dispute anything wrong or incomplete: the company generally must investigate within 30 days, fix what it cannot verify, and tell you the result. If a dispute does not resolve it, you may add a short written statement of dispute that must go out with future reports.

Every state

The 7-year rule — and what it does not do

Under the FCRA, screening companies must stop reporting most negative information after 7 years — civil judgments included. But the rule only limits what a report may contain. The underlying court record does not disappear, and a record a landlord finds by searching court files directly is not the screening company’s report. Closing the court file itself — sealing, expungement, or limited access — is a matter of state law, and states differ sharply, as the Indiana card below shows.

Every state

The cleanup, step by step

(1) Get the report. Use the company named in your adverse-action notice first (free within 60 days); before you apply anywhere else, also order your file from the big tenant-screening companies — you have FCRA rights against each of them. (2) Dispute every error in writing, with a copy of the court’s final disposition (dismissal, judgment in your favor, satisfaction) attached, and keep proof of mailing. (3) Send court orders. If your case was sealed or expunged, send the order to every screening company listing the case — a sealed record must not be reported. (4) Explain what remains. For anything accurate that is still reportable, attach a short written explanation and bring your disposition paperwork when you apply, so a landlord sees the outcome, not just the filing.

For older cases, and for tenants who lost, a tenant may petition: the court must grant nondisclosure if a money judgment in the case has been satisfied, or if 7 years have passed since a final judgment that included no money judgment.

Traffic Tickets & Court Fines in Indiana

A traffic ticket is not just a bill. On most tickets you have three choices — pay it, fight it, or ask about a middle option like traffic school or a deferral — and the choice closes doors: paying is usually a guilty plea that puts a conviction on your driving record. This section lays out the fork, then Indiana’s rules on points, courses, and what happens if a ticket is ignored.

Start here

Your three choices on almost every ticket

Pay it — in nearly every state, paying the fine is a guilty plea. The conviction goes on your driving record, points are added where the state uses them, and your insurance can go up. Fight it — plead not guilty by the date on the ticket and the court or tribunal named on it hears your case. Ask about the middle options first — traffic school, a deferral, or a reduced charge can keep a ticket off your record in many places, but they are usually gone once you pay. The one choice that always backfires is ignoring the ticket: expect a default conviction, added fees, and in most states a suspended license or a warrant.

In Indiana

Points and your license in Indiana

The Indiana BMV assigns points that stay active for two years: speeding runs 2 points (1-15 mph over) to 8 points (over 25 mph over or in a work/school zone), running a stop sign or red light is 6, following too closely is 6, and reckless driving is 6 or 8. Accumulating points triggers BMV notices and requirements, and serious balances lead to suspension; a separate rule suspends drivers convicted of 10 traffic offenses in 10 years that carry points.

Traffic school

Courses that can help in Indiana

Completing a BMV-approved Driver Safety Program removes a 4-point credit from your record, once every 3 years. The BMV can also order you to complete the program if you get two moving violations within a year or one plus an at-fault crash; failing to finish an ordered course means suspension until you do.

Your options

Fighting, deferring, or paying over time in Indiana

To contest a ticket, plead not guilty in the city, town, or county court named on the citation and set a bench trial. Some prosecutor offices offer ticket deferral programs locally, but there is no single statewide program. If you miss your court date or fail to pay, the court notifies the BMV and your driving privileges are suspended until the case is resolved and fees are paid.

Motorcycle lane splitting in Indiana

Lane splitting is a motorcycle riding between lanes of traffic. Lane filtering is the narrower version — moving between stopped or very slow cars, usually to the front at a light. Only six states allow either one, and every one of them puts strict speed limits on it. Two motorcycles riding side by side in one lane is different — that is lane sharing, and it is legal almost everywhere.

Not allowed

The rule in Indiana

Lane splitting and lane filtering are not allowed in Indiana. Indiana law (Ind. Code §§ 9-21-10-6, 9-21-10-7) gives motorcycles full use of a lane and bars a rider from passing another vehicle in the same lane or riding between lanes of traffic. A rider who does it can be ticketed for improper passing or lane use.

If you drive a car

Know the numbers — they protect you

Most drivers don’t know the numbers — and the numbers are what protect you. In most states, fault in a crash is shared out between everyone involved, so a rider who splits outside the legal limits can carry a share of the blame — and that matters for insurance claims and tickets. A few states — Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, and Virginia — use a stricter rule where even a small share of fault can block a claim entirely. And the strongest record of what happened is a dash cam: it shows the rider’s speed and position better than any argument afterward.

What to do:

  1. Riders: stay in your lane. Splitting or filtering can get you ticketed, and it can count against you if there is a crash.
  2. Drivers: before changing lanes, check your mirrors and your blind spot — a motorcycle is small and easy to miss, legal or not.
  3. Drivers: if a rider hits you while splitting illegally, save any dash cam footage and note the time and place — it is the clearest record of what the rider was doing.
  4. If there is a crash: get the police report and talk to a lawyer before giving a recorded statement to an insurer — fault is often shared, and the details matter.

Nonconsensual intimate images & AI deepfakes in Indiana

If someone shares, or threatens to share, an intimate image of you without your consent — a real photo or video, or an AI-generated fake that looks real — that is image-based sexual abuse, sometimes called “revenge porn.” It is not your fault, and you do not have to handle it alone. The law in this area is moving fast: most states now have a law against nonconsensual sharing, and a federal law now requires covered platforms to act quickly on removal requests. Work the steps below in order — preserve evidence first, then report and request removal, then consider criminal and civil routes — and get support early, because the process can take weeks.

The law now

State laws and the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act

Most states criminalize knowingly sharing an intimate image without consent, and many also allow the person depicted to sue for damages and a court order; some states also specifically cover AI-generated sexual deepfakes, and others reach them through the general nonconsensual-image law. The exact elements, penalties, and deadlines vary sharply by state, and this area changes fast, so this page does not print a single Indiana statute or penalty — the Cyber Civil Rights Initiative (CCRI) Safety Center below maintains a list of state laws, and a lawyer or legal-aid office can confirm the current Indiana law for your situation. Federally, the TAKE IT DOWN Act (signed May 19, 2025) criminalizes knowingly publishing nonconsensual intimate images, including AI “digital forgeries,” and threats to publish them, and requires covered platforms to remove a qualifying image as soon as possible and within 48 hours of a valid removal request, plus make reasonable efforts to remove known identical copies. FTC enforcement of the platform-removal duty began May 19, 2026; if a platform ignores a valid request, you can report it at TakeItDown.ftc.gov.

Juvenile law & school-to-court issues in Indiana

Juvenile court is not adult court with smaller defendants. It was built around rehabilitation rather than punishment, it uses different words, different hearings, and usually no jury, and a young person’s record is handled very differently from an adult’s. That difference is good news, but it is not automatic: records are not always sealed on their own, school discipline can still lead to court, and some serious cases can be moved to adult court. This section covers the map; the Child safety and Special education sections on this page cover abuse reporting and school disability rights, and this section does not repeat them.

How it differs

How juvenile court differs from adult court

In juvenile court, a young person is usually “taken into custody” rather than arrested, charged by petition rather than indictment, and “adjudicated delinquent” rather than convicted — the judge, not a jury, usually decides the case, in a hearing that is often closed to the public. The court’s dispositions lean toward supervision, counseling, community service, restitution, and placement in a juvenile facility rather than adult prison, and parents or guardians are usually required to attend and can be ordered to participate. Young people still have core rights — to notice of the charges, to a lawyer, to confront witnesses, and against self-incrimination — so if your child is questioned by police or school resource officers, the safest first move is to ask for a lawyer before answering questions, and to say so calmly and clearly. Age limits, the right to a jury, and the rules for detention all vary by state, so confirm the Indiana rules with the juvenile court or a juvenile-defense lawyer rather than assuming another state’s rules apply.

Office of Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention (OJJDP) — juvenile justice information →
Common matters

Common juvenile matters: delinquency, truancy, and status offenses

Delinquency means an act that would be a crime if an adult did it — theft, assault, drug offenses, vandalism. Status offenses are acts that are only offenses because of age: running away, truancy (unexcused school absence), curfew violations, and underage drinking in some states. Truancy and other status offenses usually start with school and family interventions, not jail, but repeated truancy can reach court, where the judge can order counseling, supervision, or services for the family — so answer school letters early, keep records of illness and appointments, and ask the school in writing what support it has offered before the case reaches a judge. Emancipation is a separate court process by which some minors can be declared legally independent adults before 18; it generally requires showing the young person can support themselves and manage their own affairs, it is not available in every state, and it ends parental support obligations in ways that should never be rushed into without legal advice.

School to court

When school discipline leads to court involvement

School discipline and court are connected in both directions: a suspension or expulsion hearing is a school process with its own notice and hearing rights (see the Special education section on this page for students with disabilities), while fights, threats, drug possession, or property damage at school can also be referred to police and juvenile court. If your child faces both, handle both tracks: respond to the school in writing by its deadline, keep every notice, and do not let the school process substitute for legal advice about the court case — statements made to a principal can be used later. Ask the school what alternatives it considered (restorative practices, counseling, behavior plans) and whether the referral to police was required or discretionary; that record matters to a juvenile judge deciding whether the case belongs in court at all. If your child has a disability, tell the school and the court in writing: federal disability law can change what discipline is lawful.

Records

Juvenile records: sealing and expungement are not automatic

A juvenile record is not one file: police reports, court files, probation records, and school records may each be held separately, and sealing one does not always seal the others. Sealing generally hides the record from the public and most employers, while law enforcement and courts may still see it; expungement generally erases or destroys it, where the state offers it. In many states neither happens automatically at 18 — someone must apply to the juvenile court after the case closes and any waiting period passes, and serious offenses or later adult convictions can block eligibility. The practical steps: ask the juvenile court clerk in the county where the case was heard for the sealing/expungement forms and the waiting period, confirm which agencies hold records, and get the order in writing; then check that employers’ background checks actually come back clean. The Clearing your criminal record section on this page covers adult records; juvenile sealing uses a different process and different forms, so do not file adult forms for a juvenile case.

Transferred cases

When a juvenile case can move to adult court

For the most serious offenses, and for older teens, Indiana law may allow a prosecutor to ask a judge to transfer (waive) the case to adult court, or may require it for certain charges. Transfer is the highest-stakes decision in juvenile law: an adult conviction carries adult penalties and an adult record that is far harder to clear. If transfer is raised, get a juvenile-defense lawyer immediately, attend every hearing, and gather school records, counseling records, and evidence of rehabilitation — judges deciding transfer weigh the seriousness of the offense, the young person’s history, and whether juvenile services can still help. Do not discuss the facts of the case with police, school staff, or on social media while transfer is possible.

Emancipation in Indiana — becoming a legal adult before 18

  • The process: Indiana Code § 31-34-20-6 allows a juvenile court to partially or completely emancipate a child on the child's own petition; a parallel provision, IC 31-37-19-27, applies to a child adjudicated delinquent.
  • Age & where to file: No minimum age is stated in IC 31-34-20-6. The juvenile court for the county in which the child resides, on a petition brought by the child. The court must appoint an attorney to serve as guardian ad litem, who investigates the petition and reports to the court before a hearing.
  • What the judge weighs: After the guardian ad litem's report and a hearing, the court may grant the petition if it finds emancipation is in the child's best interests and that the child wishes to be free from parental control and no longer needs it, has sufficient money for the child's own support, understands the consequences of being free from parental control, and has an acceptable plan for independent living.
  • What changes: A completely emancipated child has all the rights and responsibilities of an adult.

  1. Questioned or taken into custody? Ask for a lawyer before answering questions; parents should attend every hearing.
  2. Truancy or status offense? Answer school letters early, keep illness/appointment records, and ask in writing what support the school offered.
  3. School discipline plus a court referral? Handle both tracks in writing; do not let school statements substitute for legal advice.
  4. Emancipation? Get legal advice first — it is not available everywhere and it ends parental support duties.
  5. Record still showing? Ask the juvenile court clerk for sealing/expungement forms and waiting periods; sealing one file may not seal them all.

Special education & student rights in Indiana

If your child has a disability that affects learning, two federal laws protect them at school. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) gives eligible children an Individualized Education Program (IEP) — specially designed instruction, written down, with goals the school must work toward. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act covers children who have a disability that substantially limits a major life activity but do not need specially designed instruction — they get a 504 plan of accommodations instead, like extra time, preferential seating, or a quiet room for tests. You do not have to know which one fits before you ask: ask for an evaluation in writing, and the school must consider it.

IEP

How an IEP works — and the federal clocks

Ask for an evaluation in writing, addressed to the principal or the district’s special education director, and keep a copy with the date. The school must get your written consent before it evaluates. Under the federal default, the evaluation is completed within 60 days of your consent — your state may set its own timeline, so ask what Indiana’s is — and once your child is found eligible, the school must hold the IEP meeting within 30 days. The IEP is reviewed at least once a year, and your child must be reevaluated at least every 3 years. You are a member of the IEP team: you can bring someone with you, you can disagree, and you can ask for another meeting at any time.

504 plan

If an IEP is not the fit: a Section 504 plan

A 504 plan does not come with specially designed instruction, but it does require the school to remove barriers: accommodations in the regular classroom, changes to how work or tests are given, and access to school activities. Ask the school who its Section 504 coordinator is and put your request in writing, with a note from your child’s doctor if you have one. If the school refuses an evaluation or a plan, ask for that refusal in writing — a written refusal (called prior written notice) must explain why, and it starts your right to challenge it.

Discipline

School discipline: extra protections apply

A student with an IEP or 504 plan cannot simply be suspended or expelled like any other student. If the school wants a disciplinary change of placement lasting more than 10 school days in a row, the team — including you — must hold a manifestation determination review: was the behavior caused by, or directly related to, the disability, or did the school fail to carry out the IEP? If the answer to either is yes, the student generally must return to the placement, unless the case involves weapons, drugs, or serious bodily injury, which have their own rules. If your child is suspended, ask in writing on day one whether a manifestation review is required, and do not let the days run while you wait.

Disagreements

If you and the school disagree

Work up the ladder in this order, and keep everything in writing: first, ask the IEP team to meet again and say plainly what you want changed. Second, ask about mediation — a free, voluntary meeting with a neutral mediator that your state provides. Third, file a state complaint with Indiana’s department of education (your PTI, linked above, gives you the current form and address) if the school broke a rule or missed a deadline. Fourth, a due process hearing, where a hearing officer decides — this is the formal route, and deadlines apply, so ask your PTI or free legal help right away rather than waiting. You can also file a complaint with the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights if you believe the school discriminated because of disability.

What to do:

  1. Put every request in writing — evaluation, meeting, change to the plan — and keep a dated copy. Spoken requests are easy for a school to lose.
  2. Call your PTI before the meeting, not after. It is free, and a short prep call changes how the meeting goes.
  3. Do not sign an IEP you disagree with on the spot. You can take it home, ask the PTI to review it, and reply in writing about what you accept and what you dispute.
  4. Track the clocks: 60 days (federal default) for the evaluation, 30 days to the IEP meeting after eligibility, a review every year, a reevaluation every 3 years — and ask what Indiana’s own timelines are, since states may differ.
  5. If discipline starts, act on day one: ask in writing whether a manifestation determination review is required, and keep a log of every school day missed.

Section 8 & Public Housing in Indiana

If rent eats most of your income, two federal programs can help: Housing Choice Vouchers — still widely called Section 8 — and public housing. Both are run by a local agency called a public housing agency (PHA), not by a national office, and both have waiting lists. This section explains how the programs work, how Indiana runs them, and the exact steps to get on a list — and keep your place on it.

Federal program — all 50 states

How Section 8 and public housing work

With a voucher, you find a home from a private landlord; you generally pay about 30% of your adjusted income toward rent, and the housing agency pays the rest, up to a local limit it sets. Public housing is different: the home itself is owned by the housing agency, and rent is also based on your income. Eligibility for both turns on income — generally at or below 50% of the median income for your area, with most new vouchers reserved for households at or below 30%. A voucher can also move with you if you relocate to another housing agency’s area — ask about portability before you move. And if an agency denies you assistance, federal rules give you the right to an informal hearing to challenge the decision. General questions? HUD’s housing agency information line is (800) 955-2232.

In Indiana

Use the Indiana Housing and Community Development Authority (IHCDA) map of service providers to find the agency for the county where you want to live, then apply through that agency. Any area without a local city housing authority falls under IHCDA, which works through local subcontracting agencies — often community action agencies — that take the applications. IHCDA does not cover the city of Indianapolis or Marion County; apply to the Indianapolis Housing Agency there instead.

Some IHCDA vouchers are referral-only and you cannot apply for them directly: Mainstream and non-elderly disabled vouchers come through disability service providers, VASH vouchers through the VA, Family Unification vouchers through the Department of Child Services, and Stability Vouchers through Coordinated Entry.

First 24 hours

Safety and documentation first — before you clean up

Do not re-enter a damaged home until authorities say it is safe, and do not touch downed power lines, gas leaks, or floodwater, which can hide hazards and contamination. Make a room-by-room list with approximate purchase dates and values, save receipts for emergency repairs and temporary housing, and keep a log of every call (date, name, what was promised). If you must make emergency repairs to prevent further damage — tarping a roof, shutting off water — do so, keep the receipts, and photograph the damage first: your policy usually requires you to mitigate further damage, but it does not require you to repair everything before the adjuster sees it.

Neighbor & HOA disputes in Indiana

Most neighbor fights are not about the law — they are about a fence, a tree, a dog, or a dues bill, and about two people who stopped talking. The law still matters, because it decides who pays and who has to stop, but the cheapest wins in this section come before any courtroom: a survey, a dated letter, and a paper trail. Work the ladder in order — talk, letter, mediation, small claims — and do not skip steps, because a judge will ask what you tried first. The Deed fraud section on this page covers title and ownership scams; this section covers the everyday disputes between people who live next to each other.

Property lines

Property lines and surveys: never argue from a fence line

Fences, hedges, and driveways are often not on the real boundary — they were put up by guesswork years ago. Before you argue, or build, get the facts: your deed and plat map from the county recorder’s office, and, if the line is genuinely disputed, a licensed land surveyor’s survey. A survey costs money, but it ends arguments that a fence never will, and courts treat a surveyor’s markers as strong evidence. Do not move, remove, or destroy survey markers — tampering with them is illegal in many states. If the survey shows your neighbor’s fence, shed, or driveway is on your land, talk first and put any agreement in writing; if they refuse to move it, that written record and the survey are what a court or mediator will want to see.

Fences

Fences: who pays for a boundary fence depends on your state

Some states require neighbors to share the cost of a boundary fence equally; others put the cost on the person who wants it, or have special fence laws that apply mainly to farmland and livestock; and many cities add their own height, setback, and permit rules on top. There is no single national rule, so do not quote one to your neighbor — check Indiana law and your city or county fence ordinance before you demand half the cost or refuse to pay it. Practical rules that hold almost everywhere: get any cost-sharing agreement in writing before work starts, keep the contractor’s invoice and your payment proof, and do not build on a disputed line without a survey. If a fence is dangerous — leaning, broken, electrified without warning — report it to code enforcement as well as writing to the owner.

Trees

Trees: overhanging branches, fallen trees, and who pays

The general principles courts commonly apply are these, with state-by-state variations: you may usually trim branches and roots that cross onto your property, up to the property line, but you may not trespass onto your neighbor’s land to do it, and you must not cut so much that you kill or destabilize the tree — killing a neighbor’s tree can make you liable for its full value. If a healthy tree falls in a storm, the damage usually falls where it lands, and each owner’s insurance handles their own property; but if the tree was dead, diseased, or visibly hazardous and the owner knew or should have known, the owner may be liable for the damage. The move that protects you in both cases is a dated written notice: if a neighbor’s tree looks dead or dangerous, write to them (keep a copy) and to your city’s tree or code office before it falls, so “nobody told me” is no longer available.

Noise & nuisance

Noise and nuisance: document it, then report it

Noise limits, quiet hours, barking-dog rules, and junk or odor complaints are almost always set by city or county ordinance, not state law — so the first step is finding your local rule, usually through your city or county code-enforcement office or animal control. A complaint without a record goes nowhere: keep a dated log (date, time, duration, what happened), save any recordings or photos, and keep copies of every message you sent the neighbor. Report in writing, not just by phone, and keep the complaint number. If the problem is a rental, write to the landlord or property manager too — landlords can often fix a tenant problem faster than a city can. Repeated, documented violations are what get fines issued and what win in mediation or court; a single angry call is not.

HOA

HOA fines: your rights are in the documents — read them before you pay or fight

An HOA or condo association can fine you only for rules it actually has the power to enforce, and that power comes from three places: the declaration and bylaws (the CC&Rs), the association’s rules, and Indiana’s HOA or condo statute, which you can find through the state legislature’s website. In many states you have a right to a hearing before a fine becomes final, and a right to see the association’s records — budgets, meeting minutes, and the violation file on your home. If you get a fine: ask in writing for the exact rule you allegedly broke, the evidence, and the hearing procedure; attend the hearing and bring your evidence; and do not stop paying regular dues to protest a fine unless a lawyer tells you to — unpaid dues can lead to late fees and, in many states, a lien on your home, which is a far bigger problem than the fine. If the hearing fails, mediation or small claims court (below) is usually the next step, not ignoring the letters.

Escalation

The escalation ladder: talk, letter, mediation, small claims

Work it in order. Talk: calmly, once, with a specific ask and a deadline (“Can we split the fence repair by the end of the month?”). Letter: if talking fails, send a dated letter — by email plus certified mail for anything expensive — stating the facts, the rule or survey, what you want, and a deadline; keep a copy. Mediation: many courts and communities offer low-cost mediation, where a neutral mediator helps you reach an agreement you both sign; it is faster and cheaper than court and it preserves the relationship you still have to live next to. Small claims: if the other side will not talk or mediate, file in small claims court for the money you can prove. Going straight to court without the earlier steps does not help your case — judges notice who tried to settle.

In Indiana: small claims court, the last rung of the ladder

Medicare: signing up on time, and where to get free help

The four parts, in plain words

Part A is hospital insurance — inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing care, hospice, and some home health care; most people pay no premium for it. Part B is medical insurance — doctors, outpatient care, medical equipment, and many preventive services — and it has a monthly premium. Part C is Medicare Advantage: a private plan Medicare approves that bundles Parts A and B, and usually drug coverage too. Part D is prescription drug coverage, sold by private companies Medicare approves. Original Medicare means Parts A and B together.

Your first sign-up window — do not miss it

Your first chance to sign up is a 7-month window: the 3 months before the month you turn 65, that month, and the 3 months after. If you are already getting Social Security benefits at least 4 months before you turn 65, Parts A and B start automatically and your card comes in the mail. Everyone else signs up through Social Security — online at SSA.gov or at a local Social Security office. Miss the window and you may have to wait for the January 1 to March 31 sign-up period, and pay penalties on top.

Late sign-up penalties last

Sign up late and the penalty sticks. For Part B, your premium goes up an extra 10% for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but did not — and you usually pay that higher premium for as long as you have Part B. For Part D, going without drug coverage that counts as creditable coverage adds 1% for each month you were late, also for as long as you have drug coverage. Most people pay nothing for Part A.

Still working at 65? The 8-month rule

If you (or your spouse) are still working at 65 with health coverage from that job, you can usually sign up later without a penalty — but the clock is strict: your special sign-up period ends 8 months after the job coverage or the job ends, whichever comes first. Two traps catch people: COBRA is NOT counted as job coverage and does not extend that 8-month window, and retiree coverage ending does not start a new one. And if the employer has fewer than 20 workers, Medicare is expected to pay first — ask your state's free counselors below before you delay Part B.

Original Medicare + Medigap, or a Medicare Advantage plan?

Path one: Original Medicare plus a Medigap policy. Medigap is extra private insurance that helps pay your share of Original Medicare costs, and you can see any doctor who takes Medicare. Your best time to buy is the 6-month window that starts the month you have Part B and are 65 or older — during it, insurers cannot turn you down for health problems; later, they usually can. Path two: a Medicare Advantage plan — one private plan instead of Original Medicare, usually with a doctor network, approvals for some care, a yearly cap on out-of-pocket costs, and extras like dental, vision, or hearing. You cannot use a Medigap policy with an Advantage plan. Neither path is right for everyone — that is what the free counselors below are for.

Higher income costs more; lower income can get help

Higher income? If your tax return from 2 years ago shows income above a set amount, you pay an extra amount (called IRMAA) on top of your Part B and Part D premiums. Lower income? Medicare Savings Programs, run through your state Medicaid office, can help pay Part A and B costs like premiums — and getting one also gives you Extra Help with drug costs automatically. Extra Help also pays your Part D late penalty away while you have it.

You file in the same court that entered it

Payday loans, title loans, and the safer ways out

A payday loan is a small loan due in full on your next payday — usually a few hundred dollars, at a fee that looks small and isn’t. A title loan borrows against your car. The rules are completely different from state to state: some states cap the cost at 36% APR or ban these loans outright, and others let lenders charge what they want. Here is the federal picture, then exactly where Indiana stands.

Every state

What a payday loan really costs

The standard pitch is about $15 for every $100 borrowed, due in about two weeks. Stated the way other loans are priced, that is roughly 391% APR. The real damage comes from repeat borrowing: if you cannot pay the whole amount plus the fee on payday, a new loan (or a rollover, where allowed) starts the fee clock again, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has found most payday loans go to borrowers who take out loan after loan. Price any payday or title loan by its APR and its total payback amount — never by the fee alone.

Every state

Your bank account: the federal two-strike rule

Since March 30, 2025, a federal CFPB rule has limited how payday, title, and similar high-cost lenders can pull payments from your bank account: after two consecutive failed withdrawal attempts, the lender must get your new written authorization before trying again, and it must send you payment notices. Know the limits of that rule, too: the CFPB announced in March 2025 that it is not prioritizing enforcement of these payment provisions, and the rule never capped prices — a planned federal requirement that lenders check you can afford the loan was repealed in 2020. The Indiana law below is your main protection.

Every state

In the military? Federal law caps the cost at 36%

The federal Military Lending Act caps active-duty servicemembers and their dependents at a 36% military annual percentage rate on payday loans, title loans, and most other consumer credit — and a loan made over that cap to a covered borrower is void. Lenders must check military status before lending. If a lender charged you more, complain to the CFPB and your installation legal assistance office.

Every state

Title loans: your car is the collateral

A title loan is not like a payday loan with a different name — you hand the lender a claim on your car’s title, and if you miss payments the lender can repossess and sell the car, often without going to court first. Losing the car usually means losing the ride to work, which makes the debt harder, not easier. If a lender has taken or threatens to take your vehicle, read the Car Repossession section of this page for your rights and the steps to get the car back.

Safer exits to try first

(1) A credit union Payday Alternative Loan (PAL). Many federal credit unions offer PALs of $200 to $2,000 with APR capped at 28%, an application fee of no more than $20, and no rollovers — ask any federal credit union if it offers them. (2) Negotiate the original bill. The utility, landlord, or medical office you actually owe will often take a payment plan for far less than a payday loan costs — ask before you borrow. (3) Ask about a payment plan on the loan itself — in some states the law gives you that right (see the Indiana cards below). (4) Be wary of “debt settlement” pitches that charge fees to negotiate your debts down; nonprofit credit counselors (look for NFCC members) review your budget for free or nearly free.

Payday loans in Indiana: the law

Payday lending is authorized in Indiana. The Indiana Department of Financial Institutions (DFI) states that 'small loans' (payday loans) are governed by the Indiana Uniform Consumer Credit Code, IC 24-4.5-7, and lenders must be licensed by DFI. Under DFI's current borrower pamphlet, a single small loan may not exceed $605 (a statutory cap that is adjusted periodically), a loan may not exceed 20% of your monthly gross income, the due date must be at least 14 days out, the finance charge is tiered by amount (15% of the first $250, 13% of the next $150, 10% above $400), renewals are prohibited, and a borrower may not have small loans with more than one lender if the total exceeds the statutory limits enforced through a statewide database.

Indiana law gives you an extended payment plan: when you take a third consecutive small loan (a new loan within 7 days of paying off the last one), the lender must offer you an extended payment plan, and you may request one at any time during that loan if you are not in default. The plan lets you repay in at least four equal installments over at least 60 days, and DFI states no additional fees may be added (IC 24-4.5-7-401).

Can’t afford the payment? Income-driven plans

What default means — and why to avoid it

A federal Direct or FFEL loan goes into default after 270 days without a scheduled payment. One warning: “Fresh Start,” the temporary program you may have heard about, ended on October 2, 2024 — anyone offering to enroll you in it now is offering a program that no longer exists.

In default? The two ways out

The Department names two main exits (paying in full is a third, impractical for most people). (1) Rehabilitation — usually the better deal. You agree in writing to make nine voluntary, reasonable, and affordable payments — they can be as low as $5 — during a period of 10 consecutive months. Finish, and the default status is removed, garnishment and offset stop, and the record of default is removed from your credit history (late payments from before the default stay). Rehabilitation is a one-time opportunity per loan today; starting July 1, 2027, you will be able to rehabilitate the same loan twice. (2) Consolidation — the faster exit. Roll the defaulted loan into a new Direct Consolidation Loan after three consecutive, voluntary, on-time, full payments, or by applying with an income-driven plan. The catches: unpaid interest is added to your principal, and the record of default stays on your credit history. Compare both at StudentAid.gov/default-support, or call the Default Resolution Group at 1-800-621-3115.

Every state

Wage garnishment: no court order needed — and a current pause

On a defaulted federal loan, the Department can order your employer to withhold up to 15% of your disposable pay — this is called administrative wage garnishment, and it does not require taking you to court. You have rights first: request a hearing in writing, postmarked within 30 days of the garnishment notice, and the garnishment pauses until after the hearing; Treasury offset likewise comes with a written notice and a 65-day window to dispute it. Current status: in January 2026 the Department announced it was delaying involuntary collections — wage garnishment and Treasury offset — while the new repayment plans roll out, and no restart date has been announced on the Department’s pages; defaults are still reported to the credit bureaus during the delay. Check StudentAid.gov for the current status, and do not count on the pause lasting.

Every state

Work for government or a nonprofit? Public Service Loan Forgiveness

PSLF cancels the remaining balance on your Direct Loans after 120 qualifying monthly payments made under a qualifying repayment plan while you work full-time for a government or not-for-profit employer. An income-driven plan (or the 10-year standard plan) is the usual route. Use the PSLF Help Tool on StudentAid.gov to check your employer and submit the form — do not rely on your servicer’s say-so alone.

Servicer problems — and the fee scam

Your servicer is the company that bills you. Find yours by logging in at StudentAid.gov, or call the Federal Student Aid Information Center at 1-800-433-3243. If the servicer will not fix a problem, submit it through the FSA Feedback Center on StudentAid.gov — and if the answer looks wrong or incomplete, you can add information to your case and ask for escalation to the Office of the Ombudsman. Finally, the Department’s own warning: help with your federal loans is free. A company charging “enrollment,” “subscription,” or “maintenance” fees to get you out of default is running a scam — walk away.

Small business basics in Indiana

This section is general information for people starting a small business — a different audience from most of this site, but the same plain-language, verified-links approach. Starting a business is mostly paperwork done in the right order: pick a structure, register it, get your tax IDs, check licenses, and put agreements in writing before money changes hands. The details, fees, and forms are set by Indiana and by your city or county, so this page gives the national steps and the official places to check, and does not print a single Indiana fee or form number that it has not verified.

Official resource

Pick a structure first: sole proprietorship, LLC, partnership, corporation

The structure decides your personal liability, your taxes, and your paperwork. A sole proprietorship is the simplest — you and the business are the same legal person, so you are personally liable for its debts. An LLC (limited liability company) separates your personal assets from the business’s debts in most cases, with simpler paperwork than a corporation. A partnership splits ownership between two or more people and needs a written partnership agreement even when the law does not require one. A corporation offers the strongest separation but the most formalities (directors, bylaws, annual filings). There is no universally best choice: liability, taxes, and how many owners you have should drive it, and a tax adviser or small-business counselor can help you compare them for your situation before you file anything.

LLC formation

Forming an LLC in Indiana: the general steps

The usual sequence is: (1) choose and check a business name against Indiana’s business registry so it is not already taken; (2) name a registered agent with a physical address in Indiana to receive legal papers; (3) file articles of organization (the name varies by state) with Indiana’s secretary of state or equivalent business-filing office, usually online; (4) pay the state filing fee, which varies widely by state and is not printed here because it changes and was not verified for all 50 states in this section; (5) write an operating agreement, even if Indiana does not require filing it — it settles who owns what, who decides what, and what happens if an owner leaves; and (6) keep the business’s money separate from your own from day one. File through the secretary of state’s own site, not a paid middleman site that charges extra for the same filing: search “Indiana secretary of state business filings” and check that the site is the official .gov address before paying.

Official resource

Tax IDs: get an EIN free from the IRS

An Employer Identification Number (EIN) is a nine-digit tax ID the IRS assigns to a business; you need one if you hire employees, operate as a corporation or partnership, file certain tax returns, or open a business bank account (many banks require one). Apply online directly from the IRS — it is free, and you will usually get the number immediately on completion. Any site charging a fee for an EIN is selling you a free government service. Keep the IRS confirmation letter: banks, licensing offices, and tax filings will ask for it. Separately, check whether Indiana or your city requires a state tax ID or sales-tax registration — that is a different registration from the federal EIN, handled by Indiana’s tax or revenue department.

Contracts

Simple contracts: what a solid written agreement needs

Handshake deals fail exactly when they matter, so put it in writing before work starts. A solid simple agreement names the parties, describes the work or goods precisely, states the price and payment schedule, sets start and finish dates, says who owns the work product, covers changes (written change orders, not verbal), and states how disputes are handled and how either side can end the deal. Both sides sign and date it, and each keeps a copy. For partnerships and multi-owner LLCs, the operating or partnership agreement is the contract that matters most: it should cover ownership percentages, contributions, decision rules, what happens if an owner dies or leaves, and how the business is valued. Template contracts from the internet are a starting point, not a substitute for advice on a high-value or long-term deal — have a lawyer review anything that could sink the business if it goes wrong.

Licenses

Business licenses and permits: where to check

There is no single national business license. Check three levels: (1) Indiana — the secretary of state’s business office and the state tax/revenue department for state registration and tax permits; (2) your city or county — the clerk’s or licensing office for a general business license and any home-occupation permit if you work from home; and (3) your industry — health, food, construction, childcare, and professional services are licensed by separate state boards with their own exams and requirements. Also check zoning before signing a lease: a business that is legal in one part of town may not be permitted in another, and a home-based business may need a home-occupation permit even if no customers visit. The SBA’s guide above and your local Small Business Development Center (free counseling, found through the SBA site) can walk you through the licenses for your specific business.

Traps

Common first-timer traps

Mixing personal and business money (open a business account and pay yourself properly); signing a commercial lease or a personal guarantee without reading the term, renewal, and exit clauses; hiring a first worker as a “contractor” when the law treats them as an employee, which creates payroll-tax liability; skipping insurance (general liability at minimum, plus professional liability or workers’ compensation where required); ignoring sales tax on what you sell; and paying a middleman for a free government filing (EIN, and in many states the basic registration). Each of these is cheaper to fix before it happens than after, and each is a reason to spend one session with a small-business counselor or accountant before launch.

What to do:

  1. Pick a structure. Compare liability and taxes (sole proprietorship vs. LLC vs. partnership vs. corporation) before you file.
  2. Form it properly. Check the name, name a registered agent, file articles of organization with Indiana’s secretary of state, and write an operating agreement.
  3. Get an EIN free from the IRS. Apply on irs.gov directly; never pay a middleman for it.
  4. Check licenses at three levels: state, city/county, and your industry board — plus zoning before you sign a lease.
  5. Put agreements in writing. Scope, price, dates, ownership, changes, and exit terms, signed by both sides.
  6. Separate the money and get insured. Business bank account from day one; liability insurance and workers’ compensation where required.

Got an IRS notice? Read the number first, then act by the date on it

Start with the notice number

Every IRS notice prints a number at the top (like CP14 or LT11). Look it up free on the IRS site or the Taxpayer Advocate site before you do anything else. Common ones: CP14 is your first bill for unpaid taxes. CP501 and CP503 are reminders. CP2000 means the IRS thinks your return does not match its income records — it is a proposal, not a bill, and you can disagree in writing. CP504 warns the IRS intends to take your state tax refund. LT11 or Letter 1058 is a final notice that the IRS intends to levy — take money from wages or bank accounts — and it carries the right to ask for a hearing.

Answer by the date on the notice — even if you can't pay

Ignoring a notice never makes it smaller; interest and penalties keep growing. Respond by the deadline printed on your notice: pay, ask for a payment plan, or explain in writing why you disagree. For a final levy notice (LT11 or Letter 1058), you generally have 30 days from the letter's date to request a Collection Due Process hearing (Form 12153), which stops the levy while your case is heard. Keep copies of everything and proof of mailing.

Can't pay? The IRS's own payment plans

A short-term plan lets you pay in full within 180 days with no setup fee (individuals owing under $100,000). A long-term installment agreement can be set up online at irs.gov if you owe $50,000 or less: the setup fee is $29 with automatic bank payments, $69 without ($107 or $178 if you apply by phone or mail), and low-income taxpayers pay less or nothing. While your request is being considered — and while a plan is in effect — the IRS generally cannot levy you. If even a small payment is impossible right now, ask the IRS about a temporary delay of collection for financial hardship.

Settling for less: Offer in Compromise

If you genuinely cannot pay in full, an Offer in Compromise (Form 656) asks the IRS to settle for less, based on your income, expenses, and assets. It costs a $205 application fee plus a first payment (20% down on a lump-sum offer); low-income applicants owe no fee or payments during review. Use the IRS's free Offer in Compromise Pre-Qualifier tool first — the IRS itself says to explore a payment plan before offering.

Free help that is actually free

The Taxpayer Advocate Service (1-877-777-4778) is an independent organization inside the IRS that helps when IRS procedures are causing you financial harm or your problem is stuck; ask for help with Form 911. Low Income Taxpayer Clinics give free legal help with IRS disputes to taxpayers under income limits — the IRS lists them on irs.gov. The Low Income Taxpayer Clinic in this section is another free route.

Skip the “tax relief” companies

Companies advertising on TV and radio charge thousands of dollars up front to file the same forms you can file free above — and the FTC has shut many down for taking fees and doing nothing. Never pay a tax-relief company before you have used the IRS's free pre-qualifier tool and talked to the Taxpayer Advocate Service or a Low Income Taxpayer Clinic.
