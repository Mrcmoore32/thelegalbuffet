Timeshare exit in Michigan

How to get out of a timeshare in Michigan — the cooling-off window, deed-back and resale options, and the exit scams to avoid.

Heads up: filing deadlines and fees change. Double-check them with your local court before you file.

A timeshare can feel like a trap: the maintenance fees keep rising, you cannot use the weeks you bought, and the resort will not take it back. Getting out is possible, but the route depends on when you bought it. If you just signed, most states give you a short window — usually 3 to 15 days — to cancel the purchase for a full refund, no reason needed. In 45 states that window exists by law; in 5 states (Illinois, Kansas, Montana, North Dakota, and Wyoming) there is no statutory cooling-off right at all, and your contract is all you have. After the window closes, the realistic exits are a deed-back to the resort, a resale or giveaway, or — as a last resort — stopping payments and facing foreclosure. And the exit industry is full of scams: any company that demands a large fee up front to sell or transfer your timeshare, especially one that claims a buyer is already waiting, is a red flag. The picture here is as of November 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.

The cooling-off window: your best exit, and it is short

Almost every state that regulates timeshares gives a new buyer a brief right to cancel — the rescission or cooling-off period. It runs from 72 hours (Indiana) to 15 days (Alaska), and most states land at 5, 7, or 10 days. The clock usually starts when you sign, or when you receive the disclosure documents, whichever is later. Cancel inside the window and you get a full refund. Miss it by a day and the right is gone — there is no second window for regret or rising fees. Five states have no statutory window at all, and a few more only give one in narrow cases (Ohio’s 3-business-day right, for example, applies only if the sale was a home solicitation sale). Check your contract too: it must disclose the cancellation right, and some contracts give you more time than the statute does.

Okay, so what do I do? Find the date you signed and the date you received the disclosure documents, then count the days against your state’s window in the card below. If you are still inside it, cancel today — do not wait for the resort to call you back.

How to cancel in time

Cancellation must be in writing, sent to the developer at the address in your contract, before the deadline runs out. In most states a mailed notice counts on the postmark date, so certified mail with return receipt requested is the safest way to prove you cancelled on time. Some states also allow hand delivery or email. Keep a copy of the notice and the mailing receipt, and expect your refund within 15 to 45 days depending on the state — the developer must return all the money you paid. The right to cancel cannot be waived in most states, so a contract clause that says otherwise does not bind you.

Okay, so what do I do? Write a short cancellation letter today, name the contract and the date you signed, and send it by certified mail to the address in your contract. Photograph the letter and the receipt, and keep both. If the refund does not arrive on time, file a complaint with the agency linked in the card below.

After the window closes: your real options

Once the cooling-off period ends, there is no legal right to cancel just because you changed your mind. The realistic exits, in order of safety, are: (1) a deed-back or surrender — ask the resort in writing to take the timeshare back, and get any release in writing before you stop paying; many resorts run such programs quietly. (2) Resale or giveaway — you can sell or give the timeshare away, but only after the loan is paid off and the fees are current, and resale prices are usually very low, sometimes near zero. (3) Stop paying — the resort can foreclose and report the missed payments, which can damage your credit for years, so talk to a lawyer before you do this. Paying off the loan first makes every other option easier.

Okay, so what do I do? Write to the resort and ask about a deed-back or surrender program — put it in writing and keep the reply. If they say no, ask again in a few months; programs change. Do not sign anything that promises an exit for a fee until you have checked the company with your attorney general.

Exit scams: the upfront-fee trap

The timeshare exit business is one of the most scam-ridden corners of consumer law. The classic pattern: a company cold-calls you, claims a buyer is already waiting for your timeshare at a high price, and demands a large fee up front — thousands of dollars — before any sale. Once you pay, the buyer never appears and the company disappears. A legitimate resale broker is paid a commission only after a sale actually closes. Several states ban resale brokers from charging advance fees at all. Other scams sell you a worthless “transfer” that never actually moves the deed out of your name, leaving you still on the hook for the fees.

Okay, so what do I do? Never pay a large fee in advance for a promised sale or transfer. Check any exit company with your attorney general or consumer protection office before you sign or pay, and get any promise in writing. If a company already took your money and did nothing, file a complaint with the agency in the card below and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Timeshare exit in Michigan

Cooling-off window: 9 business days after receiving all required disclosure documents (Mich. Comp. Laws § 559.184(2)). The clock starts when you get the disclosures, not when you sign.

How to cancel: Send written notice of cancellation to the address listed in the contract, by certified mail with return receipt requested. Keep a copy of the notice and the mailing receipt as proof.

After the window closes: After the 9-business-day window closes, ask the resort in writing about a deed-back or surrender program, and only accept a deal that releases you in writing. You can also try to sell or give away the timeshare, but resale values are usually very low, so first pay off any loan on it if you can. If you stop paying maintenance fees, the resort can foreclose and report the default, which can damage your credit, so get free legal help before you stop paying.

Scam warning: Beware of companies that charge a large upfront fee to resell or transfer your timeshare and promise a buyer is already waiting — legitimate agents are paid a commission only after a sale closes. Never pay thousands of dollars in advance for a promised sale, and check any company with the Attorney General before you sign or pay.

State law: Michigan has no dedicated timeshare statute — the rules that apply come from the cancellation and consumer-protection provisions cited in this section.

Where to complain: Michigan Attorney General, Consumer Protection Team. File a complaint there if a resort or exit company misled you or took your money and did nothing.

Free legal help: Michigan Legal Help.

What to do: Check the dates against the cooling-off window above. If you are still inside it, cancel in writing today by certified mail. If the window has closed, ask the resort in writing about a deed-back, and get free help from the organization linked above before you stop paying or sign any exit contract.

Okay, so what do I do? (1) Check the dates: are you still inside your state’s cooling-off window? If yes, cancel in writing today by certified mail. (2) If the window has closed, ask the resort in writing about a deed-back or surrender program. (3) Never pay a large fee in advance to any exit or resale company — check it with your attorney general first. (4) If a company took your money and did nothing, file a complaint with the agency above and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
