What to bring to court in Montana

Walking in prepared makes the day go smoother and helps the judge see your case clearly. Use this as a packing list — a starting point, not legal advice. Your court or your lawyer may ask for more.

  1. Your photo ID and your case number. Courts run on case numbers — bring the one from your court papers.
  2. Copies of every document you filed, plus two extra sets. One set for the judge, one for the other side, one for yourself.
  3. Evidence that backs your claims, in order. Dated messages, receipts, photos, statements — a paper trail beats memory. Bring originals where you have them.
  4. A one-page timeline. What happened, in date order, and who was there. Judges love timelines.
  5. Financial records if money is at issue. Pay stubs, bank statements, bills, tax returns — bring more than you think you’ll need.
  6. A pen and paper. To write down the judge’s orders, next dates, and anything you’re told to do.

Before the day: call the courthouse (or check its website) to confirm the courtroom, the time, and anything you’re not sure about. Arrive 30 minutes early — you’ll go through security and need time to find the room. Leave anything distracting at home and silence your phone before you walk in.

Car accident injuries in Montana

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for Montana: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

Montana’s system: Montana is an at-fault state: the driver who caused the crash — and their insurance — is responsible for your injuries.

Deadline: In Montana you generally have 3 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Whistleblower protection in Montana

Fired, demoted, or punished for reporting something illegal or unsafe at work? The law may protect you — but deadlines can be as short as 30 days, so don’t wait.

Workplace safety: Federal law protects workers who report safety or health hazards. Complaints go to OSHA.

Financial fraud: The SEC runs a whistleblower program for securities fraud, with possible awards. The IRS has one for major tax fraud too.

Montana: Workers who experienced retaliation can contact the Montana Human Rights Bureau for an intake interview, and a formal complaint must be filed within 180 days of the alleged action.

Bail & bail bondsmen in Montana

Someone you love is sitting in jail and you need them out. Here's how bail works in Montana.

Two ways to post bail: pay the full amount to the court yourself (you get it back when the case ends), or hire a bail bondsman — they post it for a fee, usually around 10% of the bail amount, which you do not get back.

Montana specifics: Commercial surety bail bond producers are licensed by the Commissioner of Securities and Insurance.

Protect yourself: only use a licensed bondsman, get every fee in writing before paying, and never hand over cash without a receipt and paperwork. Unlicensed operators are a real scam risk when families are desperate.

Filming the police: your rights in Montana

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

All-party consent

State law generally requires everyone recorded to consent — but on-duty officers in public have no reasonable expectation of privacy, so courts have held that recording them is protected. (Mont. Code Ann. § 45-8-213(1)(c))

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Montana has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

Montana bars recording a conversation with a hidden device 'without the knowledge of all parties' — so notifying everyone you are recording (not their approval) satisfies the law. Openly filming officers on duty is protected by the First Amendment in the 9th Circuit (Fordyce v. City of Seattle). No Montana buffer-zone law exists.

What to do next:

  1. Keep a safe distance and don’t interfere — that’s the line the law draws.
  2. Know your state’s audio-recording rule stated above before you hit record.
  3. If you believe your rights were violated, write down exactly what happened and report it: how to report a civil rights violation.

Defamation in Montana

Someone posted or said something false about you? In Montana, defamation means a false statement of fact, shared with others, that harmed your reputation. Truth is a complete defense, and pure opinions (“I think he’s a jerk”) generally don’t count.

Deadline to sue: 2 years from when the statement was made (single 2-year limit for libel and slander (MCA § 27-2-204(1))). Miss it and you lose the right to file.

Anti-SLAPP: Montana has an anti-SLAPP law — it lets people get baseless lawsuits over speech thrown out quickly, and the person who filed can be ordered to pay the other side’s legal costs.

What to do next:

  1. Save everything. Screenshots with dates, links to the posts, and names of anyone who saw them. Don’t rely on the post staying up.
  2. Don’t fire back publicly. Responding online can hurt your case later.
  3. Talk to a lawyer who handles defamation. Many offer a free first consultation or take strong cases on contingency — you don’t pay unless you win.

Find a lawyer through the referral section on this page →

Domestic abuse & protection orders in Montana

If someone is hurting you or your kids, or threatening to, Montana courts can order them to stay away. It’s called an Order of Protection.

How to get one: go to your county courthouse and ask for the protection-order paperwork — filing is free. A judge reviews it, usually the same day, and can grant a temporary order immediately. A hearing is scheduled within a couple of weeks where both sides can speak.

If you are in danger right now, call 911. For free confidential help anytime, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788.

Abortion laws in Montana

Abortion is legal in Montana up to fetal viability (about 24 weeks), and after that when a health care professional determines it is medically indicated to protect the patient's life or health, under the 2024 constitutional amendment (CI-128).

These laws change fast — court rulings and new legislation can shift the rules with little notice. Always confirm the current law through the official source below before making decisions.

Questions? The National Abortion Federation hotline: 1-800-772-9100.
