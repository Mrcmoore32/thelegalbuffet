How divorce filing works in Minnesota

In Minnesota, divorce is legally called a “dissolution of marriage.” Here's the process in plain language:

  1. Check residency. At least one spouse must have lived in Minnesota for 180 days before filing.
  2. File in the right county. You file with the district court in the county where either spouse lives.
  3. Use the court's free tools. Minnesota's courts offer Guide & File, a free online interview that builds your divorce forms for you, plus every form as a free download.
  4. Serve your spouse and follow the steps. The court's self-help pages walk through each phase, including what happens if your spouse doesn't respond.

Car repossession in Minnesota

If you're behind on car payments, know your rights before the tow truck shows up — and after.

Your rights

What they can't do

No breaking into a locked garage, no physical force, no threats — that's "breach of the peace" and it's illegal everywhere. They must return your personal belongings from the car.

Get it back

Cure and redemption

No state cure right for vehicle loans — only what your contract allows. (The 30-day cure statute covers manufactured homes, not cars.) Pay the full balance plus reasonable costs any time before the car is sold.

Money owed

Deficiency balance

Allowed — but barred if your original loan was $7,200 or less.

Notice

What they must tell you

No advance warning required. Pre-sale notice (time, place, manner, redemption amount) and post-sale accounting required.

What to do next:

  1. If your car was taken, call the lender immediately and ask for the exact payoff or cure amount in writing.
  2. Remove valuables from your car now — don't wait for repossession.
  3. If the repo agent used force, threats, or broke into a locked space, document everything and contact free legal help in Minnesota.

What to bring to court in Minnesota

Walking in prepared makes the day go smoother and helps the judge see your case clearly. Use this as a packing list — a starting point, not legal advice. Your court or your lawyer may ask for more.

  1. Your photo ID and your case number. Courts run on case numbers — bring the one from your court papers.
  2. Copies of every document you filed, plus two extra sets. One set for the judge, one for the other side, one for yourself.
  3. Evidence that backs your claims, in order. Dated messages, receipts, photos, statements — a paper trail beats memory. Bring originals where you have them.
  4. A one-page timeline. What happened, in date order, and who was there. Judges love timelines.
  5. Financial records if money is at issue. Pay stubs, bank statements, bills, tax returns — bring more than you think you’ll need.
  6. A pen and paper. To write down the judge’s orders, next dates, and anything you’re told to do.

Before the day: call the courthouse (or check its website) to confirm the courtroom, the time, and anything you’re not sure about. Arrive 30 minutes early — you’ll go through security and need time to find the room. Leave anything distracting at home and silence your phone before you walk in.

Car accident injuries in Minnesota

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for Minnesota: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

Minnesota’s system: Minnesota is a no-fault state: your own auto insurance pays your medical bills first, no matter who caused the crash.

Deadline: In Minnesota you generally have 6 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Whistleblower protection in Minnesota

Fired, demoted, or punished for reporting something illegal or unsafe at work? The law may protect you — but deadlines can be as short as 30 days, so don’t wait.

Workplace safety: Federal law protects workers who report safety or health hazards. Complaints go to OSHA.

Financial fraud: The SEC runs a whistleblower program for securities fraud, with possible awards. The IRS has one for major tax fraud too.

Minnesota: Workers who believe their employer retaliated against them for making a safety or health complaint, reporting an injury, or contacting OSHA can file with MNOSHA Compliance within 30 days of the retaliatory action.

Bail & bail bondsmen in Minnesota

Someone you love is sitting in jail and you need them out. Here's how bail works in Minnesota.

Two ways to post bail: pay the full amount to the court yourself (you get it back when the case ends), or hire a bail bondsman — they post it for a fee, usually around 10% of the bail amount, which you do not get back.

Minnesota specifics: Commercial bail bond agencies operate under state license — 'surety bail bond producers' are licensed through the Department of Commerce.

Protect yourself: only use a licensed bondsman, get every fee in writing before paying, and never hand over cash without a receipt and paperwork. Unlicensed operators are a real scam risk when families are desperate.

Filming the police: your rights in Minnesota

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (Minn. Stat. § 626A.02, subd. 2(d))

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Minnesota has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

One-party consent state: any participant may record a conversation. Minneapolis's January 2025 DOJ consent decree explicitly orders officers not to interfere with civilians photographing or recording them in public. Some federal sources note the First Amendment right to record police is not yet 'clearly established' in the 8th Circuit, which affects civil suits against officers.

What to do next:

  1. Keep a safe distance and don’t interfere — that’s the line the law draws.
  2. Know your state’s audio-recording rule stated above before you hit record.
  3. If you believe your rights were violated, write down exactly what happened and report it: how to report a civil rights violation.

Defamation in Minnesota

Someone posted or said something false about you? In Minnesota, defamation means a false statement of fact, shared with others, that harmed your reputation. Truth is a complete defense, and pure opinions (“I think he’s a jerk”) generally don’t count.

Deadline to sue: 2 years from when the statement was made (single 2-year limit for libel and slander (Minn. Stat. § 541.07(1))). Miss it and you lose the right to file.

Anti-SLAPP: Minnesota has an anti-SLAPP law — it lets people get baseless lawsuits over speech thrown out quickly, and the person who filed can be ordered to pay the other side’s legal costs.

What to do next:

  1. Save everything. Screenshots with dates, links to the posts, and names of anyone who saw them. Don’t rely on the post staying up.
  2. Don’t fire back publicly. Responding online can hurt your case later.
  3. Talk to a lawyer who handles defamation. Many offer a free first consultation or take strong cases on contingency — you don’t pay unless you win.

Domestic abuse & protection orders in Minnesota

If someone is hurting you or your kids, or threatening to, Minnesota courts can order them to stay away. It’s called an Order for Protection.

How to get one: go to your county courthouse and ask for the protection-order paperwork — filing is free. A judge reviews it, usually the same day, and can grant a temporary order immediately. A hearing is scheduled within a couple of weeks where both sides can speak.

If you are in danger right now, call 911. For free confidential help anytime, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788.
