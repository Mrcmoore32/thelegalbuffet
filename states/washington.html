Car accident injuries in Washington

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for Washington: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

Washington’s system: Washington is an at-fault state: the driver who caused the crash — and their insurance — is responsible for your injuries.

Deadline: In Washington you generally have 3 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Whistleblower protection in Washington

Fired, demoted, or punished for reporting something illegal or unsafe at work? The law may protect you — but deadlines can be as short as 30 days, so don’t wait.

Workplace safety: Federal law protects workers who report safety or health hazards. Complaints go to OSHA.

Financial fraud: The SEC runs a whistleblower program for securities fraud, with possible awards. The IRS has one for major tax fraud too.

Washington: Washington workers punished for exercising workplace safety and health rights can file a retaliation complaint with the state Department of Labor & Industries through its online form, by mail, or in person at an L&I office.

Filming the police: your rights in Washington

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

All-party consent

State law generally requires everyone recorded to consent — but on-duty officers in public have no reasonable expectation of privacy, so courts have held that recording them is protected. (Wash. Rev. Code § 9.73.030)

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Washington has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

State v. Flora (Wash. Ct. App. 1992) held that conversations with police during an arrest are not 'private,' so the all-party-consent law does not bar recording on-duty police in public. The state attorney general has also opined that interactions with on-duty police are presumed public. The 9th Circuit recognized a First Amendment right to film matters of public interest in Fordyce v. City of Seattle (1995).

If a police officer, employer, landlord, or other authority treats you worse because of your race, sex, disability, religion, or another protected trait, that’s a civil rights violation — and you can report it.
