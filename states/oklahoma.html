Filming the police: your rights in Oklahoma

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (Okla. Stat. tit. 13, § 176.4)

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Oklahoma has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

The 10th Circuit (Irizarry v. Yehia, 2022), which covers Oklahoma, held the First Amendment protects filming on-duty police in public and that the right was clearly established by 2019. Recording itself is not obstructing an officer (Okla. Stat. tit. 21, § 540), but physically getting in the way still is.

Civil rights violations in Oklahoma

If a police officer, employer, landlord, or other authority treats you worse because of your race, sex, disability, religion, or another protected trait, that’s a civil rights violation — and you can report it. Deadlines are short: many complaints must be filed within 180 days to a year of what happened, so don’t wait.
