Car accident injuries in Alaska

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for Alaska: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

Alaska’s system: Alaska is an at-fault state: the driver who caused the crash — and their insurance — is responsible for your injuries.

Deadline: In Alaska you generally have 2 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Whistleblower protection in Alaska

Fired, demoted, or punished for reporting something illegal or unsafe at work? The law may protect you — but deadlines can be as short as 30 days, so don’t wait.

Workplace safety: Federal law protects workers who report safety or health hazards. Complaints go to OSHA.

Financial fraud: The SEC runs a whistleblower program for securities fraud, with possible awards. The IRS has one for major tax fraud too.

Alaska: Alaska workers can file a whistleblower retaliation complaint with AKOSH (Alaska Occupational Safety and Health) within 30 days of the retaliatory action.

Bail & bail bondsmen in Alaska

Someone you love is sitting in jail and you need them out. Here's how bail works in Alaska.

Alaska specifics: Alaska largely replaced money bail with risk-based release in 2018 (judges keep some discretion); licensed bail bond producers still operate for remaining cases.

Protect yourself: only use a licensed bondsman, get every fee in writing before paying, and never hand over cash without a receipt and paperwork.

Filming the police: your rights in Alaska

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (Alaska Stat. § 42.20.310(a))

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Alaska has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

A 2025 governor-backed bill (SB 85) would have switched Alaska to all-party consent, but it stalled in committee and never passed. The 9th Circuit (Fordyce v. City of Seattle) protects filming police doing their jobs in public.

Domestic abuse & protection orders in Alaska

If someone is hurting you or your kids, or threatening to, Alaska courts can order them to stay away. It’s called a Domestic Violence Protective Order.

How to get one: go to your county courthouse and ask for the protection-order paperwork — filing is free. A judge reviews it, usually the same day, and can grant a temporary order immediately. A hearing is scheduled within a couple of weeks where both sides can speak.

If you are in danger right now, call 911. For free confidential help anytime, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788.
