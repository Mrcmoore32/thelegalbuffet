Filming the police: your rights in New Hampshire

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

All-party consent

State law generally requires everyone recorded to consent — but on-duty officers in public have no reasonable expectation of privacy, so courts have held that recording them is protected. (N.H. Rev. Stat. § 570-A:2)

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

New Hampshire has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

All-party consent state for private conversations, but Gericke v. Begin, 753 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. 2014), held that openly filming police during a traffic stop in New Hampshire is a clearly established First Amendment right. RSA 91-A separately gives an explicit right to record public meetings. No buffer-zone law exists.
