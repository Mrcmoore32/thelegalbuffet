Car accident injuries in Nevada

A car crash can turn your life upside down in seconds. Here is the practical rundown for Nevada: what to do first, how the insurance system works, and your deadline to act.

Right after the crash: call 911, get a police report, exchange information with the other driver, take photos, see a doctor even if you feel okay, don't admit fault, and notify your own insurer promptly.

Nevada’s system: Nevada is an at-fault state: the driver who caused the crash — and their insurance — is responsible for your injuries.

Deadline: In Nevada you generally have 2 years from the crash to file an injury lawsuit. Miss it and you lose the right to sue.

Cost: Most car-accident lawyers work on contingency — free consultation, and they only get paid if you win, out of the settlement.

Whistleblower protection in Nevada

Fired, demoted, or punished for reporting something illegal or unsafe at work? The law may protect you — but deadlines can be as short as 30 days, so don’t wait.

Workplace safety: Federal law protects workers who report safety or health hazards. Complaints go to OSHA.

Financial fraud: The SEC runs a whistleblower program for securities fraud, with possible awards. The IRS has one for major tax fraud too.

Nevada: Nevada workers can file a complaint online with the Nevada Equal Rights Commission.

Filming the police: your rights in Nevada

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (Nev. Rev. Stat. § 200.650 (in-person); § 200.620 (phone calls))

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Nevada has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

Nevada is split: face-to-face conversations need only one party's consent (NRS 200.650), while phone calls need all-party consent (NRS 200.620). Nevada goes further than most states to protect filming: NRS 171.1233, enacted 2020, expressly says anyone may record law enforcement activity and bars officers from preventing, threatening, or ordering someone to stop recording.
