Spousal support and dividing retirement accounts (QDROs)

Two money questions decide more divorces than anything else: will one spouse pay support to the other, and who gets what share of the retirement savings? Here is how Virginia answers both — plus the extra court order, the QDRO, that most people have never heard of until it is almost too late. One federal tax note first: for divorces finalized after December 31, 2018, spousal support is not tax-deductible for the payer and not taxable income for the receiver.

Spousal support

How Virginia decides support — and how long it lasts

Virginia calls it spousal support / maintenance and support. For temporary (pendente lite) support Virginia DOES use a presumptive formula in the statute opened: with minor children in common, 26% of the payer's monthly gross income minus 58% of the payee's; with no minor children in common, 27% of the payer's minus 50% of the payee's — and the court may deviate for good cause. That formula only applies when combined monthly gross income does not exceed $10,000 (Va. Code 16.1-278.17:1). The pendente lite formula is only for support while the case is pending.

Dividing retirement

Retirement accounts and pensions in a Virginia divorce

No retirement-division fact is stated here because the official equitable-distribution page was not opened before the browser limit. Virginia Retirement System (VRS) benefits are divided through an Approved Domestic Relations Order (ADRO), not a standard ERISA QDRO: VRS publishes its own ADRO form for its defined-benefit plan, the order is submitted to VRS after the judge enters it, and VRS determines whether it qualifies as an ADRO, per the VRS form/instructions in official varetire.org search results.

The QDRO: the separate order that actually splits a workplace plan

For most workplace plans — 401(k)s and private pensions covered by the federal ERISA law — the divorce decree by itself does not split the account. A separate court order called a QDRO (Qualified Domestic Relations Order) does that. The usual steps: (1) the settlement or judgment states exactly what share each spouse gets; (2) the QDRO is drafted — many plans publish a model form, so ask the plan administrator for theirs and have the plan pre-approve your draft before the judge signs; (3) the judge signs it; (4) a certified copy goes to the plan administrator, who approves it and sets up the former spouse’s separate share. Start before the divorce is final and finish right after: waiting can cost the benefit entirely if the plan member retires, dies, or remarries first. Government plans do not use QDROs — federal civilian and military retired pay, railroad retirement, and most state and local plans each have their own kind of court order and their own forms (the Virginia card above covers the state plan where it applies). Tax note: money paid from a plan to a former spouse under a QDRO can be rolled into an IRA to defer the tax, and a QDRO payout is exempt from the usual 10% early-withdrawal penalty.

Prenuptial agreements: what they can and cannot do

A prenuptial agreement ("prenup") is a contract two people sign before marrying that sets their own rules for property and debts if the marriage ends in divorce or one of them dies — instead of the defaults in this section. It can say that a business, a house, or an inheritance stays one spouse's separate property, decide which debts belong to whom, and in many states limit or waive spousal support. What it can never do is decide child custody or child support: those belong to the court at the time, judged by the child's best interests, and a judge will not let a contract signed before the wedding override them. For a prenup to hold up, the pattern in most states is the same: it must be in writing and signed; both sides must give a full, honest picture of their money first (hidden assets are the classic way prenups die); and it must be signed willingly, with real time to think — an agreement presented the week of the wedding invites a coercion challenge. The single strongest protection is that each person has their own lawyer; one shared lawyer, or none, is the fact judges look at hardest when the poorer spouse later challenges the deal. Agreements signed after the wedding (postnuptial agreements) exist too, but courts in many states scrutinize them more closely. If you are the one being asked to sign, the consultation fee for your own lawyer to read it is the best money in the whole wedding budget.

Postnuptial agreements: the contract you sign after the wedding

A postnuptial agreement (“postnup”) is the same kind of contract as a prenup — it sets your own rules for property, debts, and sometimes spousal support if the marriage ends — except you sign it after the wedding, while you are married and planning to stay that way. (It is not a separation agreement: that is the paperwork for couples who are already splitting up.) Courts look harder at postnups than at prenups. Before the wedding, two people bargain at arm’s length; after it, they are spouses, and in many states the law says spouses owe each other a fiduciary duty — a duty of honesty and fair dealing like the one between business partners — so a judge checks a postnup for pressure, secrecy, and unfairness much more closely. Only two states, Colorado and North Dakota, have adopted the Uniform Law Commission’s model act covering these agreements (the UPMAA, 2012); everywhere else the rules come from each state’s own statutes or court decisions, and a few states barely enforce postnups at all. Almost everywhere, the basics are the same: the agreement must be in writing and signed by both spouses, entered into freely, based on a full and honest disclosure of each spouse’s money, and not grossly unfair. And like a prenup, it can never decide child custody or child support — those always belong to the court.

Virginia: Virginia law expressly lets married couples make the same kind of property and support agreement as engaged couples, effective as soon as it is signed.

Common-law marriage: married without a wedding — in a few states, if you meant to be

A common-law marriage is a real marriage formed without a license or ceremony: the couple agrees, now, to be married, lives together, and holds themselves out to the world as spouses. The popular version — "live together seven years and you're married" — is a myth; no state has a years rule, and simply living together, however long, creates no marriage anywhere. Only a few states still let couples form one: as of this writing, Colorado, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, and Texas (which calls it "informal marriage"), plus the District of Columbia — some by statute, Oklahoma and Rhode Island through their court decisions. Utah is narrower still: it does not have common-law marriage, but a court can declare an unsolemnized relationship a valid marriage if the couple petitions in time — during the relationship or within a year after it ends — and Utah closes that door to new petitions on May 5, 2027, so Utah couples should not wait. New Hampshire's rule operates only after a partner dies, for inheritance purposes. Several other states honor common-law marriages that were validly formed before they abolished the practice. And every state recognizes a common-law marriage that was validly formed in a state that allows it — which cuts both ways: move away and you are still married. That is why this card sits in the divorce section: a common-law marriage is ended the same way as any other, with a divorce, and splitting up without one leaves you legally married — to someone you may have to find years later when a house, a pension, or a new marriage depends on it. If you believe you may be in one, a family-law consultation is worth far more than the guesswork.

What to do next:

  1. Make sure you meet the residency requirement listed above before you file.
  2. Get the court’s free forms or online filing tool (linked above) and fill them out completely.
  3. File with the district court in the county where either spouse lives, and pay the filing fee.
  4. Know the cost. The filing fee is about $89.
  5. Serve your spouse properly and follow the court’s steps from there — the self-help pages walk through each phase.

Fishing & Hunting Violations in Virginia

A day outdoors can end with a citation — no license, one fish over the limit, the wrong season — and in every state that piece of paper is a real court case with real penalties. This section covers both sides in Virginia: what the everyday violations cost, why poaching is punished so much harder, what to do if a game warden cites you, and how to report poaching when you see it.

If you’re cited

Everyday violations — and what they cost in Virginia

Hunting, trapping, or fishing without a license when one is required is a Class 3 misdemeanor — a fine of not more than $500 — plus a fee equal to the cost of the required license. Taking game or fish during the closed season or exceeding the bag or creel limit is a Class 2 misdemeanor, as is the general penalty for other violations of Title 29.1 unless a different penalty is specified; a Class 2 misdemeanor carries up to six months in jail and a fine of not more than $1,000, either or both.

Poaching costs more

When it stops being a ticket

For closed-season or over-limit taking and other Title 29.1 violations, the court may also prohibit hunting, trapping, or fishing in Virginia for one to five years, and a second conviction within three years revokes the license and privileges so the person cannot reapply or exercise them for 12 months. The court must also assess the approximate replacement value of the animals, birds, or fish unlawfully taken and order it paid (the statute sets no fixed dollar amount), illegally taken wildlife is forfeited to the Commonwealth, and unlawful devices are forfeited — including a boat and firearm used to kill deer from watercraft. Selling or buying wildlife illegally is a Class 1 misdemeanor, and it becomes a Class 6 felony when the sales or purchases total $1,000 or more during any 90-day period.

What to do:

  1. Treat a citation like a court case — because it is one. A game warden’s ticket carries a court date or a payment deadline. Ignoring it can add charges and cost you your license on top of the fine. If you believe the citation is wrong, your chance to fight it is in court, not at the truck.
  2. Write everything down the same day. Where you were, what the officer said, who was with you. Keep your license, tags, and the citation together — memories fade faster than court dates arrive.
  3. A conviction in Virginia can follow you to other states. Most states honor each other’s hunting and fishing suspensions through the Interstate Wildlife Violator Compact, so losing your privileges here can mean losing them across most of the country.
  4. Serious charge? Talk to a lawyer before your court date. If the case involves big fines, possible jail, paying restitution for an animal, or years of license loss, a lawyer who handles fish and game cases can usually do more before the hearing than after it.
  5. Saw poaching instead? Report it. Report a violation to the Virginia DWR Wildlife Crime Line at 1-800-237-5712, or by email to WildCrime@dwr.virginia.gov; DWR states on another official page that violations can be reported anonymously to that hotline.

Seasons, bag limits, and legal methods change every year and differ by species and area — the current Virginia hunting and fishing regulations are the final word, not last year’s booklet. Intellectual Property in Virginia

Intellectual property is the law of what you create — and it comes in four kinds that people mix up all the time. Copyright covers creative work: writing, music, photos, software. A trademark protects a business name, logo, or slogan. A patent protects an invention. A trade secret protects valuable information you keep secret. Most of the rules are federal, the same in every state — but Virginia adds two things of its own: a cheaper state trademark registration and its own trade secret law, both covered below.

Copyright

Your work is protected the moment you create it

Copyright is automatic — you own your original work as soon as you write it, record it, or take the photo, and the © symbol is optional. Registering with the U.S. Copyright Office is what gives that protection its muscle: in most cases you cannot sue for infringement until you have registered, and registering early can make you eligible for statutory damages and attorney fees if someone copies you. Registration is online and cheap — $45 for a single work by one author, $65 for a standard application. A smaller dispute (up to $30,000) can go to the Copyright Claims Board instead of federal court — a process designed to work without a lawyer.

Register a trademark in Virginia

Using a name or logo in business gives you some rights automatically, but registration makes them much stronger. A federal registration with the USPTO costs a $350 base fee per class and protects your mark nationwide. A state registration costs far less and protects your mark inside Virginia — often the right first step for a business that only sells in-state. In Virginia, trademark registration is handled by the State Corporation Commission, Division of Securities and Retail Franchising. The filing fee is $30 per class, and the Virginia registration lasts 5 years. Always search before you file: if someone is already using a similar name for similar goods, your application can be refused and the fee is gone.

Patents

Inventors: start with a provisional application

Patents are federal only — there is no state patent. The affordable first step is a provisional application: it locks in your filing date for 12 months while you test and improve the idea, and the USPTO filing fee is $65 for most individual inventors (micro entity), $130 for a small entity, or $325 at the standard rate. Miss the 12-month deadline and that filing date is gone for good. Virginia inventors are served by the Federal Circuit Bar Association National Clearinghouse, part of the USPTO’s Patent Pro Bono Program — free help from volunteer patent attorneys for inventors who qualify. And be careful with “invention promotion” companies that promise to make you rich — federal law requires them to give you their success rates in writing before you sign, and very few of their customers ever make money.

Trade secrets

No registration — protection lasts only while it stays secret

A recipe, a customer list, a way of doing the work: if it has value because it is secret, trade secret law can protect it — but only while you actually treat it as secret. Use written confidentiality agreements (NDAs), passwords, and need-to-know access; a secret you hand out freely stops being one. In Virginia, trade secrets are covered by the Uniform Trade Secrets Act, Va. Code §§ 59.1-336 through 59.1-343. The federal Defend Trade Secrets Act also lets you sue in federal court in every state.

Scam warning

Official-looking trademark letters are usually not official

After a trademark filing becomes public, private companies mine the records and mail letters that look like government bills — hundreds of dollars to “register,” “renew,” or “monitor” your mark. Real USPTO fees are paid only through the USPTO itself, never by mailing a check to a company with a Washington-sounding name. Check any letter against your own application record at uspto.gov before paying anyone.

What to do:

  1. Match the problem to the right kind of protection. Copyright = creative work. Trademark = name, logo, slogan. Patent = invention. Trade secret = valuable secret. Filing the wrong kind wastes the fee.
  2. Search before you spend anything. Use the USPTO’s free trademark search for names and logos. For an invention, search existing patents first — Google Patents is free — because someone else may already own the idea.
  3. Register your copyright online. $45 to $65 at copyright.gov — and do it soon after you publish, while the strongest remedies are still on the table.
  4. Pick state or federal for your trademark. Only selling in Virginia? The state filing above is cheaper. Selling — or planning to sell — across state lines? Go federal at the USPTO.
  5. Inventors: provisional first, deadline in ink. File the provisional application, write the 12-month deadline on the calendar, and ask the Patent Pro Bono Program for free help before paying any promoter.
  6. Keep secrets like secrets. NDAs in writing, passwords on files, customer lists shared on a need-to-know basis. The law protects the secrets you protect.
  7. Got an official-looking fee letter? Stop. Compare it against uspto.gov first. Most of those letters are private solicitations you can throw away.

Copyright, trademark, and patent rules are mostly federal: the Copyright Act (17 U.S.C.), the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. §§ 1051–1127), the Patent Act (35 U.S.C.), and the Defend Trade Secrets Act (18 U.S.C. § 1836); Uniform Trade Secrets Act, Va. Code §§ 59.1-336 through 59.1-343 (Virginia). End of Life & Death with Dignity in Virginia

This section covers end-of-life law in Virginia: medical aid in dying, advance directives, naming someone to make health care decisions for you, POLST medical orders, and refusing treatment or choosing hospice care. The law differs sharply from state to state on these questions, so what follows is what Virginia law says, in plain language. This is general information, not legal advice.

Medical aid in dying in Virginia

Medical aid in dying is not authorized in Virginia. Va. Code § 54.1-2990(D) states that the Health Care Decisions Act shall not be construed to condone, authorize, or approve mercy killing or euthanasia or to permit any affirmative or deliberate act or omission to end life other than to permit the natural process of dying, and Va. Code § 8.01-622.1 provides an injunction against assisted suicide.

  • Agent (appointed in an Advance Directive): Appoint an adult agent in your advance directive. POST (Physician Orders for Scope of Treatment) in Virginia

POST is a physician order recognized as a Durable Do Not Resuscitate order under 12VAC5-66. It must be signed by a physician, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant, and it accompanies the patient on transfer or discharge.

You may direct withholding or withdrawal of life-prolonging procedures in an advance directive when you have a terminal condition. Comfort care and pain relief must still be provided, and a physician who finds proposed care medically or ethically inappropriate must document that finding and inform you or your agent.

If you run a daycare or you are choosing one for your child, Virginia has one licensing agency and one set of rules. Licensing in Virginia — at a glance

  • Licensing agency: Virginia Department of Education, Office of Child Care Health and Safety

Pulled Over: Your Rights in Virginia

Getting pulled over is stressful even when nothing is wrong. Most of a traffic stop works the same in every state — the steps and rights below are federal. The card at the top covers the few things your state does differently.

At a traffic stop in Virginia

  • Carrying a firearm: If you are carrying a firearm, you must disclose it when the officer asks — including showing your permit on demand.
  • Refusing a DUI test: First refusal: 1-year suspension, stacked on the administrative suspension. A repeat refusal within 10 years brings 3 years, and a repeat breath refusal is also a Class 1 misdemeanor.
  • Signing the ticket: Refusing to sign does not by itself get you arrested — the officer notes the refusal on the summons and must release you.
  • Official source: Official Virginia traffic-stop source

Worth knowing in Virginia

Failure to display your concealed-handgun permit on demand carries a $25 civil penalty, which a court may waive if you later present a valid permit.

Step by step: what to do at the stop

  1. Pull over safely. Slow down, signal, and stop somewhere safe and lit if you can. Hazards on.
  2. Set the stage. Engine off, window down, interior light on at night, hands on the wheel where they can be seen.
  3. Announce your movements. "My registration is in the glove box" before you reach. Sudden reaching is what makes stops dangerous.
  4. Hand over license, registration, and insurance. The driver must provide these when asked — that is the law everywhere.
  5. You don't have to chat. Beyond identifying yourself, you may politely decline questions: "I'd rather not answer questions." A routine stop does not require Miranda warnings — silence is still your right.
  6. Don't consent to a search. If asked, say clearly: "I don't consent to a search." Consent makes a search legal even when the officer had no right to search without it. Never physically resist a search — say no, and fight it in court.
  7. Orders are different from requests. An officer may order you — and your passengers — out of the car. Comply with orders; ask about the rest.
  8. Ask before you assume it's over: "Am I free to go?" If yes, leave calmly. If you are being arrested, say you want a lawyer and then stay silent.
  9. Note the details right after: the officer's name and badge number, the agency, time, place, and what was said. Write it down while it is fresh.

Searches, dogs, and how long a stop can last

  • A stop needs a lawful reason to start — an officer may not pull you over on a hunch (Delaware v. Prouse).
  • Without probable cause, an officer needs your permission to search — and permission is yours to withhold. Under Rodriguez v. United States, a stop that is finished — or should be — cannot be stretched out to wait for a drug dog without reasonable suspicion.
  • An officer can pat down your outer clothing if they reasonably believe you are armed (Terry v. Ohio) — that is not the same as a full search, and it does not need your consent.

If it's a DUI stop

  • Roadside field sobriety tests are voluntary in most states — but refusing the chemical test after arrest is different. Every state has an implied-consent law, and refusal carries the consequence in your state's card above; in a few states, refusal is itself a crime.
  • Police generally need a warrant for a blood draw (Birchfield v. North Dakota) — breath tests are treated differently.
  • If you are arrested, further questions require Miranda warnings. Say you want a lawyer, clearly, and then stop talking.

If you believe you were profiled

Being stopped because of your race is unconstitutional — but be clear-eyed about how these cases work:

  • Under Whren v. United States, if the officer genuinely observed a traffic violation, courts usually uphold the stop even when the driver believes race was the real reason. That makes the pattern and the paper trail what matters.
  • Write down everything while it's fresh: badge number, agency, time, place, the reason given, and anything said about race. Ask whether dashcam or bodycam video exists — complaints and lawsuits can obtain it.
  • File the complaint anyway: with the agency itself, your state attorney general or civil-rights agency, and the U.S. Department of Justice Civil Rights Division. This page's Civil Rights section has the links and the deadlines — many run out in 180 days to a year.
  • Some states ban racial profiling by statute and require officers to record data at every stop; where those laws exist, your complaint becomes part of an official record that can prove a pattern.
  • If you were ticketed or arrested at a stop you believe was profiling, tell your lawyer about it at the first meeting — it can matter to the case even when the stop itself is hard to challenge.

Virginia allows online sports betting through licensed operators. There is no licensed online casino.

There is no federal gambling license and no single national law. Since the Supreme Court struck down the federal sports-betting ban in 2018, each state decides for itself — and most states allow some kinds of online gambling while banning others. An ad on national TV does not mean the app is licensed in your state.

Online gambling in Virginia at a glance

  • Online casino: Not offered — no licensed online casino operates.
  • Online poker: Not offered — no licensed online poker operates.
  • Minimum age: 21+.
  • Regulated by: Virginia Lottery.
  • Sweepstakes casinos: No ban in force — sweeps sites still operate here, in a legal gray area.
  • Official source: Official Virginia gambling source

Worth knowing in Virginia

You cannot bet on Virginia college teams — wagers on in-state schools such as UVA and Virginia Tech are prohibited.

Licensed vs. offshore — the distinction that matters

The single most important distinction in online gambling is not what you play — it is who holds the license.

  • Licensed sites are approved by the state regulator, must verify your age and location, must pay winnings, must offer self-exclusion and deposit limits, and answer to the state when something goes wrong.
  • Offshore sites (licensed in Curaçao, or nowhere) operate outside U.S. law. If one refuses to pay you, there is no regulator to call and usually no way to get your money. Running them is illegal in the U.S., and in a few states using them is an offense for the player too.
  • The 30-second check: a licensed operator appears on your state regulator's official licensee list, and its app will refuse your bet when you are physically outside the state. If a site takes your bet from anywhere and never checks where you are, it is not a U.S.-licensed site.

Sweepstakes casinos and prediction markets

  • Sweepstakes casinos — the "free" coin sites that let you cash out winnings — claimed they were not gambling at all. States have been shutting that door since 2025; check your state's line above. Where they are banned, operating one is usually the crime, not playing — but your balance can still vanish when a site is forced out of a state.
  • Prediction markets sell "event contracts" on sports and claim federal regulation lets them operate even where sports betting is illegal. State regulators disagree, lawsuits are flying, and New Jersey's case against one operator has reached the U.S. Supreme Court. Until the courts settle it, treat prediction-market sports contracts as legally unsettled: money deposited there sits outside your state's gambling protections.

Taxes — and if gambling stops being fun

  • Gambling winnings are taxable income in every state, licensed or not — and the IRS does not care whether the bet was legal. Big wins generate a W-2G form.
  • You may deduct losses only up to your winnings, and you must itemize. Starting with tax year 2026, only 90% of losses count — so a year where you broke even can still leave you owing tax on "profit" you never saw. Keep records of wins and losses as you go.
  • Every legal state runs a self-exclusion program: one signup bars you from every licensed site and sportsbook in the state, and the sites must stop marketing to you. Licensed apps also have to offer deposit and time limits.
  • Free, confidential help is available 24/7 at 1-800-522-4700 (National Problem Gambling Helpline) — call or text. Using it does not go on any record.

Heads up

Worth knowing in Virginia. Retail sales are now authorized by 2026 legislation but are not scheduled to begin until July 1, 2027 — until then, medical dispensaries are the only legal sellers. The public possession limit was also raised from 1 oz to 2 oz in 2026.

Federal law

The federal catch — read this before you travel, apply for a job, or buy a gun. Washington changed the rules in April 2026 — but only partway. A Justice Department order that took effect April 28, 2026 moved FDA-approved marijuana medicines and state-licensed medical marijuana to Schedule III, the less-strict federal drug category. Recreational marijuana was left out on purpose: it is still a Schedule I drug — illegal under federal law in every state, no matter what your state says. A separate case that could move all marijuana is still being decided. Until then:

Guns. Federal law bars anyone who uses a controlled substance unlawfully from buying or owning a gun — and recreational use is still unlawful federally, even where your state allows it. The federal firearm purchase form asks about marijuana use; answering falsely is itself a federal crime.

Jobs and drug tests. Federal employees, truck drivers, and anyone in a federally regulated drug-testing job can still lose the job — or the offer — over marijuana that was legal at home. A state medical card is not a shield.

Housing. Federally assisted housing can deny an application, or evict, over marijuana use — including state-legal medical use.

Federal land. National parks, national forests, and other federal land follow federal law. Possession there is a federal offense, even inside a legal state.

Travel. Never take marijuana across a state line — not even between two legal states — and never bring it to an airport or onto a plane.

Driving. In every state, driving after using marijuana is a DUI — a legal purchase is no defense. Keep any marijuana sealed and out of the driver’s reach, ideally in the trunk.

Deadline

The gas-station loophole closes this winter. The delta-8, THCA flower, and THC seltzers sold at gas stations and smoke shops under the farm-bill “hemp” loophole are losing their federal legal cover in two steps: lab-made cannabinoids the hemp plant cannot produce naturally on November 12, 2026, and — from December 11, 2026 — any finished hemp product with more than 0.4 mg of total THC in the container counts as marijuana under federal law. Products from licensed state dispensaries are not affected.

Trespassing in Virginia

Trespassing cuts both ways, so this section does too: what to do about the person who will not stay off your property, and what it means when you are the one told to leave or banned from a place. Most of it turns on notice — being told, a sign, a fence, or paint — though some offenses need no warning at all. Here is how Virginia draws the line, and what the basic offense costs.

Your property

Someone on your property who won’t leave?

Virginia is a pure warn-first state: § 18.2-119 makes it an offense to go upon or remain upon the lands, buildings, or premises of another after having been forbidden to do so, orally or in writing, by the owner, lessee, custodian, agent of the owner or lessee, or other person lawfully in charge of the premises — or after having been forbidden by signs posted by one of those persons at places where the signs may reasonably be seen. No forbidding and no posted sign means no offense under this section. Signs need only be reasonably visible; there are no size rules. Do not confuse this with Virginia's hunting paint law: posting against hunting, fishing, and trapping under § 18.2-134.1 uses aluminum or silver paint, not purple, and covers only those activities. There is no state warning form or registry.

Accused

Told you’re trespassing — or banned from a place?

Trespass after having been forbidden to do so is a Class 1 misdemeanor (§ 18.2-119) — Virginia's highest misdemeanor class. Whatever the merits, leave when you are told to: arguing your case on the spot can turn a warning into an arrest. Write down who told you, when, and where, and do not go back until it is resolved. If you believe the warning was wrong — you had permission, the place was public, or nobody ever actually told you — that is a defense to raise afterward, in writing or in court, not in the doorway.

Car booted or towed in Virginia

A boot — a clamp locked over your wheel — means your car is not moving until money changes hands. Cars get booted two ways: a city clamps the cars of people who pile up unpaid parking tickets, and a private parking-lot operator clamps a car it says parked without permission or payment. Who put it there decides which rules protect you, and in Virginia the two rulebooks are very different. Start with the notice left on your car: it names who booted you, what they say you owe, and the number to call.

City boot

Booted for unpaid parking tickets?

Local by state authorization — Va. Code § 46.2-1216 lets counties, cities, and towns adopt ordinances to remove or immobilize vehicles with three or more unpaid, unsettled parking-violation notices, done by or under the direction of the police or sheriff. Move today: many city programs tow a booted car that is not settled promptly — sometimes within 24 to 72 hours — and daily storage charges pile up after that. When you call, ask two questions: is there a payment plan, and how do I contest the tickets?

Private lot

Booted in a parking lot?

Under Va. Code § 46.2-1231, a lot owner or operator may have a trespassing vehicle immobilized with a boot instead of towed, provided the device does not damage the vehicle and signs at all entrances disclose that trespassing vehicles may be removed, towed, or immobilized, with a contact number. The charge for removing the boot may not exceed $25 or such other limit as the local governing body sets by ordinance, and localities may regulate further under § 46.2-1232. If the company broke these rules — no required sign, a charge over the cap, no release after you paid — you may not owe the fee at all. If you need the car now, pay and keep the receipt: paying gets your car back, it does not end the fight. Then complain to the regulator named in the official source below and ask for your money back.

Step 1

Becoming a U.S. citizen: naturalization, step by step

Who can apply — the basics

Naturalization is how a green-card holder becomes a U.S. citizen. In general you must be at least 18, have been a permanent resident for 5 years (3 years if you are married to and living with a U.S. citizen), have lived in the U.S. continuously and been physically present for at least half that time, have lived at least 3 months in the state where you file, and be a person of good moral character. You can file up to 90 days before you reach the 3- or 5-year mark. USCIS's eligibility worksheet (Form M-477, linked from the N-400 page below) walks through every requirement.

The form and the fee

You apply on Form N-400, online or on paper. As of the current USCIS fee schedule (Form G-1055), the fee is $710 filed online or $760 on paper, with biometrics included. If your household income is at or below 400% of the federal poverty guidelines, a reduced fee of $380 is available on a paper filing (requested in Part 10 of the N-400). If you receive a means-tested benefit, your income is at or below 150% of the guidelines, or you have a financial hardship, you may qualify for a full fee waiver with Form I-912 — the fee becomes $0. Fees change: confirm the current amount on the USCIS fee schedule before you file, because a wrong fee means a rejected application.

The two tests — and who is excused from them

At your interview an officer tests your English (speaking, reading, writing) and your knowledge of U.S. history and government (civics). Which civics test you take depends on your filing date: applications filed on or after October 20, 2025 use the 2025 test (study 128 questions; the officer asks 20 and you must answer 12 correctly); earlier applications use the 2008 test (100 questions; 10 asked, 6 to pass). Free study materials are on the USCIS site. Age-and-residence exemptions excuse the English test only: at 50 with 20 years as a permanent resident (50/20), or 55 with 15 years (55/15), you may take civics in your own language with your own interpreter. At 65 with 20 years (65/20), the civics test shrinks to 20 designated questions. A disability that prevents you from meeting the test requirements can excuse English, civics, or both — that takes Form N-648, completed by a medical professional.

Interview, decision, and the oath

After you file, USCIS schedules fingerprinting (biometrics), then the interview, where the officer reviews your application and administers the tests. Bring your green card, ID, and the documents your appointment notice lists; be ready to explain trips abroad and anything in your record — arrests must be disclosed even if charges were dropped. If approved, you take the Oath of Allegiance at a ceremony (sometimes the same day) and receive your Certificate of Naturalization. You are not a citizen until the oath — then register to vote and update your Social Security record.

Get help before you file if anything is complicated

A clean case is usually a do-it-yourself case. But if you have any arrest or immigration violation in your past, long trips abroad, tax or child-support problems, or a prior denial, have an accredited representative or immigration lawyer review your case first — the legal-aid organizations earlier in this section include immigration help, and a consultation costs far less than a denied application. Never pay a "notario" for immigration legal advice; in the U.S. that word does not mean lawyer.

Guardianship, conservatorship & long-term care in Virginia

When an adult can no longer make safe decisions about their own health, home, or money — after dementia, a brain injury, or a serious disability — someone has to be legally able to decide for them. Guardianship and conservatorship are the court’s answer, but they are also the most restrictive answer: a court order takes away an adult’s right to decide. That is why courts are supposed to try the least restrictive option first, and why this section starts with the alternatives. The Wills & probate section on this page covers what happens after death; this section covers what happens while the person is still alive and needs help deciding.

What it means

Guardianship vs. conservatorship: who decides what

A guardian is a person a court appoints to make personal decisions for an adult who cannot safely make them — where they live, what medical care they get, and day-to-day care. A conservator is a person a court appoints to handle money and property — paying bills, managing accounts, and protecting assets. In many states those are two separate jobs; in others, one appointment covers both, or the state uses only one of the words. The exact titles and powers in Virginia come from Virginia law and from the judge’s order itself, so read the order, not just the label: a good order is narrow, naming only the decisions the person truly cannot make, and leaving the rest with the person.

Step 1

Who can petition, and how the court process works

Usually a family member, close friend, or concerned professional files a petition in the probate court of the county where the adult lives, explaining what decisions the person can no longer make safely and what evidence supports that. The adult must be given notice and a chance to be heard, and in most states the court orders an independent evaluation — often by a doctor or a court visitor — and appoints a lawyer for the adult if they do not have one. At a hearing, the judge decides whether the person lacks capacity for specific decisions and, if so, who should serve and how broad the powers should be. Bring medical records, a list of the person’s existing helpers (family, home aides, a power of attorney), and any written wishes the person expressed while they could still decide.

Contested?

If family members disagree, or the adult objects

Guardianships are contested more often than people expect — siblings disagree about who should serve, or the adult themselves says they do not need a guardian. Anyone with an interest can object, present their own evidence, and ask the judge to choose a different person or a narrower order. If you object, say so in writing by the court’s deadline and show up: an unopposed petition is usually granted. After appointment, the guardian or conservator must report to the court — typically an annual report on the person’s condition and, for money, an accounting of every dollar in and out. If a guardian misuses money, ignores the person’s wishes, or is no longer needed, anyone can ask the court to review, limit, replace, or end the guardianship.

Try first

Less-restrictive alternatives: try these before court

Courts are supposed to prefer the least restrictive option that keeps the person safe, and these often avoid guardianship entirely: a durable power of attorney lets a trusted person handle money and sign documents (durable means it stays valid if the person later loses capacity); a health care directive or proxy names who makes medical decisions; supported decision-making — now recognized in a growing number of states — is a written agreement where the person keeps their legal rights and names supporters who help them understand options and communicate choices, rather than deciding for them; and practical tools like joint accounts, automatic bill pay, and a Social Security representative payee can solve a single problem without a court case. If the person can still understand and communicate with support, an alternative usually fits better than a guardianship — and it is far cheaper and faster.

A nursing-home resident keeps their rights: to be treated with dignity, to take part in their own care plan, to have visitors, to manage their own money or choose someone who will, and to complain — to the facility, to the state, or to the ombudsman — without retaliation. If you get a discharge notice, do not move the resident out quietly and do not miss the appeal deadline on the notice — call the ombudsman the same day (below). Yes. Virginia recognizes handwritten (holographic) wills (Va. Code §64.2-403(B)). The rules are exact — key parts usually must be in the maker’s own handwriting and signed — and a small mistake can void the will. A witnessed will is always safer.

When someone dies: the first week

Avoiding probate: trusts, TOD deeds, and beneficiary names

Probate is not always a disaster — the small-estate shortcuts elsewhere in this section may be all your family needs. But three tools can move your biggest assets outside probate entirely, and they are cheaper than most people expect. What they cannot do is replace a will: a will is still the safety net for everything the tools miss.

Tool 1 — every state

A living trust only works if it is funded

A revocable living trust lets a successor trustee hand your property to your family without probate — but only for assets you actually transferred into the trust while you were alive. Signing the trust paper and leaving the house, accounts, and titles in your own name accomplishes nothing; lawyers call the transfer step “funding,” and it is the step people forget. A pour-over will catches leftovers at death and pours them into the trust — but anything that arrives that way still passes through probate first. A trust costs more to set up than a will; what it buys is privacy and skipping the court process for the assets inside it.

Tool 2 — every state

Beneficiary designations beat your will

Retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death (POD) bank accounts, and transfer-on-death (TOD) brokerage accounts go to whoever is named on the account — not to whoever your will names. If the two disagree, the account paperwork wins. That makes designations the cheapest probate-avoidance there is, and the easiest to get wrong: review them after every marriage, divorce, birth, or death in the family, and name a backup (contingent) beneficiary too.

Tool 3 — in Virginia

Transfer-on-death deeds in Virginia

Virginia does authorize a transfer-on-death deed for real estate. It is called a 'transfer on death deed' under the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act, Va. Code §§ 64.2-624 through 64.2-634, and the circuit court clerk's office in the city or county where the land lies is where it is recorded. The deed does nothing until the owner dies, can be revoked at any time during life, and must be recorded before the owner's death to be effective; the Act does not supply an official fill-in form, so the deed must meet the statutory requirements.

Tool 4 — with a catch

Joint ownership: easy now, tradeoffs later

Property owned jointly with right of survivorship passes to the surviving owner automatically, with no probate. The honest tradeoff: while you are alive, the property is exposed to the other owner’s debts, lawsuits, and divorce, you generally cannot sell or change the arrangement without their signature, and for property that has grown in value the survivor may lose part of the tax basis step-up a probate transfer would have given. It is a real tool — just not a free one.

Quitclaim deeds: fast and common — but they promise nothing

What a quitclaim deed actually does

It transfers whatever interest the signer actually has in the property — if any. That is all. Unlike a warranty deed, a quitclaim makes no promise that the signer owns the property, that the title is clean, or that nobody else has a claim. If a problem turns up later, the person who received the property usually has no claim against the person who signed it over.

Where it is commonly used

Between people who trust each other and know the property: transfers between family members, adding or removing a spouse's name in a divorce, moving a home into a living trust, or clearing a possible claim from someone who may not own anything at all. Buying a home from a stranger with only a quitclaim is risky — you take every hidden title problem as your own.

The traps to check first

The mortgage does not move with the deed. If there is a loan, the borrower still owes it — and many mortgages let the lender demand full payment when ownership changes (federal law protects some family, divorce, and trust transfers; ask the lender in writing before you sign). Title insurance usually does not carry over to the new owner. And a gift transfer keeps the giver's original tax basis — no step-up — and can count against Medicaid eligibility if care is needed within the look-back window. For an expensive property, a short consult with a local real estate lawyer is cheap insurance.

How to do it right

Copy the names and the full legal description exactly from the current deed (get it from the county recorder if you do not have it). Follow your state's signing rules — many states print a statutory form (see the state card) and most deeds must be notarized. Then record the signed deed with the county recorder or clerk promptly: an unrecorded deed can lose to a later recorded claim. Keep a copy with your estate papers and tell your homeowner's insurer about the change.

Workers’ compensation in Virginia

If you are hurt at work or made sick by your job, workers’ compensation is the system that pays for your medical care and replaces part of your lost wages — and you do not have to prove your employer did anything wrong to use it. But the system runs on deadlines and paperwork, and the first steps matter more than people expect: report the injury in writing right away, get medical care and tell the provider it is a work injury, and keep a copy of everything. This section walks through the claim from the day of the injury to a denial or a return to work. It is the dedicated guide; the Workplace rights section on this page only mentions workers’ compensation in passing.

Step 1

Report the injury in writing, the same day if you can

Tell your supervisor or employer about the injury or illness as soon as possible, in writing — an email or a dated note counts, and keep a copy for yourself. Say what happened, when, where, and what part of your body was hurt. Deadlines for reporting vary by state, and in some states they are short (days or weeks), so do not wait to see if it gets better on its own. A late report is one of the most common reasons claims get denied. If your injury or illness developed over time — a bad back, a breathing problem, a repetitive-strain injury — report it as soon as a doctor connects it to your work.

Step 2

Get medical care, and say it is a work injury

Get care promptly, and tell every provider the injury or illness is work-related so the visit is billed to workers’ compensation, not to you or your health plan. In some states your employer or its insurer picks the first doctor; in others you pick. Follow the treatment plan and go to every appointment — missed care is used against claims. Keep every bill, work note, and restriction slip (for example, “no lifting over 10 pounds”), and give copies to your employer.

Step 3

Your employer reports it, the insurer decides

Your employer must report the injury to its workers’ compensation insurer, which opens a claim and decides whether to accept or deny it. Ask your employer for the insurer’s name, the claim number, and the adjuster’s contact information, and write them down. If your employer will not report it, or has no insurance, contact the Virginia agency below directly — that is what it is there for. Never pay for work-injury care out of pocket without telling the agency and the insurer first.

Benefits

What workers’ compensation pays: medical care and part of your wages

An accepted claim generally pays for reasonable and necessary medical treatment for the work injury, with no copay or deductible to you. If the injury keeps you from working, wage-replacement benefits replace part of your pay — in many states about two-thirds of your average weekly wage, up to a state maximum, after a short waiting period (a few days, which are often paid back if you are out longer). Exact rates, waiting periods, and how long benefits last are set by Virginia law, so check the agency page below rather than trusting a single national number. Permanent injury may also bring a separate scheduled payment.

Denied?

If the claim is denied, appeal — and watch the deadline

A denial letter must explain why, and it starts a clock: you can challenge it by filing an appeal or a claim petition with the Virginia agency, which leads to a hearing before a workers’ compensation judge or board. Appeal deadlines vary by state and are strict — read the letter the day it arrives, file even if your evidence is not complete yet, and bring your medical records, your written injury report, and any witness names to the hearing. Many workers’ compensation lawyers take these cases on contingency (paid only if you win), and the agency below can explain the process for free. If you are fired, demoted, or punished after filing, tell the agency promptly and get legal help — many states prohibit retaliation for filing a claim.

Return to work

Returning to work: light duty, restrictions, and honest communication

When your doctor clears you with restrictions, your employer may offer light-duty work that fits them. Accepting suitable light duty usually keeps benefits flowing and protects your job; refusing work that fits your restrictions can cut off wage benefits, so talk to your doctor and the agency before refusing anything. If no suitable work exists, say so in writing and keep certifying your restrictions. Never work outside your restrictions to “prove” you are fine — re-injury hurts your health and your claim.

Veterans & military legal help in Virginia

Veterans and active-duty service members have three separate legal toolkits that most people never hear about in one place: a way to challenge a VA benefits decision, a way to upgrade a less-than-honorable discharge, and a federal law that pauses or caps civilian debts and lawsuits while you serve. All three are federal, so they work the same in every state — what changes by state is only where you find free local help. The one veteran resource already on this page, the VA homelessness hotline, is not this guide: this section is about benefits decisions, discharge upgrades, and service-member protections.

VA decision

VA denied your claim? Pick the lane that matches what is wrong

You have three decision-review options, and the right one depends on your evidence. Higher-Level Review: a senior reviewer looks at the exact same file for an error — you cannot add new evidence, but you can request a one-time informal conference to point out the mistake. Supplemental Claim: you bring new and relevant evidence the VA did not have before, such as a new medical opinion or a buddy statement. Board Appeal: a Veterans Law Judge decides — on the existing record (Direct Review), with new evidence added within 90 days (Evidence Submission), or after a hearing where you testify (Hearing docket). You can choose a different option for each issue, but you cannot use two options for the same issue at the same time, and you cannot request a second Higher-Level Review or a second Board Appeal in a row on the same issue.

Deadline

The one-year VA clock — counted from the date on the letter

For most benefits, you have 1 year from the date printed on your VA decision letter — not the date you received it — to request a Higher-Level Review or a Board Appeal. A Supplemental Claim can be filed at any time, but filing it within that same year keeps your claim continuously pursued, which protects your original effective date and your back pay; filed later, the effective date generally moves to the new filing date. Exception: in a contested claim, where two people claim a benefit only one can receive, a Board Appeal is due within 60 days. Some benefit types have shorter limits stated on the letter itself, so read your letter first. After a Board decision you disagree with, you can file a Supplemental Claim with new and relevant evidence, or appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims within 120 days. VA’s published average goals are about 125 days for Higher-Level Reviews and Supplemental Claims, and 365 days (Direct Review), 550 days (Evidence Submission), or 730 days (Hearing) for Board appeals — goals, not guarantees.

Discharge

Discharge upgrade: DD Form 293 within 15 years, DD Form 149 after that

A discharge below Honorable can block VA benefits, jobs, and education aid, and it can be challenged. If you were discharged within the last 15 years, apply to your branch’s Discharge Review Board with DD Form 293 — the board can upgrade the characterization and change the reason, and it cannot make your discharge worse, but it cannot review a general court-martial discharge. If you are past 15 years, apply to your branch’s Board for Correction of Military Records with DD Form 149; its usual 3-year filing limit is often waived in the interest of justice. A denial from the review board can still go to the correction board. Include your DD 214, a short statement of why the discharge was unjust or in error, medical records, and proof of your life since service — and if PTSD, traumatic brain injury, or sexual trauma during service played a role, say so plainly: the boards must give that evidence liberal consideration.

SCRA

On active duty? The SCRA caps interest at 6% and pauses lawsuits

The Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (federal law, 50 U.S.C. §§ 3901–4043) protects active-duty members, reservists and Guard members on federal orders, and commissioned Public Health Service and NOAA officers, plus many protections for their dependents. The three most-used protections: (1) 6% interest cap on debts from before you entered service — credit cards, car loans, mortgages — but you must request it in writing from each creditor with a copy of your orders; the interest above 6% is forgiven, not deferred. (2) Lease termination without penalty when you receive orders for a permanent change of station, a deployment of 90 days or more, or separation/retirement — give written notice with your orders. (3) Default-judgment protection: a court cannot enter a default judgment against you while service keeps you from responding; it must appoint an attorney for you and can stay the case. Landlords also generally cannot evict you without a court order, and a pre-service mortgage cannot be foreclosed without a court order. None of this is automatic — you have to ask, in writing, with your orders attached.

Unemployment benefits in Virginia

Losing a job is expensive enough without losing benefits to a missed week or a missed deadline. Unemployment benefits replace part of your pay while you look for new work, but they are not automatic: you must file promptly, prove you are able to work and looking for work, and certify every week to keep the money coming. The rules below are the ones that hold in most states; where a number varies by state, this section says so and sends you to the Virginia agency for the exact figure, because trusting a national number is how people miss a deadline.

Step 1

File in your first week — benefits generally are not retroactive

File your claim in the first week you are out of work, even if you are still waiting on a final paycheck or severance paperwork. In most states you will not be paid for weeks before the week you filed, no matter how good the reason you waited. You will need your Social Security number, your employer’s name and address, your dates of employment, and your recent earnings. File in the state where you worked, not necessarily the state where you live now. If you were laid off, fired, or quit, answer honestly about why — the agency checks with your employer, and a mismatch delays everything.

Step 2

Certify every week, and keep looking for work

Once your claim is open, you must certify — usually online or by phone, every week or every two weeks depending on the state — that you were able to work, available for work, and looking for work, and you must report any earnings. Miss a certification and that week is not paid, even if you were eligible. Keep a written log of every job you apply for, with dates and employer names: the agency can ask for it, and a thin log is a common reason benefits stop. If you are sick, in school, or unavailable for a week, report it rather than certifying something untrue.

Denied?

If you are denied, appeal the day you get the notice

A denial (often called a determination) can be appealed to a hearing, usually before an administrative law judge, where you can present evidence and witnesses. The deadline varies by state — commonly about 10 to 30 days — and the clock usually runs from the mailing date printed on the notice, not the date you opened it, so read every notice the day it arrives and file your appeal that same day even if your evidence is not ready yet; you can add it before the hearing. If you miss the deadline, you may still file late with a good-cause reason, such as serious illness or never receiving the notice, but “I did not understand” is rarely enough. Keep certifying while your appeal is pending — if you win, you can only be paid for weeks you certified.

Overpayment

Overpayment notice? Do not ignore it — appeal it or ask about a waiver

An overpayment notice says you were paid benefits you were not entitled to, and it states an amount and a reason. In most cases overpayments must be repaid even if you already spent the money, and the agency can take it from future benefits or tax refunds if you do nothing. But you have options, and both have deadlines: appeal if the decision is wrong (for example, earnings you did report), and ask about a waiver if you were not at fault and repayment would cause real hardship — many states waive non-fault overpayments on hardship grounds, but the rules and the form are state-specific, so use the agency link below and do not assume a waiver is automatic. Never ignore the notice while you decide: an unanswered overpayment grows penalties and collection action.

Part-time work

Part-time work: report every dollar, and partial benefits may still be possible

Taking part-time or temporary work does not automatically end your benefits — but you must report all earnings for the week you earned them, not the week you were paid, and failing to report earnings is the fastest route to an overpayment and a fraud penalty. In most states part of your earnings is disregarded and the rest reduces your weekly benefit dollar-for-dollar or by a formula; earn too much in a week and that week pays nothing, but the claim itself usually stays open. The exact disregard and formula are set by Virginia law, so check the agency page below before you turn down a shift out of fear of losing everything, or accept one without reporting it.

Retaliation

Punished for speaking up?

It is illegal for an employer to fire, demote, or cut the hours of a worker for filing a discrimination charge, reporting a safety problem, or asking about unpaid wages. If that happens, tell the agency you filed with right away — retaliation is its own violation, with its own deadline.

Family and medical leave — FMLA and state paid leave

Need time off for a baby, your own serious illness or surgery, or to care for a family member? Two layers of law can protect your job — and in a growing number of states, part of your pay while you are out: the federal FMLA, and Virginia’s own leave laws. Leave under these laws generally runs at the same time as (not on top of) other leave, and most require you to ask in advance when the need is foreseeable — tell your employer as early as you can, and keep copies of everything.

Federal law — FMLA

The federal floor: 12 weeks of job-protected leave

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) gives eligible workers up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave a year — for a new child (birth, adoption, or foster placement), your own serious health condition, or to care for a spouse, child, or parent with a serious health condition — with your health coverage continued and your job (or an equivalent one) waiting when you return. You are eligible if your employer has 50 or more employees within 75 miles of your worksite (all public agencies and schools are covered regardless of size) and you have worked there at least 12 months and at least 1,250 hours in the last year. Leave can often be taken intermittently — a few hours or days at a time — and your employer can require a medical certification form, so return it by its deadline. If your employer denies leave you qualify for, punishes you for taking it, or refuses to restore your job, you can complain to the U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division at 1-866-487-9243.

Federal law — pregnancy

Pregnant workers: accommodation rights (PWFA)

Severance packages and releases: read before you sign

When a job ends, some employers offer a severance package: money (and sometimes continued benefits) paid after you leave. Almost always, the money comes with a paper to sign — a release giving up your right to sue the employer. That signature is the real transaction, and it is worth understanding before you trade. Here is the national picture, then exactly where Virginia stands.

Every state

Severance is almost never owed to you — it is a trade

No federal law requires an employer to pay severance. The U.S. Department of Labor states that severance pay is a matter of agreement between an employer and an employee (or the employee’s representative) — it comes from a company policy, a union contract, your employment contract, or negotiation, not from a general legal duty. The rare exceptions are narrow state rules for big plant closings and mass layoffs, covered in the Virginia card below. Because the money is voluntary, the employer is buying something with it: your signature on a release. That is also why the amount and terms can often be negotiated — before you sign, not after.

Every state

What the release takes — and what to ask for

A typical agreement asks you to give up claims for wrongful termination, discrimination, unpaid wages and overtime, and sometimes more — and may add confidentiality, a promise not to disparage the company, and limits on working for competitors. Before signing, read for: what claims you release, any non-compete or non-solicit clause, what happens to your unused vacation and final pay, the last-day and pay-through dates, and when the money is actually paid. Common things employees ask for in exchange: more weeks of pay, a later separation date, a neutral reference or agreed reference letter, help with health-coverage costs, and removal of a non-compete. A one-hour review by an employment lawyer is cheap insurance on a document that ends your claims.

Every state

Age 40 or older? Federal law gives you time

If you are 40 or older and the agreement asks you to waive age-discrimination claims, the federal Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (part of the ADEA) sets strict conditions: the waiver must be written in understandable language, must specifically mention the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, cannot waive claims that arise after you sign, must give you something beyond what you were already owed, and must advise you in writing to consult a lawyer. You must get at least 21 days to consider it — 45 days if it is part of a group layoff or exit-incentive program — and after signing you get 7 days to revoke. In a group program the employer must also give you written information about who was selected and who was not, by job title and age. An agreement that skips these steps may not hold up as a waiver of your age claims at all.

Every state

A big layoff or shutdown? The 60-day federal notice rule

The federal WARN Act requires employers with 100 or more employees to give at least 60 calendar days’ written notice before a plant closing or mass layoff affecting 50 or more workers at a single site. It is a notice law, not a severance law — but an employer that skips the notice can owe back pay and benefits for the violation period, up to 60 days, and some states add their own notice laws (and a couple add real severance). The Virginia card below tells you which rules apply where you work.

Lost the job? COBRA lets you keep the health plan — at a price

What COBRA is

COBRA is a federal law that lets you keep your employer's group health plan after the job ends or your hours are cut (termination for gross misconduct is the exception). It generally covers employers with 20 or more employees. You keep the same plan and the same doctors — what changes is who pays the premium.

The clock: 60 days to decide, and coverage backdates

You get at least 60 days from the date your election notice is provided to say yes. If you elect and pay, coverage is retroactive — it backdates to the day your group coverage ended, so there is no gap — and you get at least 45 days after electing to make the first payment. Let the election window close and the right is gone for good.

The real cost: up to 102% of the premium

While you worked there, your employer probably paid most of the premium. On COBRA, the plan can charge you the entire premium — both shares — plus up to 2% for administration: up to 102% of the plan's cost. The sticker shock is normal, and it is why the Marketplace comparison below matters.

How long it lasts

For job loss or reduced hours, federal COBRA lasts up to 18 months. For some family events — the employee's death, divorce, or becoming entitled to Medicare — a spouse or child can continue for up to 36 months. A disability determination can extend the 18 months by 11 more (at up to 150% of the premium).

Compare before you pay: the Marketplace

Losing job-based coverage also opens a 60-day Special Enrollment Period at HealthCare.gov. With income down, premium tax credits often make a Marketplace plan far cheaper than COBRA — price both before your COBRA window closes. One caution: dropping COBRA early, before it runs out, usually does not open a new Marketplace window. Questions about COBRA itself? The Labor Department's benefits advisors answer free at 1-866-444-3272.

What Virginia adds

Virginia Code section 38.2-3541 requires group hospital, medical and surgical, or major medical policies to provide continuation for 12 months when eligibility ends, where the policyholder is not required by federal law to provide COBRA continuation.

Called a contractor, treated like an employee? The label does not decide

The label does not decide — the work does

Whether you are an employee or an independent contractor turns on the real working relationship — who controls how, when, and where the work is done, how central the work is to the business, how you are paid, and how permanent the arrangement is. A 1099 form, a job title, or a signed "independent contractor agreement" does not settle it, and neither does the fact that you agreed to it.

What a wrong label costs you

Classified as a contractor, you usually get no unemployment benefits when the work ends, no workers' compensation if you are hurt on the job, no minimum-wage or overtime protection, and no employer share of payroll taxes — you owe self-employment tax on both halves of Social Security and Medicare. If the label is wrong, those protections were yours all along.

Get an official ruling: IRS Form SS-8

A worker or a firm can file Form SS-8 and ask the IRS to rule on the worker's status for federal tax purposes — it is free, though it can take months. If you were paid as a contractor but believe you were an employee, Form 8919 lets you report wages and pay only the employee share of Social Security and Medicare tax instead of full self-employment tax.

Unpaid wages or overtime? File a claim

If the label cost you minimum wage or overtime, you can file a wage claim with the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division (free help at 1-866-487-9243) or with your state labor agency. State unemployment agencies also audit classification because it decides who pays unemployment tax — a report there can trigger a review of your whole workplace.

How Virginia decides it

Virginia Tax says an individual performing services for an employer for remuneration is presumed an employee unless shown to be an independent contractor according to IRS guidelines, which fall into behavioral control, financial control, and type of relationship.

If your professional license is threatened

Nurses, teachers, contractors, real-estate agents, cosmetologists, counselors, and dozens of other workers hold a state-issued license — and once granted, that license is a legal right the licensing board cannot take without process. If you get a letter saying a complaint was filed or you are under investigation, treat it like a lawsuit with a deadline, because it is one: the letter states what you must do and by when, and ignoring it is the fastest route to losing by default. The usual sequence, in general terms, is complaint, investigation, a formal notice of the charges, a hearing before the board or an administrative law judge where you can present your side, a written decision, and then an appeal to court. One warning above all: do not surrender your license "voluntarily" to make an investigation go away without legal advice first. A surrender is usually recorded and reported like a revocation — it can follow you to other states through licensing compacts and background checks, and it can end careers in other fields that ask whether you have ever had a license disciplined. Respond in writing, on time, briefly and professionally; keep a copy of everything; and do not discuss the matter with investigators beyond what the process requires until you have talked to a lawyer who handles license defense — this is administrative law, and many bar lawyer-referral programs in this section can point you to one. Discipline can also reach your job (many employers must be told), other licenses you hold, and future applications, which is exactly why the cheap move is advice at the letter stage, not after the hearing.

  3. If you lost the job, file for unemployment the same week — then keep up the weekly certifications.
  4. Do not sign a severance agreement or a “resignation” letter under pressure. You can ask for time to have a lawyer or legal aid look at it first.
  5. If money is tight, ask legal aid about employment cases — many discrimination lawyers also offer a free first consultation.

Adoption, paternity & parentage in Virginia

Two different questions live in this section, and both come down to one thing: who counts, legally, as a child’s parent. Adoption creates a legal parent-child relationship where none existed before. Parentage (often called paternity when the father is the parent being established) proves that relationship when a child is born to unmarried parents. Parentage matters beyond the birth certificate: until parentage is legally established, a court generally cannot order custody, visitation, or child support. The Child custody and Child support sections on this page cover what happens after parentage exists; this section covers how you get there, and how adoption works from the other side.

Adoption types

The four paths to adoption

Agency adoption goes through a licensed public or private agency, which matches children with families and completes the required home study. Private (independent) adoption is arranged directly between birth parents and adoptive parents, usually with lawyers on both sides and a court-approved home study. Stepparent adoption is when a spouse adopts their partner’s child — often the simplest path, but it still requires the other birth parent’s rights to be terminated or surrendered, unless that parent is deceased or their rights were already terminated. Foster-care adoption is adopting a child already in state care, usually after parental rights have been terminated; children adopted from foster care who meet the state’s definition of special needs may qualify for federal or state adoption assistance to help with costs. Every path ends the same way: a court hearing that finalizes the adoption and issues a new birth certificate naming the adoptive parents.

Adoption steps

How adoption works, step by step

First, the birth parents’ legal rights must end — by voluntary surrender or consent, or by a court order terminating rights after a hearing. Second, the adoptive family completes a home study: interviews, background checks, a home visit, and references, done by a licensed agency or social worker. Third, the child is placed with the family for a supervision period set by state law. Fourth, the court holds a finalization hearing and signs the adoption decree. Two warnings: never pay for a child or promise payment in exchange for consent — baby-selling is illegal in every state, and legitimate adoption costs are fees for agencies, lawyers, and the home study, not payments to birth parents — and never skip the home study or the court hearing, because an adoption that is not finalized by a court is not an adoption.

Parentage

Establishing paternity and parentage: three ways

When parents are married, the spouse is usually presumed to be the legal parent automatically. When they are not, parentage must be established one of three ways. Voluntary acknowledgment: both parents sign a Voluntary Acknowledgment of Paternity (VAP) — hospitals are required to offer this form at birth, and it can also be signed later. Signing is a legal act with real consequences, including child support, so do not sign it casually or under pressure; most states give a short window to rescind it. Court order: either parent, or the child-support agency, can file a parentage case and ask a judge to decide. Genetic testing: the court can order DNA testing, and the child-support agency can usually arrange testing without a full court case. If the test excludes a man, he is not the legal father; if it matches, the court declares parentage.

Why it matters

Why parentage comes first: no parentage, no custody or support order

Parentage is the key that unlocks everything else. Without it, a father generally cannot get a court order for custody or visitation, and a mother generally cannot get a court order for child support from him — the court has no legal parent to order. Parentage also affects the child’s rights: inheritance, health-insurance coverage through a parent, Social Security benefits on a parent’s record, and access to family medical history. If you are unsure whether parentage is established for your child, check whether a VAP was signed or a court order exists; a name on the birth certificate alone does not always settle the legal question, and the rules vary by state.

When CPS investigates your family

The cards above are about reporting harm and about custody between parents. This part is for the other side of the knock on the door: what happens when child protective services — CPS — comes to investigate your family. An investigation is not a criminal charge and it is not a removal, and most end without a court case. Protecting children and knowing your own rights are both real — this block covers the process and your rights, in Virginia and everywhere.

Every state

What an investigation is — and what a caseworker can and cannot do

Someone reported a concern; the agency screened the report and sent a caseworker to look into it. The worker will want to see the child, see the home, and ask questions — of you, the child, and people who know your family. In general, a caseworker cannot come into your home without your permission, a court order, or a genuine emergency, and workers can often interview a child at school without asking first. What you say can be written down and used later, including in court. A safety plan — a written agreement about who cares for the child and what changes at home — is voluntary: it is not a court order. Read it before you sign, ask for time to think, and know you can ask a lawyer first; also know that refusing can lead the agency to ask a court to step in. If the agency believes a child is in immediate danger, it can involve police or a court to remove the child temporarily — and a judge must review that quickly.

In Virginia

How an investigation runs in Virginia

In Virginia, CPS sits inside your local department of social services, working under Virginia Department of Social Services (VDSS) supervision. A report to the state hotline (800-552-7096) or the local agency is screened; accepted reports are routed as either an investigation (for serious allegations) or a family assessment (a non-adversarial response track), and the worker must attempt face-to-face contact with the child on timelines that depend on the assigned response priority. Investigations end in a founded or unfounded disposition, issued at Level 1, 2, or 3 based on severity; family assessments do not produce a finding against anyone.

Your rights

Your rights during and after an investigation in Virginia

The local agency must notify you of the report and the finding, and VDSS publishes a parent brochure, 'A Guide to Child Protective Services,' explaining the process. If a disposition is founded, VDSS's official CPS appeals guidance says you may request an appeal of the finding; founded dispositions are kept in the state's central registry, with Level 1 findings retained the longest. If the case goes to court as an abuse/neglect or foster care matter, the court appoints counsel for indigent parents in those proceedings under Virginia law.

Grandparents and kin: visitation rights and raising a relative’s child

The cards above are about custody and visitation between parents. This part is for grandparents and other kin. Every state has a law that lets a grandparent ask a court for visitation — but the doorway is narrow, and it is different in every state. It is also a separate door from kinship care, where a relative steps in to raise the child. Both are covered here for Virginia.

Every state

Fit parents get the benefit of the doubt — in every state

In Troxel v. Granville (2000), the U.S. Supreme Court held that fit parents have a constitutional right to decide who spends time with their children, and that a court must give a fit parent’s decision special weight. That is why the laws below ask for more than a close bond: most states require a trigger — a parent’s death, a divorce, the child having lived with you — and proof that being cut off would harm the child, that a parent is unfit, or both.

Every state

Agreement and mediation come before court

A court fight is the last resort. Ask in writing, keep the answer, and try to agree on a schedule — many states let the court order mediation in grandparent cases, and in some the person asking has to pay for it. If you do reach an agreement, put it in writing; if a custody case between the parents is already open, ask whether your time can be written into that order.

In Virginia

Who can ask for visitation in Virginia — and when

Virginia does not limit petitions to grandparents: under Va. Code Secs. 20-124.1 and 20-124.2, a 'person with a legitimate interest' - broadly construed to include grandparents, step-grandparents, stepparents, former stepparents, blood relatives, and family members who have intervened in the suit or are otherwise properly before the court - may seek custody or visitation, and Sec. 16.1-278.15 likewise allows a petition by any party with a legitimate interest, including grandparents, stepparents, former stepparents, blood relatives, and family members. The definition excludes, among others, a person whose parental rights were terminated and, where the child has since been adopted, a person whose interest derives through someone whose rights were terminated; the statutes state no requirement that a parent has died, that the parents are divorced, or that the child lived with the petitioner.

In Virginia

What you must prove in Virginia

The court gives primary consideration to the best interests of the child, gives due regard to the primacy of the parent-child relationship, and may award custody or visitation to a person with a legitimate interest only on a showing by clear and convincing evidence that the best interest of the child would be served (Va. Code Sec. 20-124.2(B)). Section 20-124.2(B2) adds that when a natural or adoptive parent is deceased or incapacitated, that parent's parent may introduce evidence of the deceased or incapacitated parent's consent to grandparent visitation, and if consent is proved by a preponderance the court then decides best interest. No mediation-first rule appears in Sec. 20-124.2.

Kinship care

Raising a relative’s child in Virginia

Virginia DSS describes Virginia as a 'kin-first' state, meaning children in foster care are placed with relatives or close family friends whenever possible, and it publishes a Parental Child Safety Placement Program and voluntary placements of up to 90 days with a parent-chosen relative or close friend. For permanency, Va. Code Secs. 63.2-1305 and 63.2-1306 establish federal- and state-funded Kinship Guardianship Assistance programs that pay maintenance assistance to a relative guardian when adoption and reunification are not appropriate, including for a child who lived with the prospective kinship guardian as a foster placement for at least six consecutive months (federal program).

Financial scams in Virginia

Scammers are professional liars, and they are good at it — Americans lose billions of dollars to fraud every year, and most of it is never reported. Falling for one is not a character flaw; these crews run scripts tested on thousands of people. The good news: almost every financial scam shares the same handful of tells, and once you know them, the spell breaks.

The golden rule: anyone who pressures you to pay right now with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, a cash app, or cash is running a scam — full stop. No real agency, bank, utility, or business demands payment that way. The second someone insists on secrecy (“don’t tell your family or your bank”), hang up. Urgency plus secrecy plus a strange payment method is the whole game.

Know the signs

Government impostors

A call, text, or email claims to be the IRS, Social Security, Medicare, or the sheriff: you owe money, your benefits will stop, or there is a warrant for your arrest. Real agencies almost always contact you by mail first, never threaten arrest over the phone, and never ask for gift cards or wire transfers. Hang up and call the agency yourself at the number on its official website or your paperwork.

Know the signs

The family emergency — now with cloned voices

“Grandma, I’m in jail, I need bail money — please don’t tell Mom.” Scammers can now clone a loved one’s voice from a few seconds of online audio, so it can sound exactly like them. Defenses that work: agree on a family code word in advance, and always hang up and call your loved one back on the number you already have. Real trouble survives a callback; a scam can’t.

Know the signs

Romance and investment scams

An online sweetheart who can never meet — and eventually needs money — is a script, not a relationship. So is the friendly stranger pitching can’t-miss crypto or investment returns, sometimes showing you a fake account that looks like it is growing (“pig butchering”). Guaranteed high returns with no risk do not exist. If you have to pay fees or taxes before you can withdraw your own money, it was never real.

Know the signs

Fake checks, phishing, and tech support

A check that overpays you — for a car, a job, a prize — with a request to send part back is counterfeit; it bounces days later and you owe the bank. Texts and emails about a “locked account” or a “missed delivery” are phishing for your logins — never use their link; type the company’s address yourself. And a pop-up warning that your computer has a virus, with a number to call, is fake: real companies never do that.

How a court case actually moves: filing, service, answer, hearing

Step 1 — Filing starts the case, but filing alone changes nothing yet

The person suing (the plaintiff) files a complaint or petition at the courthouse and pays the filing fee, and the court issues a summons. But the case cannot move against the other side until they are formally told about it — that telling is called service of process, and it is the step everything else hangs on. If you are suing, the court or clerk can tell you the approved ways to serve in your court; budget for a sheriff's or process server's fee on top of the filing fee.

Step 2 — Service of process: the formal delivery that starts the clock

Service usually means hand-delivery of the summons and complaint to the defendant by a sheriff's deputy or a licensed process server — someone who is not a party to the case. Leaving papers with another adult at the home, or mailing them, counts only where the rules allow it (sometimes after personal delivery has been tried, sometimes when the defendant signs a waiver of service). The server files a proof or affidavit of service with the court stating when and how delivery happened. That date starts the defendant's deadline to respond, and service is what gives the court power over the defendant: a judgment entered without valid service can be attacked and undone, as the default-judgment block in the Credit Card & Debt section explains.

Step 3 — The answer: the defendant's response, on a deadline printed in the papers

Once served, the defendant has a set number of days to file a written answer responding to the claims — the exact deadline is stated in the summons or the court's rules, so read the papers you were handed, not a website. Miss it and the plaintiff can win by default without proving anything. The answer is also where defenses and counterclaims are raised. If you were served with papers that look wrong — wrong person, wrong address, papers left with someone else — do not ignore them: improper service can be challenged, but usually only if you raise it promptly, so get advice or call the clerk right away.

Step 4 — Hearing, then judgment

After the answer, the court sets the hearing or trial date (some courts first order mediation or a settlement conference). Each side presents documents and witnesses — the "What to bring to court" list on this page is your packing checklist — and the judge decides. The loser can owe the amount plus court costs, and a judgment does not collect itself: the collecting-a-judgment block in the Credit Card & Debt section covers what happens next, and the appeals block below covers challenging the result.

Do this now: if papers were handed to you, find the deadline in the summons and calendar it today — that date, not the hearing, is the first one that can sink you. If you are the one suing, ask the clerk at filing exactly how service works in that court and what it costs.

If you lost: appealing a civil judgment

An appeal is not a new trial — with one big exception

In a regular civil case, an appeal does not redo the trial. The higher court reads the record of what already happened and looks for legal errors — a wrong rule applied, evidence admitted that should not have been, a decision no reasonable judge could reach on that record. New evidence and new arguments you never made below are generally not allowed. The exception: appeals from small claims and the lowest courts are, in many states, a genuine fresh start — a trial de novo in the next court up, where the case is heard all over again as if the first one never happened. Your state's line below tells you which kind your appeal is, where the official source says.

The deadline is short, and missing it usually ends the matter

You start an appeal by filing a notice of appeal — and the window is measured in days, not months, in most states, counted from entry of the judgment or from notice of it. These deadlines are treated as jurisdictional: file late and the appeal is typically dismissed without anyone reaching the merits, however strong your argument is. The periods in the state lines are what the official rules publish; if your state has no line, or your case type is different, confirm your exact deadline with the clerk of the court that decided your case — immediately, not after you have written the brief.

Appeals from general district court to circuit court are heard de novo, tried from the beginning as though there had been no prior trial.

Winning below means the judgment can usually be enforced while you appeal — wages garnished, accounts levied — unless you get a stay. A stay often requires posting an appeal bond (supersedeas bond) for the judgment amount, which guarantees the winner gets paid if you lose again. Ask the clerk about a stay the same day you file the notice of appeal; every week of delay is a week the judgment is working. In some courts and case types a bond or a deposit is also required just for the appeal itself to proceed — another clerk question on day one.

Costs, fees, and honest odds

Appeals carry a filing fee and usually the cost of preparing the trial transcript, which can exceed the fee by a lot. If you cannot afford them, the court fee-waiver block in the Free and Low-Cost Legal Help section covers asking the court to waive appeal costs too. Be clear-eyed about the standard: the appeals court is not asking "who should have won?" but "did the judge make a legal mistake that mattered?" Disagreeing with how the judge weighed the witnesses is rarely enough. A one-hour consultation with an appellate lawyer before the deadline — many legal-aid and lawyer-referral programs in this section offer them — is the cheapest reality check in law.

Do this now: find your notice-of-appeal deadline — in your state's line below or from the clerk today — and calendar it with days to spare. The appeal can be thought through later; the deadline cannot.

If you receive a subpoena, do not ignore it

A subpoena is a court order — not an invitation. It commands you to appear and testify, to hand over documents or records, or both, in someone else's case or your own. Ignoring one can end in a contempt finding, fines, or a warrant; "I was never part of this lawsuit" is not a defense, because witnesses and record-holders are exactly who subpoenas are for. Start by reading it closely: who issued it (a court or an attorney acting for one), what it demands, and the date by which you must appear or produce — that deadline drives everything. If the demand is overbroad, reaches private records, was served improperly, or gives too little time, the routes are a written objection and a motion to quash or modify, filed with the issuing court before the deadline — in general terms these exist in every court system, and the clerk can point you to the local form. Do not just skip it and explain later. If the subpoena touches your own legal exposure — your records could incriminate you, or it is really aimed at you — talk to a lawyer before you produce anything; witnesses are usually entitled to a small attendance fee and mileage, which the subpoena or clerk can confirm.

Pardons and clemency: forgiveness when expungement is not an option

A pardon is forgiveness from the state — it is not the same as expungement

Expungement or sealing (covered above in this section) asks a court to hide or erase a record, and it only reaches convictions the statute covers. A pardon is different: the governor, or a pardons board, formally forgives the conviction. A pardon can restore rights a conviction took away — voting, serving on a jury, holding office, sometimes firearm rights — and it can matter for jobs, licenses, and immigration even when nothing can be erased. What it usually does NOT do is delete the record: in many states the conviction still shows, marked as pardoned. If both routes exist for your conviction, compare them — and many people pursue expungement first because it follows rules instead of discretion.

How the process works everywhere

You apply — you are not automatically considered. In most states the application goes to a board of pardons or parole board, which investigates, may hold a hearing, and recommends; the governor decides. In a few states the board itself grants pardons. Expect to document your whole sentence completed (including probation, fines, and restitution), your life since — work, family, community — and why the pardon should be granted. Waiting periods after the sentence are common. There is no deadline pressure the way a court filing has, but there is also no right to a pardon: it is an act of grace, and most applications are denied. A complete, honest application is the whole game.

Eviction records and rental screening: clean up your record

An eviction case can follow you for years — even one you won, and even one that was dismissed — because tenant-screening companies scrape court filings and sell them to landlords. Federal law gives you rights against bad screening reports, and a growing number of states let you seal, expunge, or limit the court record itself. Here is the federal picture, then exactly where Virginia stands.

Every state

A filing is not a judgment

When a landlord files an eviction case, that filing exists as a public court record from day one — before any judge hears anything, and even if the case is later dismissed, settled, or decided in your favor. Screening companies collect those filings in bulk, so a case that went nowhere can still show up as an “eviction” on a report and cost you an apartment. That is why cleaning up the report (below) and the court record itself (the Virginia card) are two separate jobs — most people need both.

Every state

Your federal rights when a landlord screens you

A tenant-screening report is a consumer report under the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA). If a landlord turns you down — or charges you more — based on one, the landlord must give you an adverse-action notice naming the screening company that made the report. You then have the right to a free copy of that report from the company if you ask within 60 days, and to dispute anything wrong or incomplete: the company generally must investigate within 30 days, fix what it cannot verify, and tell you the result. If a dispute does not resolve it, you may add a short written statement of dispute that must go out with future reports.

Every state

The 7-year rule — and what it does not do

Under the FCRA, screening companies must stop reporting most negative information after 7 years — civil judgments included. But the rule only limits what a report may contain. The underlying court record does not disappear, and a record a landlord finds by searching court files directly is not the screening company’s report. Closing the court file itself — sealing, expungement, or limited access — is a matter of state law, and states differ sharply, as the Virginia card below shows.

Every state

The cleanup, step by step

(1) Get the report. Use the company named in your adverse-action notice first (free within 60 days); before you apply anywhere else, also order your file from the big tenant-screening companies — you have FCRA rights against each of them. (2) Dispute every error in writing, with a copy of the court’s final disposition (dismissal, judgment in your favor, satisfaction) attached, and keep proof of mailing. (3) Send court orders. If your case was sealed or expunged, send the order to every screening company listing the case — a sealed record must not be reported. (4) Explain what remains. For anything accurate that is still reportable, attach a short written explanation and bring your disposition paperwork when you apply, so a landlord sees the outcome, not just the filing.

In Virginia

Eviction records in Virginia: sealing and access

Virginia automatically expunges many eviction (unlawful detainer) court records, with no petition or hearing, if the action was dismissed and 30 days have passed, or a voluntary nonsuit was taken and six months have passed, and no order of possession was entered in the case. If judgment was entered in favor of the defendant (the tenant), the record is not automatic, but the defendant may file a petition and the court must expunge it without a hearing. For cases begun before July 1, 2024, the tenant uses the Supreme Court petition form in the general district court where the case was filed.

During a lease, the rent is locked

What counts as retaliation

In most states a landlord may not punish you for exercising a legal right — reporting health or code violations to an inspector, asking for repairs the law requires, complaining to a government agency, joining a tenant association, or withholding rent through the legal process where that is allowed. Traffic Tickets & Court Fines in Virginia

A traffic ticket is not just a bill. On most tickets you have three choices — pay it, fight it, or ask about a middle option like traffic school or a deferral — and the choice closes doors: paying is usually a guilty plea that puts a conviction on your driving record. This section lays out the fork, then Virginia’s rules on points, courses, and what happens if a ticket is ignored.

Start here

Your three choices on almost every ticket

Pay it — in nearly every state, paying the fine is a guilty plea. The conviction goes on your driving record, points are added where the state uses them, and your insurance can go up. Fight it — plead not guilty by the date on the ticket and the court or tribunal named on it hears your case. Ask about the middle options first — traffic school, a deferral, or a reduced charge can keep a ticket off your record in many places, but they are usually gone once you pay. The one choice that always backfires is ignoring the ticket: expect a default conviction, added fees, and in most states a suspended license or a warrant.

In Virginia

Points and your license in Virginia

Virginia DMV runs two balances: demerit points for violations and safe driving points you earn, one for each full calendar year without a violation, up to 5. Speeding 1-9 mph over is 3 demerit points, 10-19 over is 4, and 20 or more over (or over 85 mph) is reckless driving at 6; running a stop sign or red light is 4, and improper passing is 4. Reaching 12 demerit points in 12 months (or 18 in 24) triggers a 90-day probation period, and reaching 18 in 12 months (or 24 in 24) can mean a 90-day license suspension.

Traffic school

Courses that can help in Virginia

Completing a DMV-approved driver improvement clinic can add 5 safe driving points to offset demerits, once every 2 years, as long as you take it voluntarily and not because DMV or a court ordered it. A court can also send you to a clinic as part of your case, but an ordered clinic earns no points.

Your options

Fighting, deferring, or paying over time in Virginia

Traffic cases are heard in the general district court for the city or county on your summons; you can prepay many minor tickets (prepaying is a guilty plea) or appear and plead not guilty to contest. Deferred dispositions exist for some charges at the judge's discretion, but Virginia has no general statutory ticket-deferral program like some states. If you fail to appear or fail to pay fines and costs on time, the court can suspend your driving privilege in Virginia until you comply.

Motorcycle lane splitting in Virginia

Lane splitting is a motorcycle riding between lanes of traffic. Lane filtering is the narrower version — moving between stopped or very slow cars, usually to the front at a light. Only six states allow either one, and every one of them puts strict speed limits on it. Two motorcycles riding side by side in one lane is different — that is lane sharing, and it is legal almost everywhere.

Not allowed

The rule in Virginia

Lane splitting and lane filtering are not allowed in Virginia. Virginia treats lane splitting as improper passing — the DMV motorcycle handbook says it is against the law, and it can be cited as reckless driving (Va. Code § 46.2-857).

If you drive a car

Know the numbers — they protect you

Hit-and-run and uninsured drivers: who pays when they run, or carry nothing

Slip and fall: hurt on someone else's property

What the owner owed you depends on why you were there

Premises liability is the other half of injury law: instead of a driver, the careless party is the store, landlord, or homeowner whose property hurt you. In general terms, owners owe the most care to people invited in — customers, guests, delivery drivers: they must fix or warn about dangers they know of, or should have found by looking. People allowed on the property for their own purposes get warnings about known hidden dangers. Even trespassers are owed a basic duty not to be harmed by traps, and children get extra protection around things like unfenced pools. The fight in almost every case is the same: was the danger there long enough that the owner should have known, and were you paying reasonable attention?

Document the condition before it disappears

A spill gets mopped, ice melts, a broken step gets fixed — your evidence has a shelf life of hours. Photograph the exact spot and what caused the fall, from several angles, before you leave if you can. Report it to the manager or owner and get a copy of the incident report (or write down who took it). Keep the shoes and clothes you wore, unwashed. Get names of anyone who saw it. Then get medical care the same day: the records connect the injury to the fall, and "I felt fine at first" is the insurer's favorite sentence.

The fault rule and the filing deadline in Virginia

The fault rule that can shrink — or erase — your claim

Most states use comparative fault: your recovery is reduced by your share of blame, and in many states you recover nothing once your share passes half. A few states still use the old contributory-negligence rule, where being even slightly at fault can bar recovery entirely — Maryland's, North Carolina's, and Virginia's official sources below confirm their courts still apply it, which is why your state's line matters so much. Either way, expect the property's insurer to argue you weren't watching where you were going; your photos and the incident report are your answer.

Falls in a public building, on a public sidewalk, or in a park usually require a formal written notice of claim to the government body within a short window — often a few months — before any lawsuit is possible, on top of the deadline in your state's line. If the property was public, treat the notice as the first task, not the last.

Juvenile law & school-to-court issues in Virginia

Juvenile court is not adult court with smaller defendants. It was built around rehabilitation rather than punishment, it uses different words, different hearings, and usually no jury, and a young person’s record is handled very differently from an adult’s. That difference is good news, but it is not automatic: records are not always sealed on their own, school discipline can still lead to court, and some serious cases can be moved to adult court. This section covers the map; the Child safety and Special education sections on this page cover abuse reporting and school disability rights, and this section does not repeat them.

How it differs

How juvenile court differs from adult court

In juvenile court, a young person is usually “taken into custody” rather than arrested, charged by petition rather than indictment, and “adjudicated delinquent” rather than convicted — the judge, not a jury, usually decides the case, in a hearing that is often closed to the public. The court’s dispositions lean toward supervision, counseling, community service, restitution, and placement in a juvenile facility rather than adult prison, and parents or guardians are usually required to attend and can be ordered to participate. Young people still have core rights — to notice of the charges, to a lawyer, to confront witnesses, and against self-incrimination — so if your child is questioned by police or school resource officers, the safest first move is to ask for a lawyer before answering questions, and to say so calmly and clearly. Age limits, the right to a jury, and the rules for detention all vary by state, so confirm the Virginia rules with the juvenile court or a juvenile-defense lawyer rather than assuming another state’s rules apply.

Common matters

Common juvenile matters: delinquency, truancy, and status offenses

Delinquency means an act that would be a crime if an adult did it — theft, assault, drug offenses, vandalism. Status offenses are acts that are only offenses because of age: running away, truancy (unexcused school absence), curfew violations, and underage drinking in some states. Truancy and other status offenses usually start with school and family interventions, not jail, but repeated truancy can reach court, where the judge can order counseling, supervision, or services for the family — so answer school letters early, keep records of illness and appointments, and ask the school in writing what support it has offered before the case reaches a judge. Emancipation is a separate court process by which some minors can be declared legally independent adults before 18; it generally requires showing the young person can support themselves and manage their own affairs, it is not available in every state, and it ends parental support obligations in ways that should never be rushed into without legal advice.

School to court

When school discipline leads to court involvement

School discipline and court are connected in both directions: a suspension or expulsion hearing is a school process with its own notice and hearing rights (see the Special education section on this page for students with disabilities), while fights, threats, drug possession, or property damage at school can also be referred to police and juvenile court. If your child faces both, handle both tracks: respond to the school in writing by its deadline, keep every notice, and do not let the school process substitute for legal advice about the court case — statements made to a principal can be used later. Ask the school what alternatives it considered (restorative practices, counseling, behavior plans) and whether the referral to police was required or discretionary; that record matters to a juvenile judge deciding whether the case belongs in court at all. If your child has a disability, tell the school and the court in writing: federal disability law can change what discipline is lawful.

Records

Juvenile records: sealing and expungement are not automatic

A juvenile record is not one file: police reports, court files, probation records, and school records may each be held separately, and sealing one does not always seal the others. Sealing generally hides the record from the public and most employers, while law enforcement and courts may still see it; expungement generally erases or destroys it, where the state offers it. In many states neither happens automatically at 18 — someone must apply to the juvenile court after the case closes and any waiting period passes, and serious offenses or later adult convictions can block eligibility. The practical steps: ask the juvenile court clerk in the county where the case was heard for the sealing/expungement forms and the waiting period, confirm which agencies hold records, and get the order in writing; then check that employers’ background checks actually come back clean. The Clearing your criminal record section on this page covers adult records; juvenile sealing uses a different process and different forms, so do not file adult forms for a juvenile case.

Transferred cases

When a juvenile case can move to adult court

For the most serious offenses, and for older teens, Virginia law may allow a prosecutor to ask a judge to transfer (waive) the case to adult court, or may require it for certain charges. Transfer is the highest-stakes decision in juvenile law: an adult conviction carries adult penalties and an adult record that is far harder to clear. If transfer is raised, get a juvenile-defense lawyer immediately, attend every hearing, and gather school records, counseling records, and evidence of rehabilitation — judges deciding transfer weigh the seriousness of the offense, the young person’s history, and whether juvenile services can still help. Do not discuss the facts of the case with police, school staff, or on social media while transfer is possible.

Special education & student rights in Virginia

If your child has a disability that affects learning, two federal laws protect them at school. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) gives eligible children an Individualized Education Program (IEP) — specially designed instruction, written down, with goals the school must work toward. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act covers children who have a disability that substantially limits a major life activity but do not need specially designed instruction — they get a 504 plan of accommodations instead, like extra time, preferential seating, or a quiet room for tests. You do not have to know which one fits before you ask: ask for an evaluation in writing, and the school must consider it.

IEP

How an IEP works — and the federal clocks

Ask for an evaluation in writing, addressed to the principal or the district’s special education director, and keep a copy with the date. The school must get your written consent before it evaluates. Under the federal default, the evaluation is completed within 60 days of your consent — your state may set its own timeline, so ask what Virginia’s is — and once your child is found eligible, the school must hold the IEP meeting within 30 days. The IEP is reviewed at least once a year, and your child must be reevaluated at least every 3 years. You are a member of the IEP team: you can bring someone with you, you can disagree, and you can ask for another meeting at any time.

504 plan

If an IEP is not the fit: a Section 504 plan

A 504 plan does not come with specially designed instruction, but it does require the school to remove barriers: accommodations in the regular classroom, changes to how work or tests are given, and access to school activities. Ask the school who its Section 504 coordinator is and put your request in writing, with a note from your child’s doctor if you have one. If the school refuses an evaluation or a plan, ask for that refusal in writing — a written refusal (called prior written notice) must explain why, and it starts your right to challenge it.

Discipline

School discipline: extra protections apply

A student with an IEP or 504 plan cannot simply be suspended or expelled like any other student. Section 8 & Public Housing in Virginia

If rent eats most of your income, two federal programs can help: Housing Choice Vouchers — still widely called Section 8 — and public housing. Both are run by a local agency called a public housing agency (PHA), not by a national office, and both have waiting lists. This section explains how the programs work, how Virginia runs them, and the exact steps to get on a list — and keep your place on it.

Federal program — all 50 states

How Section 8 and public housing work

With a voucher, you find a home from a private landlord; you generally pay about 30% of your adjusted income toward rent, and the housing agency pays the rest, up to a local limit it sets. Public housing is different: the home itself is owned by the housing agency, and rent is also based on your income. Eligibility for both turns on income — generally at or below 50% of the median income for your area, with most new vouchers reserved for households at or below 30%. A voucher can also move with you if you relocate to another housing agency’s area — ask about portability before you move. And if an agency denies you assistance, federal rules give you the right to an informal hearing to challenge the decision. General questions? HUD’s housing agency information line is (800) 955-2232.

In Virginia

Finding your housing agency in Virginia

Virginia Housing runs its Housing Choice Voucher program through 32 local housing agencies, and each agency keeps a separate waiting list for its own area. You must apply online — Virginia Housing accepts no mailed, faxed, or emailed applications — and only when a waiting list is open; openings are published in the local newspaper two weeks before the list opens. For public housing and for voucher areas Virginia Housing does not cover, apply directly to the local public housing agency or redevelopment and housing authority for that city or county.

All of Virginia Housing's local agencies give a residency preference, so people who live or work in that agency's area are placed ahead of applicants from outside the area. You may apply to more than one agency's waiting list, and Virginia Housing says applying is free — if you are asked to pay a fee, you are on a fraudulent website.

Neighbor & HOA disputes in Virginia

Most neighbor fights are not about the law — they are about a fence, a tree, a dog, or a dues bill, and about two people who stopped talking. The law still matters, because it decides who pays and who has to stop, but the cheapest wins in this section come before any courtroom: a survey, a dated letter, and a paper trail. Work the ladder in order — talk, letter, mediation, small claims — and do not skip steps, because a judge will ask what you tried first. The Deed fraud section on this page covers title and ownership scams; this section covers the everyday disputes between people who live next to each other.

Property lines

Property lines and surveys: never argue from a fence line

Fences, hedges, and driveways are often not on the real boundary — they were put up by guesswork years ago. Before you argue, or build, get the facts: your deed and plat map from the county recorder’s office, and, if the line is genuinely disputed, a licensed land surveyor’s survey. A survey costs money, but it ends arguments that a fence never will, and courts treat a surveyor’s markers as strong evidence. Do not move, remove, or destroy survey markers — tampering with them is illegal in many states. If the survey shows your neighbor’s fence, shed, or driveway is on your land, talk first and put any agreement in writing; if they refuse to move it, that written record and the survey are what a court or mediator will want to see.

Fences

Fences: who pays for a boundary fence depends on your state

Some states require neighbors to share the cost of a boundary fence equally; others put the cost on the person who wants it, or have special fence laws that apply mainly to farmland and livestock; and many cities add their own height, setback, and permit rules on top. There is no single national rule, so do not quote one to your neighbor — check Virginia law and your city or county fence ordinance before you demand half the cost or refuse to pay it. Practical rules that hold almost everywhere: get any cost-sharing agreement in writing before work starts, keep the contractor’s invoice and your payment proof, and do not build on a disputed line without a survey. If a fence is dangerous — leaning, broken, electrified without warning — report it to code enforcement as well as writing to the owner.

Trees

Trees: overhanging branches, fallen trees, and who pays

The general principles courts commonly apply are these, with state-by-state variations: you may usually trim branches and roots that cross onto your property, up to the property line, but you may not trespass onto your neighbor’s land to do it, and you must not cut so much that you kill or destabilize the tree — killing a neighbor’s tree can make you liable for its full value. If a healthy tree falls in a storm, the damage usually falls where it lands, and each owner’s insurance handles their own property; but if the tree was dead, diseased, or visibly hazardous and the owner knew or should have known, the owner may be liable for the damage. The move that protects you in both cases is a dated written notice: if a neighbor’s tree looks dead or dangerous, write to them (keep a copy) and to your city’s tree or code office before it falls, so “nobody told me” is no longer available.

Noise & nuisance

Noise and nuisance: document it, then report it

Noise limits, quiet hours, barking-dog rules, and junk or odor complaints are almost always set by city or county ordinance, not state law — so the first step is finding your local rule, usually through your city or county code-enforcement office or animal control. A complaint without a record goes nowhere: keep a dated log (date, time, duration, what happened), save any recordings or photos, and keep copies of every message you sent the neighbor. Report in writing, not just by phone, and keep the complaint number. If the problem is a rental, write to the landlord or property manager too — landlords can often fix a tenant problem faster than a city can. Repeated, documented violations are what get fines issued and what win in mediation or court; a single angry call is not.

HOA

HOA fines: your rights are in the documents — read them before you pay or fight

An HOA or condo association can fine you only for rules it actually has the power to enforce, and that power comes from three places: the declaration and bylaws (the CC&Rs), the association’s rules, and Virginia’s HOA or condo statute, which you can find through the state legislature’s website. In many states you have a right to a hearing before a fine becomes final, and a right to see the association’s records — budgets, meeting minutes, and the violation file on your home. If you get a fine: ask in writing for the exact rule you allegedly broke, the evidence, and the hearing procedure; attend the hearing and bring your evidence; and do not stop paying regular dues to protest a fine unless a lawyer tells you to — unpaid dues can lead to late fees and, in many states, a lien on your home, which is a far bigger problem than the fine. If the hearing fails, mediation or small claims court (below) is usually the next step, not ignoring the letters.

Escalation

The escalation ladder: talk, letter, mediation, small claims

Work it in order. Talk: calmly, once, with a specific ask and a deadline (“Can we split the fence repair by the end of the month?”). Letter: if talking fails, send a dated letter — by email plus certified mail for anything expensive — stating the facts, the rule or survey, what you want, and a deadline; keep a copy. Mediation: many courts and communities offer low-cost mediation, where a neutral mediator helps you reach an agreement you both sign; it is faster and cheaper than court and it preserves the relationship you still have to live next to. Small claims: if the other side will not talk or mediate, file in small claims court for the money you can prove. Going straight to court without the earlier steps does not help your case — judges notice who tried to settle.

Your assessment is not final

If the government wants your land: eminent domain and just compensation

Eminent domain is the government's power to take private property for a public use — a road, a school, a utility line. The Fifth Amendment and every state constitution attach two strings: the use must be public, and the government must pay just compensation, which means the fair market value of what is taken, and in many states something for damage to the part you keep when only a strip is taken. You cannot simply refuse a lawful taking, but you almost never have to accept the first number: the government's offer rests on its appraisal, and owners who get their own appraisal reviewed regularly find value the first one missed. In general terms, you can also challenge whether the taking is truly for a public use in some situations, and challenges to the amount are common and expected — the process typically runs notice, written offer, negotiation, and only then a court condemnation case if you cannot agree. Two traps: signing the initial offer or a "voluntary" agreement without advice waives the chance to contest the price, and the notices carry deadlines that matter as much as in any lawsuit. If the government (or a contractor) occupies or damages your property without ever filing — a flooded field after a drainage change, a road project that takes your access — that is called inverse condemnation, and the claim is yours to bring. Condemnation lawyers commonly review a case free and are often paid from the amount they win above the offer, so get that review before you sign anything.

Medicare: signing up on time, and where to get free help

The four parts, in plain words

Part A is hospital insurance — inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing care, hospice, and some home health care; most people pay no premium for it. Part B is medical insurance — doctors, outpatient care, medical equipment, and many preventive services — and it has a monthly premium. Part C is Medicare Advantage: a private plan Medicare approves that bundles Parts A and B, and usually drug coverage too. Part D is prescription drug coverage, sold by private companies Medicare approves. Original Medicare means Parts A and B together.

Your first sign-up window — do not miss it

Your first chance to sign up is a 7-month window: the 3 months before the month you turn 65, that month, and the 3 months after. If you are already getting Social Security benefits at least 4 months before you turn 65, Parts A and B start automatically and your card comes in the mail. Everyone else signs up through Social Security — online at SSA.gov or at a local Social Security office. Miss the window and you may have to wait for the January 1 to March 31 sign-up period, and pay penalties on top.

Late sign-up penalties last

Sign up late and the penalty sticks. For Part B, your premium goes up an extra 10% for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but did not — and you usually pay that higher premium for as long as you have Part B. For Part D, going without drug coverage that counts as creditable coverage adds 1% for each month you were late, also for as long as you have drug coverage. Most people pay nothing for Part A.

Still working at 65? The 8-month rule

If you (or your spouse) are still working at 65 with health coverage from that job, you can usually sign up later without a penalty — but the clock is strict: your special sign-up period ends 8 months after the job coverage or the job ends, whichever comes first. Two traps catch people: COBRA is NOT counted as job coverage and does not extend that 8-month window, and retiree coverage ending does not start a new one. And if the employer has fewer than 20 workers, Medicare is expected to pay first — ask your state's free counselors below before you delay Part B.

Original Medicare + Medigap, or a Medicare Advantage plan?

Path one: Original Medicare plus a Medigap policy. Medigap is extra private insurance that helps pay your share of Original Medicare costs, and you can see any doctor who takes Medicare. Your best time to buy is the 6-month window that starts the month you have Part B and are 65 or older — during it, insurers cannot turn you down for health problems; later, they usually can. Path two: a Medicare Advantage plan — one private plan instead of Original Medicare, usually with a doctor network, approvals for some care, a yearly cap on out-of-pocket costs, and extras like dental, vision, or hearing. You cannot use a Medigap policy with an Advantage plan. Neither path is right for everyone — that is what the free counselors below are for.

Higher income costs more; lower income can get help

Higher income? If your tax return from 2 years ago shows income above a set amount, you pay an extra amount (called IRMAA) on top of your Part B and Part D premiums. Lower income? Medicare Savings Programs, run through your state Medicaid office, can help pay Part A and B costs like premiums — and getting one also gives you Extra Help with drug costs automatically. Extra Help also pays your Part D late penalty away while you have it.

If you’re sued over a debt: answer the lawsuit

A summons and complaint means a creditor or debt buyer has started a court case against you. This is the one envelope you must not ignore: if you do nothing, the court can enter a default judgment against you for the full amount claimed — and a judgment is what leads to the wage garnishment and bank levies described in the garnishment block above. Answering is usually a short form, it costs little or nothing in many courts, and it forces the other side to prove its case.

Your deadline

How long you have to answer in Virginia

In circuit court, Virginia Supreme Court Rule 3:8 requires you to file your response within 21 days after service of the summons and complaint. If you timely waived service when asked, the deadline is longer — 60 days after the waiver request was sent, or 90 days if you were addressed outside Virginia.

Filing your answer

How to file an answer in Virginia

File your answer with the circuit court named in your papers within that 21-day period. Rule 3:8 says the answer must respond to the paragraphs of the complaint — a general denial of the whole complaint is not allowed in Virginia circuit court.

Defenses

Defenses and things worth checking

Rule 3:8 lists the other responses you can file instead of or alongside an answer — a demurrer, a plea, a motion to dismiss, a motion for a bill of particulars, a motion craving oyer, or a written motion raising a preliminary defense — but each counts only for the specific count it addresses. If the court overrules your motions or pleas, you then have 21 days from that order to file your answer.

A default judgment against you? How to ask the court to undo it

A default judgment means you lost a lawsuit without a hearing — because the court treated you as never having answered it. Many people first learn about one when wages are garnished or a bank account is frozen. In every state there is a way to ask the court that entered the judgment to undo it — called setting aside, vacating, or opening the judgment, depending on the state. The “Sued Over a Debt” block above is about answering a lawsuit in time; this block is for when the judgment is already entered.

Every state

You file in the same court that entered it

The motion goes to the court that entered the judgment — it is not an appeal and not a new lawsuit somewhere else. If the court grants it, the judgment is undone and the case reopens: the claim against you does not disappear, but you get the chance to defend it that you missed the first time.

Every state

Bring your defense with you

Courts rarely reopen a case just because you ask. In most states the motion must do two things: explain why you did not answer in time (you were never served, the papers went to an old address, a genuine mistake or emergency), and state a real defense to the claim itself — you already paid it, the debt is not yours, the amount is wrong, the claim is too old. Attach the answer you want to file and any proof you have; several states require it, and everywhere it makes the motion stronger.

Every state

The clock is the whole ballgame

Every state sets a deadline, and they differ enormously — as short as 10 to 30 days in some situations, up to a year for the common grounds, and sometimes longer for a judgment that is void or was entered without serving you at all. Most rules also say “within a reasonable time,” judged by how fast you moved once you learned of the judgment. The Virginia card below gives your rule and your deadline. Your deadline: The statute states an express deadline for only one ground: a motion based on fraud on the court must be made within two years from the date of the judgment. Payday loans, title loans, and the safer ways out

A payday loan is a small loan due in full on your next payday — usually a few hundred dollars, at a fee that looks small and isn’t. A title loan borrows against your car. The rules are completely different from state to state: some states cap the cost at 36% APR or ban these loans outright, and others let lenders charge what they want. Here is the federal picture, then exactly where Virginia stands.

Every state

What a payday loan really costs

The standard pitch is about $15 for every $100 borrowed, due in about two weeks. Stated the way other loans are priced, that is roughly 391% APR. The real damage comes from repeat borrowing: if you cannot pay the whole amount plus the fee on payday, a new loan (or a rollover, where allowed) starts the fee clock again, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has found most payday loans go to borrowers who take out loan after loan. Price any payday or title loan by its APR and its total payback amount — never by the fee alone.

Every state

Your bank account: the federal two-strike rule

Since March 30, 2025, a federal CFPB rule has limited how payday, title, and similar high-cost lenders can pull payments from your bank account: after two consecutive failed withdrawal attempts, the lender must get your new written authorization before trying again, and it must send you payment notices. Know the limits of that rule, too: the CFPB announced in March 2025 that it is not prioritizing enforcement of these payment provisions, and the rule never capped prices — a planned federal requirement that lenders check you can afford the loan was repealed in 2020. The Virginia law below is your main protection.

Every state

In the military? Federal law caps the cost at 36%

The federal Military Lending Act caps active-duty servicemembers and their dependents at a 36% military annual percentage rate on payday loans, title loans, and most other consumer credit — and a loan made over that cap to a covered borrower is void. Lenders must check military status before lending. If a lender charged you more, complain to the CFPB and your installation legal assistance office.

Every state

Title loans: your car is the collateral

A title loan is not like a payday loan with a different name — you hand the lender a claim on your car’s title, and if you miss payments the lender can repossess and sell the car, often without going to court first. Losing the car usually means losing the ride to work, which makes the debt harder, not easier. If a lender has taken or threatens to take your vehicle, read the Car Repossession section of this page for your rights and the steps to get the car back.

Every state

Safer exits to try first

(1) A credit union Payday Alternative Loan (PAL). Many federal credit unions offer PALs of $200 to $2,000 with APR capped at 28%, an application fee of no more than $20, and no rollovers — ask any federal credit union if it offers them. (2) Negotiate the original bill. The utility, landlord, or medical office you actually owe will often take a payment plan for far less than a payday loan costs — ask before you borrow. (3) Ask about a payment plan on the loan itself — in some states the law gives you that right (see the Virginia cards below). (4) Be wary of “debt settlement” pitches that charge fees to negotiate your debts down; nonprofit credit counselors (look for NFCC members) review your budget for free or nearly free.

In Virginia

Payday loans in Virginia: the law

Virginia replaced traditional payday loans on January 1, 2021. Under the Fairness in Lending Act reforms, Chapter 18 of Title 6.2 of the Code of Virginia is now the 'Short-Term Loans' chapter, administered by the State Corporation Commission's Bureau of Financial Institutions. A licensed short-term loan is an installment loan, payable in substantially equal installments of principal, fees, and interest combined. Interest may not exceed 36% a year; the only other routine charge is a monthly maintenance fee of no more than the lesser of 8% of the loan amount or $25; and total fees and charges are capped at 50% of the amount borrowed on loans of $1,500 or less (60% above that). The lender must make a reasonable attempt to verify your income, every loan is checked against a statewide database, and you may cancel the loan by 5 p.m. on the third business day. A loan made by an unlicensed lender is void - nothing on it is collectible (Va. Code sections 6.2-1801, 6.2-1817, 6.2-1818.3).

Student loans: repayment, default, and the way out

Student loans split into two worlds. Federal loans come with repayment plans based on your income, official ways out of default, and free help from the U.S. Department of Education. Private loans are just a contract with a lender — the federal options do not apply. The federal rules changed on July 1, 2026 under the 2025 law, and this block follows what the Department of Education itself publishes now. This area is still moving, so confirm your own status in your StudentAid.gov account before you act.

Every state

First: is your loan federal or private?

Everything below about repayment plans, default, and getting out of default applies to federal loans. The Department does not cover private student loans on StudentAid.gov: for a private loan, your rights are the contract you signed, plus Virginia law (see the Virginia card below if there is one) — private lenders generally do not have to offer income-based payments, deferment, or forgiveness. Not sure which kind you have? Log in at StudentAid.gov: every federal loan you owe is listed there. A loan that is not listed is private — contact that lender directly about your options.

Every state

Can’t afford the payment? Income-driven plans

Ask about an income-driven plan before you miss payments. The Department’s current plans are Income-Based Repayment (IBR) and the new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP), which started July 1, 2026. The older PAYE and ICR plans are being phased out under the 2025 law; borrowers in them with loans from before July 1, 2026 have until July 1, 2028 to choose RAP, the new Tiered Standard plan, or IBR. Under RAP, payments run between 1% and 10% of your income depending on what you earn, reduced by $50 a month for each dependent; on-time payments get leftover monthly interest waived plus a matching principal payment of up to $50, and a remaining balance can be discharged after 360 on-time payments. Loans first taken out on or after July 1, 2026 use RAP or the Tiered Standard plan (fixed terms of 10, 15, 20, or 25 years depending on the amount borrowed) — and if you never choose, the servicer will place a new loan on Tiered Standard. Applying takes about 10 minutes in your StudentAid.gov account. The SAVE plan is not among the plans the Department currently lists; if you were in SAVE, check your account and the Department’s announcements for your loans’ current status.

Every state

What default means — and why to avoid it

A federal Direct or FFEL loan goes into default after 270 days without a scheduled payment. The consequences the Department lists: the entire unpaid balance becomes due at once; tax refunds and federal benefit payments can be taken through Treasury offset; wages can be garnished; you lose deferment, forbearance, and your choice of repayment plan; you lose eligibility for more federal student aid; and the default is reported to the credit bureaus. Even before default, a delinquency of 90 days or more is reported. One warning: “Fresh Start,” the temporary program you may have heard about, ended on October 2, 2024 — anyone offering to enroll you in it now is offering a program that no longer exists.

Every state

In default? The two ways out

The Department names two main exits (paying in full is a third, impractical for most people). (1) Rehabilitation — usually the better deal. You agree in writing to make nine voluntary, reasonable, and affordable payments — they can be as low as $5 — during a period of 10 consecutive months. Finish, and the default status is removed, garnishment and offset stop, and the record of default is removed from your credit history (late payments from before the default stay). Rehabilitation is a one-time opportunity per loan today; starting July 1, 2027, you will be able to rehabilitate the same loan twice. (2) Consolidation — the faster exit. Roll the defaulted loan into a new Direct Consolidation Loan after three consecutive, voluntary, on-time, full payments, or by applying with an income-driven plan. The catches: unpaid interest is added to your principal, and the record of default stays on your credit history. Compare both at StudentAid.gov/default-support, or call the Default Resolution Group at 1-800-621-3115.

Every state

Wage garnishment: no court order needed — and a current pause

On a defaulted federal loan, the Department can order your employer to withhold up to 15% of your disposable pay — this is called administrative wage garnishment, and it does not require taking you to court. You have rights first: request a hearing in writing, postmarked within 30 days of the garnishment notice, and the garnishment pauses until after the hearing; Treasury offset likewise comes with a written notice and a 65-day window to dispute it. Current status: in January 2026 the Department announced it was delaying involuntary collections — wage garnishment and Treasury offset — while the new repayment plans roll out, and no restart date has been announced on the Department’s pages; defaults are still reported to the credit bureaus during the delay. Check StudentAid.gov for the current status, and do not count on the pause lasting.

Every state

Work for government or a nonprofit? Public Service Loan Forgiveness

PSLF cancels the remaining balance on your Direct Loans after 120 qualifying monthly payments made under a qualifying repayment plan while you work full-time for a government or not-for-profit employer. An income-driven plan (or the 10-year standard plan) is the usual route. Use the PSLF Help Tool on StudentAid.gov to check your employer and submit the form — do not rely on your servicer’s say-so alone.

Every state

Servicer problems — and the fee scam

Your servicer is the company that bills you. Find yours by logging in at StudentAid.gov, or call the Federal Student Aid Information Center at 1-800-433-3243. If the servicer will not fix a problem, submit it through the FSA Feedback Center on StudentAid.gov — and if the answer looks wrong or incomplete, you can add information to your case and ask for escalation to the Office of the Ombudsman. Finally, the Department’s own warning: help with your federal loans is free. A company charging “enrollment,” “subscription,” or “maintenance” fees to get you out of default is running a scam — walk away.

In Virginia

Extra borrower protections in Virginia

Virginia's Qualified Education Loan Servicers law, Code of Virginia Title 6.2, Chapter 26 (sections 6.2-2600 et seq.), requires a person to get a State Corporation Commission license before acting as a qualified education loan servicer (section 6.2-2601) and bans fraud, unfair or deceptive practices, misapplying payments, and providing inaccurate credit-bureau information (section 6.2-2610). A borrower who suffers damage from a servicer's failure to comply may bring a private cause of action for actual damages of at least $500 per violation, plus other relief the court deems proper (section 6.2-2619).

Virginia's Office of the Qualified Education Loan Ombudsman, at the State Council of Higher Education for Virginia, provides timely assistance to Virginia borrowers, acts as a liaison with loan servicers to explore repayment options and try to resolve complaints, and refers abusive, unfair, deceptive or fraudulent matters to the Attorney General.

Debt settlement and credit repair companies: the rules that protect you

The advance-fee ban — the single most important rule

If a debt relief company sells by phone — including when you call them after seeing an ad — the FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule makes it illegal for them to collect any fee before they have actually settled or renegotiated at least one of your debts, you have agreed to the written settlement, and you have made at least one payment under it. A demand for an upfront enrollment, setup, or document fee in that setting breaks federal law. Before you sign, the company must also disclose how long results will take, the total cost, and the negative consequences — and it may not misrepresent what it can do.

Credit repair has its own federal law

The Credit Repair Organizations Act bars a credit repair company from taking any payment before the promised service has been fully performed. You must get a written contract and a statement of your rights, and you have three business days to cancel. No company can legally remove accurate negative information from your report, and one that tells you to dispute true information, lie on an application, or use a new "credit profile number" is telling you to break the law.

How debt settlement really works — the part ads skip

Settlement programs usually tell you to stop paying your creditors and save into a separate account instead. While you save, late fees and interest pile up, your credit score falls, and creditors can sue you — and many debts never settle at all. Debt that is forgiven can also be taxed as income (watch for a Form 1099-C). None of this means settlement never works; it means the risks are real and the fee should only be paid for results actually delivered.

Try the free route first

Nonprofit credit counselors — the kind listed earlier in this section — typically review your whole budget in a first session that is free or low-cost, and can set up a debt management plan that pays creditors in full at reduced interest, without the stop-paying gamble. You can also negotiate with a creditor yourself: many will settle or reduce rates directly, and every dollar of fee you skip is a dollar off the debt.

What Virginia requires

Virginia's Bureau of Financial Institutions, a division of the State Corporation Commission, lists debt settlement services providers and credit counseling agencies among the non-depository institutions it regulates. Each such institution is required to obtain a certificate of authority or a license prior to engaging in business.

Small business basics in Virginia

This section is general information for people starting a small business — a different audience from most of this site, but the same plain-language, verified-links approach. Starting a business is mostly paperwork done in the right order: pick a structure, register it, get your tax IDs, check licenses, and put agreements in writing before money changes hands. The details, fees, and forms are set by Virginia and by your city or county, so this page gives the national steps and the official places to check, and does not print a single Virginia fee or form number that it has not verified.

Official resource

Pick a structure first: sole proprietorship, LLC, partnership, corporation

The structure decides your personal liability, your taxes, and your paperwork. A sole proprietorship is the simplest — you and the business are the same legal person, so you are personally liable for its debts. An LLC (limited liability company) separates your personal assets from the business’s debts in most cases, with simpler paperwork than a corporation. A partnership splits ownership between two or more people and needs a written partnership agreement even when the law does not require one. A corporation offers the strongest separation but the most formalities (directors, bylaws, annual filings). There is no universally best choice: liability, taxes, and how many owners you have should drive it, and a tax adviser or small-business counselor can help you compare them for your situation before you file anything.

LLC formation

Forming an LLC in Virginia: the general steps

The usual sequence is: (1) choose and check a business name against Virginia’s business registry so it is not already taken; (2) name a registered agent with a physical address in Virginia to receive legal papers; (3) file articles of organization (the name varies by state) with Virginia’s secretary of state or equivalent business-filing office, usually online; (4) pay the state filing fee, which varies widely by state and is not printed here because it changes and was not verified for all 50 states in this section; (5) write an operating agreement, even if Virginia does not require filing it — it settles who owns what, who decides what, and what happens if an owner leaves; and (6) keep the business’s money separate from your own from day one. Tax IDs: get an EIN free from the IRS

An Employer Identification Number (EIN) is a nine-digit tax ID the IRS assigns to a business; you need one if you hire employees, operate as a corporation or partnership, file certain tax returns, or open a business bank account (many banks require one). Apply online directly from the IRS — it is free, and you will usually get the number immediately on completion. Any site charging a fee for an EIN is selling you a free government service. Keep the IRS confirmation letter: banks, licensing offices, and tax filings will ask for it. Separately, check whether Virginia or your city requires a state tax ID or sales-tax registration — that is a different registration from the federal EIN, handled by Virginia’s tax or revenue department.

Contracts

Simple contracts: what a solid written agreement needs

Handshake deals fail exactly when they matter, so put it in writing before work starts. A solid simple agreement names the parties, describes the work or goods precisely, states the price and payment schedule, sets start and finish dates, says who owns the work product, covers changes (written change orders, not verbal), and states how disputes are handled and how either side can end the deal. Both sides sign and date it, and each keeps a copy. For partnerships and multi-owner LLCs, the operating or partnership agreement is the contract that matters most: it should cover ownership percentages, contributions, decision rules, what happens if an owner dies or leaves, and how the business is valued. Template contracts from the internet are a starting point, not a substitute for advice on a high-value or long-term deal — have a lawyer review anything that could sink the business if it goes wrong.

Licenses

Business licenses and permits: where to check

There is no single national business license. Check three levels: (1) Virginia — the secretary of state’s business office and the state tax/revenue department for state registration and tax permits; (2) your city or county — the clerk’s or licensing office for a general business license and any home-occupation permit if you work from home; and (3) your industry — health, food, construction, childcare, and professional services are licensed by separate state boards with their own exams and requirements. Also check zoning before signing a lease: a business that is legal in one part of town may not be permitted in another, and a home-based business may need a home-occupation permit even if no customers visit. The SBA’s guide above and your local Small Business Development Center (free counseling, found through the SBA site) can walk you through the licenses for your specific business.

Traps

Common first-timer traps

Mixing personal and business money (open a business account and pay yourself properly); signing a commercial lease or a personal guarantee without reading the term, renewal, and exit clauses; hiring a first worker as a “contractor” when the law treats them as an employee, which creates payroll-tax liability; skipping insurance (general liability at minimum, plus professional liability or workers’ compensation where required); ignoring sales tax on what you sell; and paying a middleman for a free government filing (EIN, and in many states the basic registration). Each of these is cheaper to fix before it happens than after, and each is a reason to spend one session with a small-business counselor or accountant before launch.

What to do:

  1. Pick a structure. Compare liability and taxes (sole proprietorship vs. LLC vs. partnership vs. corporation) before you file.
  2. Form it properly. Check the name, name a registered agent, file articles of organization with Virginia’s secretary of state, and write an operating agreement.
  3. Get an EIN free from the IRS. Apply on irs.gov directly; never pay a middleman for it.
  4. Check licenses at three levels: state, city/county, and your industry board — plus zoning before you sign a lease.
  5. Put agreements in writing. Scope, price, dates, ownership, changes, and exit terms, signed by both sides.
  6. Separate the money and get insured. Business bank account from day one; liability insurance and workers’ compensation where required.

Got an IRS notice? Read the number first, then act by the date on it

Start with the notice number

Every IRS notice prints a number at the top (like CP14 or LT11). Look it up free on the IRS site or the Taxpayer Advocate site before you do anything else. Common ones: CP14 is your first bill for unpaid taxes. CP501 and CP503 are reminders. CP2000 means the IRS thinks your return does not match its income records — it is a proposal, not a bill, and you can disagree in writing. CP504 warns the IRS intends to take your state tax refund. LT11 or Letter 1058 is a final notice that the IRS intends to levy — take money from wages or bank accounts — and it carries the right to ask for a hearing.

Answer by the date on the notice — even if you can't pay

Ignoring a notice never makes it smaller; interest and penalties keep growing. Respond by the deadline printed on your notice: pay, ask for a payment plan, or explain in writing why you disagree. For a final levy notice (LT11 or Letter 1058), you generally have 30 days from the letter's date to request a Collection Due Process hearing (Form 12153), which stops the levy while your case is heard. Keep copies of everything and proof of mailing.

Can't pay? The IRS's own payment plans

A short-term plan lets you pay in full within 180 days with no setup fee (individuals owing under $100,000). A long-term installment agreement can be set up online at irs.gov if you owe $50,000 or less: the setup fee is $29 with automatic bank payments, $69 without ($107 or $178 if you apply by phone or mail), and low-income taxpayers pay less or nothing. While your request is being considered — and while a plan is in effect — the IRS generally cannot levy you. If even a small payment is impossible right now, ask the IRS about a temporary delay of collection for financial hardship.

Settling for less: Offer in Compromise

If you genuinely cannot pay in full, an Offer in Compromise (Form 656) asks the IRS to settle for less, based on your income, expenses, and assets. It costs a $205 application fee plus a first payment (20% down on a lump-sum offer); low-income applicants owe no fee or payments during review. Use the IRS's free Offer in Compromise Pre-Qualifier tool first — the IRS itself says to explore a payment plan before offering.

Free help that is actually free

The Taxpayer Advocate Service (1-877-777-4778) is an independent organization inside the IRS that helps when IRS procedures are causing you financial harm or your problem is stuck; ask for help with Form 911. Low Income Taxpayer Clinics give free legal help with IRS disputes to taxpayers under income limits — the IRS lists them on irs.gov. The Low Income Taxpayer Clinic in this section is another free route.

Skip the “tax relief” companies

Companies advertising on TV and radio charge thousands of dollars up front to file the same forms you can file free above — and the FTC has shut many down for taking fees and doing nothing. Never pay a tax-relief company before you have used the IRS's free pre-qualifier tool and talked to the Taxpayer Advocate Service or a Low Income Taxpayer Clinic.
