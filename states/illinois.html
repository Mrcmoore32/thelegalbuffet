Filming the police: your rights in Illinois

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

All-party consent

State law generally requires everyone recorded to consent — but on-duty officers in public have no reasonable expectation of privacy, so courts have held that recording them is protected. (720 ILCS 5/14-2 (amended 2014))

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Illinois has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

Illinois was all-party, but the old eavesdropping law was struck down in People v. Melongo and People v. Clark (Ill. 2014) and, as applied to recording police, by ACLU v. Alvarez (7th Cir. 2012). The replacement law covers only private conversations where parties have a reasonable expectation of privacy, which does not include on-duty officers in public.
