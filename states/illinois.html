Tenant rights in Illinois

Renting comes with rights most people never hear about until something goes wrong. Here are the key ones in Illinois.

Eviction

Notice before eviction

30 days to end month-to-month; 5-day notice to pay or quit for nonpayment

Security deposit

Deposit limits & return

No statewide cap (Chicago/Cook County have own rules); returned within 45 days, itemized statement within 30 days if deductions

Repairs

When the landlord won't fix things

May repair and deduct after written notice (cap $500 or half month's rent)

DUI / DWI in Illinois

Driving under the influence carries some of the stiffest penalties in everyday law. Here's how Illinois handles it.

Legal limit

Blood alcohol (BAC) limit

0.08; enhanced penalties at 0.16 (100 hrs community service)

First offense

Typical penalties

Up to 1 year jail, up to $2,500 fine, 6-month license suspension (12 months if test refused)

Gun laws in Illinois

Concealed carry

Carrying a concealed handgun

Permit required (Concealed Carry License), shall-issue, 21+ (FOID also required)

Open carry

Carrying openly

Prohibited

Background checks & waiting periods

Yes — (Firearms Restraining Orders). Courts can temporarily order firearms removed from someone found to be a danger.

Filming the police: your rights in Illinois

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

All-party consent

State law generally requires everyone recorded to consent — but on-duty officers in public have no reasonable expectation of privacy, so courts have held that recording them is protected. (720 ILCS 5/14-2 (amended 2014))

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Illinois has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

Illinois was all-party, but the old eavesdropping law was struck down in People v. Melongo and People v. Clark (Ill. 2014) and, as applied to recording police, by ACLU v. Alvarez (7th Cir. 2012). The replacement law covers only private conversations where parties have a reasonable expectation of privacy, which does not include on-duty officers in public.
