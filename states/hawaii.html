How divorce filing works in Hawaii

Hawaiʻi's Judiciary publishes official Family Court divorce packets — with and without children — for each circuit. Start with the packets before anything else.

Official court site

Official divorce packets with and without children from the First Circuit Family Court.

What to do next:

  1. Make sure you meet the residency requirement listed above before you file.
  2. Get the court’s free forms or online filing tool (linked above) and fill them out completely.
  3. File with the district court in the county where either spouse lives, and pay the filing fee.
  4. Know the cost. The filing fee is $215 ($265 if you have minor children).
  5. Serve your spouse properly and follow the court’s steps from there — the self-help pages walk through each phase.

Car repossession in Hawaii

If you're behind on car payments, know your rights before the tow truck shows up — and after.

Your rights

What they can't do

No breaking into a locked garage, no physical force, no threats — that's "breach of the peace" and it's illegal everywhere. They must return your personal belongings from the car.

Get it back

Cure and redemption

No state cure right — only what your loan contract allows. Pay the full loan balance plus costs any time before the car is sold.

Money owed

Deficiency balance

The lender can sue for the remaining balance after a fair sale.

Notice

What they must tell you

No advance warning required. Notice before the sale is required.

What to do next:

  1. If your car was taken, call the lender immediately and ask for the exact payoff or cure amount in writing.
  2. Remove valuables from your car now — don't wait for repossession.
  3. If the repo agent used force, threats, or broke into a locked space, document everything and contact free legal help in Hawaii.

Medical malpractice in Hawaii

A bad outcome is not always malpractice. To have a case, you usually must show the doctor or hospital fell below the accepted standard of care — what a careful provider would have done — and that the mistake caused your injury. These cases turn on strict deadlines and expert review, so the clock matters here more than almost anywhere else.

Take action

Complain to the Hawaii Medical Board

Filing a complaint with the state medical board is free. The board can investigate and discipline a doctor, but it cannot award you money — that is what a lawsuit is for. Filing a complaint does not pause your lawsuit deadline.

File a complaint or look up a doctor →
Deadline

Hawaii's deadline to sue

You generally have 2 years from when you discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, the injury — and never more than 6 years from the act or omission. Miss the deadline and your case is usually over, no matter how strong it is — talk to a lawyer well before it runs out.

Free help

Talk to a malpractice lawyer

Most malpractice lawyers give a free first consultation and work on contingency — you pay nothing up front, and they take a percentage only if you win. Many states also require an expert to certify the case before you can file; a lawyer handles that.

Your records

Get your medical records

You have the right to copies of your own medical records. Ask every provider involved, in writing, and keep the copies somewhere safe — records are the backbone of any malpractice case.

What to do next:

  1. Request complete copies of your medical records from every provider involved.
  2. Write down your timeline — dates, symptoms, what you were told — while it is fresh.
  3. Do not sign anything from the hospital or its insurer, and do not accept a quick settlement, before a lawyer looks at it.
  4. Get a free consultation with a malpractice attorney well before Hawaii's deadline above.
  5. If you want the doctor investigated, file the free board complaint too — just remember it does not stop your lawsuit clock.

Wrongly accused in Hawaii

Being accused of a crime you did not commit is frightening — but you have rights from the first minute. You are presumed innocent, you do not have to talk to police, and if you cannot afford a lawyer, the court must appoint one to defend you for free. What you do in the first days matters most.

Free help

Get a free defense lawyer

If you have been charged and cannot afford a lawyer, you are entitled to a free public defender. You usually apply through the court at your first appearance — the Hawaii Office of the Public Defender site explains how Hawaii's system works and how to reach your local office.

Hawaii Office of the Public Defender →
Take action

Wrongfully convicted? Ask Hawai'i Innocence Project for a case review

The Hawai'i Innocence Project reviews cases of people convicted of crimes they did not commit and fights to free the innocent. If you or a loved one was wrongfully convicted in Hawaii, you can ask them to review the case — most take applications by mail or online.

Learn how to ask for a case review →
Know your rights

Your first court appearance

At your first appearance (arraignment), the judge reads the charges and asks how you plead. Pleading not guilty keeps every option open — it does not lock you into a trial. This is also where you ask the court to appoint a public defender if you cannot afford a lawyer. Do not plead guilty just to get it over with.

What to do next:

  1. Do not talk to police without a lawyer. Say: “I am using my right to remain silent, and I want a lawyer” — then stop talking.
  2. Do not consent to searches of your home, car, or phone. Saying “I do not consent to a search” protects you later, even if police search anyway.
  3. Write down your timeline and where you were, plus the names of anyone who can confirm it, while it is fresh.
  4. Save everything — texts, emails, receipts, photos — and do not delete anything, even if it looks bad.
  5. Do not contact the person who accused you, and do not post about the case online.
  6. Ask the court for a public defender at your first appearance if you cannot afford a lawyer.

Immigration help in Hawaii

Immigration law is mostly federal — the same rules apply in every state — but the help is local. The organizations below give free or low-cost immigration legal help in Hawaii, and they are recognized to do this work. If money is tight, start there instead of paying someone who is not a lawyer.

Free help

Immigration legal help: Hawai'i Immigrant Justice Center at Legal Aid Society of Hawaii

Hawai'i Immigrant Justice Center at Legal Aid Society of Hawaii helps people in Hawaii with immigration cases — green cards, citizenship, asylum, deportation defense, and work permits. Call before your situation becomes an emergency; these offices are busy and waitlists are common.

Get immigration legal help →
Know your rights

If immigration officers stop you or come to your home

You have the right to remain silent, and you do not have to answer questions about where you were born. You do not have to open your door unless officers show a warrant signed by a judge — an ICE administrative warrant is not the same thing. Do not sign anything, and do not show false documents, before talking to a lawyer. Say: “I want to remain silent and I want to speak to a lawyer.”

Driver’s licenses

Can you get a license in Hawaii?

Hawaii lets residents get a driver’s license regardless of immigration status. Ask the legal help organization above which documents you will need to bring.

State resources

Hawaii Dept. of Labor & Industrial Relations, Office of Community Services – Employment Services

Hawaii’s own office or program for newcomers and refugees — services, benefits, and local programs in your area.

State resources for immigrants →

What to do next:

  1. Call the legal help organization above before any deadline or court date — immigration deadlines are strict and missing one can end a case.
  2. Beware of “notarios” and immigration consultants who are not lawyers or accredited representatives. In the U.S., a notario is not a lawyer, and bad paperwork help can get you deported.
  3. Gather your documents — passport, any papers you have ever received from immigration, court notices — and keep copies somewhere safe.
  4. Never sign a document you do not understand, and never pay in cash without a written agreement and a receipt.
  5. Write down important dates: entries, filings, hearings. Immigration cases turn on dates.

Wills & probate in Hawaii

When someone dies, their property does not just move on its own. Probate is the court process that proves the will, pays the debts, and transfers what is left to the family. Every state also has a shortcut for small estates — and if you are making your own will, Hawaii’s signing rules decide whether it counts. Here is where to start in Hawaii.

Where probate happens

Hawaiʻi State Judiciary — Overview of the Hawaiʻi Judicial System (Circuit Courts hold probate jurisdiction)

This is the official court starting point for wills and estates in Hawaii — forms, filing steps, and the court that handles the estate.

Start with the probate court →
Small estates

Hawaii’s small-estate shortcut

Hawaii has a small-estate affidavit procedure for modest estates (Haw. Rev. Stat. §560:3-1201) — ask the probate court clerk for the current dollar limit before you file.

Making a will

Are handwritten wills valid in Hawaii?

Hawaii’s will-signing rules are in Haw. Rev. Stat. §560:2-502. Handwritten wills are risky everywhere — if a handwritten will is all you have, take it to the probate court before assuming it controls anything, and if you are making a will, follow the statute’s witness rules exactly.

First steps

When someone dies: the first week

Order several certified copies of the death certificate — banks, insurers, and agencies each want one. Find the original will (check the home, a safe deposit box, and the court or lawyer who prepared it). Secure the home and vehicles. Do not pay the person’s debts from your own money — debts are paid from the estate, in an order the law sets. Notify Social Security and the person’s employer and insurers.

What to do next:

  1. Get certified death certificates and locate the original will.
  2. Check the estate’s size against Hawaii’s small-estate shortcut above — it can save months and court fees.
  3. If the estate is larger, or includes a house or a dispute, open probate at the court above — and ask legal aid or an estate lawyer about a free first consultation.
  4. Do not distribute property, sell the car, or clean out accounts until the court says who has authority — handing things out early can make you personally responsible.
  5. Making your own will? Follow Hawaii’s signing and witness rules exactly, tell your executor where the original is, and review it after marriages, divorces, births, and deaths.

Bankruptcy in Hawaii

Bankruptcy is a legal way to wipe out or reorganize debts you cannot pay. Chapter 7 erases most unsecured debts like credit cards and medical bills, usually in a few months. Chapter 13 is a 3-to-5-year payment plan that can stop a foreclosure and let you catch up. The day you file, an “automatic stay” stops most collections, lawsuits, wage garnishments, and shutoffs. Some debts survive — most student loans, recent taxes, and child support among them.

Your court

The bankruptcy court for Hawaii

Bankruptcy is a federal case — you file in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court, not a state court. Hawaii is served by the court below; the court site has the forms, filing fees, courthouse locations, and local rules.

U.S. Bankruptcy Court — District of Hawaii →
Keeping your home

How much home equity does Hawaii protect?

Hawaii protects $30,000 of home equity if you are head of family or 65 or older, and $20,000 for everyone else (Haw. Rev. Stat. §651-92).

Your choice: Hawaii lets you pick either the state exemption list or the federal exemption list — whichever protects more of what you own. The federal list protects about $31,575 of home equity per filer (adjusted for inflation), plus a wildcard amount that can cover more. A bankruptcy lawyer or legal aid can run both lists against your situation before you file.

Free help

Free bankruptcy guidance: Legal Aid Society of Hawaii

Legal Aid Society of Hawaii helps people in Hawaii understand whether bankruptcy is the right move, and what the alternatives are. Many bankruptcy lawyers also offer a free first consultation — ask what the full cost will be before you hire anyone.

Get free bankruptcy guidance →
Before you file

Credit counseling is required

Within 180 days before filing, you must complete a short credit counseling course from an approved agency and file the certificate with the court — without it, your case can be dismissed. The U.S. Trustee Program keeps the official list of approved agencies.

Find an approved credit counseling agency →

What to do next:

  1. List everything: every debt, every asset, your income, and your monthly expenses. A lawyer or counselor can only help with the full picture.
  2. Get the free guidance above first — bankruptcy is powerful, but it stays on your credit report for up to 10 years, and sometimes a payment plan or negotiation works better.
  3. Stop using credit cards once you decide to file, and do not transfer or give away property to friends or family — the court can undo transfers and it can sink your case.
  4. Complete the required credit counseling and keep the certificate.
  5. Ask the court about the filing fee: if you cannot afford it, you can ask to pay in installments, or to have the Chapter 7 fee waived.

Workplace rights in Hawaii

Two problems hit workers hardest: being treated unfairly on the job, and losing the job altogether. Both have agencies, both have deadlines, and both are free to use. Here is where each one goes in Hawaii.

Discrimination

Filing a discrimination charge: Hawai'i Civil Rights Commission

If an employer treats you unfairly because of your race, sex, age (40+), disability, religion, national origin, or another protected trait, you file a charge with the Hawai'i Civil Rights Commission. The deadline in Hawaii is 1 year (Haw. Rev. Stat. § 368-11(c). Missing it can end your claim before it starts — file first, gather documents second.

File a discrimination charge →
Unemployment

Lost your job? File for unemployment in Hawaii

Unemployment benefits are run by the Hawai'i Department of Labor and Industrial Relations, Unemployment Insurance Division. File in your first week out of work — benefits do not backdate just because you waited, and most states make you certify every week to keep them coming. You will need your Social Security number, your employer’s details, and your recent work history.

File for unemployment benefits →
Pay & hours

Minimum wage, overtime, and unpaid pay

The federal floor is a $7.25 minimum wage and time-and-a-half after 40 hours in a week for most workers — and Hawaii’s own wage law may promise more. If your pay itself is short, see the unpaid wages section on this page, and know that the U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division also takes complaints for free.

U.S. Department of Labor — Wage and Hour Division →
Retaliation

Punished for speaking up?

It is illegal for an employer to fire, demote, or cut the hours of a worker for filing a discrimination charge, reporting a safety problem, or asking about unpaid wages. If that happens, tell the agency you filed with right away — retaliation is its own violation, with its own deadline.

What to do next:

  1. Write down what happened, with dates, names, and witnesses, while it is fresh — and keep pay stubs, schedules, and messages somewhere your employer cannot delete them.
  2. Check the deadline above and file your charge before it runs out, even if you are still gathering documents.
  3. If you lost the job, file for unemployment the same week — then keep up the weekly certifications.
  4. Do not sign a severance agreement or a “resignation” letter under pressure. You can ask for time to have a lawyer or legal aid look at it first.
  5. If money is tight, ask legal aid about employment cases — many discrimination lawyers also offer a free first consultation.

Child custody & visitation in Hawaii

When parents live apart, a court order is what turns an arrangement into something enforceable — who the child lives with, when each parent has them, and who decides about school and medical care. You do not need a lawyer to start, and in Hawaii the courts publish the path. Here it is.

Where to start

Family Court Forms for Oʻahu (First Circuit) — Hawaiʻi State Judiciary (companion page: Self-Help Resources)

This is the official court starting point for custody cases in Hawaii — the process, the forms, and the terms the court uses.

Start with the court →
The words matter

What Hawaii calls it

In Hawaii, the courts call it custody / visitation (forms include "Custody/Visitation Statement," 1F-P-704). Whatever the label, the judge is deciding the same two things: who makes the big decisions for the child, and where the child lives and spends time.

Forms & parenting plans

Free forms and the parenting plan

The court offers free forms — including, in most states, a parenting plan you fill in with the schedule, holidays, and decision-making you are asking for. A complete, realistic plan is the single most persuasive thing you can file.

Safety

If a child is in danger

If a child is in immediate danger, call 911. Courts can issue emergency and temporary custody orders quickly when a child’s safety is at risk — ask the clerk how to request one. Many states also require or offer mediation before a custody hearing; it is cheaper and faster than a courtroom fight, and it does not require you to give up your rights.

What to do next:

  1. Gather the basics: the child’s birth certificate, any existing court orders, and school and medical information.
  2. Write down the schedule you are asking for — school weeks, weekends, holidays, summers — and how it serves the child. Judges decide on the child’s best interests, not on which parent argues best.
  3. File in the court for the county where the child lives, using the court’s own forms and terms above.
  4. Do not withhold the child, move away, or skip a court date out of frustration — each one damages your case.
  5. If you cannot afford a lawyer, ask the court about legal aid and filing-fee waivers — custody help is one of the most common things legal aid offices handle.

Voting rights in Hawaii

Voting rules are set by each state, and the two that trip people up are ID and registration deadlines. Here is exactly what Hawaii’s own elections officials say — plus what to do if something goes wrong at the polls.

Start here

Office of Elections

Hawaii’s official voter page: check your registration, register or update your information, and find where and when to vote. Not sure of your state? Vote.gov routes every voter to the right state.

Go to Hawaii’s official voter page →
At the polls

What ID do you need in Hawaii?

Hawaii votes mostly by mail. First-time voters who register by mail may be asked for ID — check the Office of Elections page for the current rule.

Deadlines

Registration deadlines in Hawaii

For the Nov. 3, 2026 general election, the paper registration deadline was October 26, 2026. You can register online at any time, or in person at a voter service center — centers open 10 business days before the election and offer same-day registration.

Problems at the polls

If you are turned away

Do not just leave. If officials say you are not on the list or your ID is not accepted, ask for a provisional (or affidavit) ballot — federal law gives you that right, and your vote counts once your registration is confirmed. Then call your county elections office or the official voter page above and report what happened. If you believe your rights were violated, you can also call the national, nonpartisan Election Protection hotline at 866-OUR-VOTE (866-687-8683).

What to do next:

  1. Check your registration on the official page above now — not the week of the election — and fix any problem while there is time.
  2. Check the ID rule for Hawaii above and get the document in hand early; several states give free voter ID cards to people who ask.
  3. Find your polling place and its hours, or vote early or by mail if your state offers it and your schedule is tight.
  4. If anything goes wrong on the day, ask for a provisional ballot, write down the names of the officials involved, and report it.

Utility shutoffs in Hawaii

A shutoff notice is not the end of the story. Most states restrict when a utility can turn off your heat or power — especially in winter — and there is a federally funded program in every state that helps pay the bill. Here is Hawaii’s rule, the help, and where to complain.

Winter protection

Hawaii’s shutoff rule

No statewide cold- or heat-weather shutoff rule was found in Hawai‘i’s official sources in our research. If you receive a shutoff notice, complain to the Public Utilities Commission right away and apply for the Hawai‘i Home Energy Assistance Program (H-HEAP) below — do not wait for the shutoff date.

Help paying

Energy assistance: Hawaiʻi Home Energy Assistance Program (H-HEAP), Hawaiʻi Dept. of Human Services — https://humanservices.hawaii.gov/bessd/liheap/

The federal energy assistance program (LIHEAP, under its Hawaii name above) helps low-income households pay heating and cooling bills and can cover crisis reconnections. Apply as early as you can — in many states, being approved for assistance is itself what switches the winter protection on, and the money runs out each season.

Apply for energy assistance →
Complaints

If the utility will not work with you: Hawaiʻi Public Utilities Commission

The Hawaiʻi Public Utilities Commission regulates utilities in Hawaii and takes consumer complaints. Filing a complaint often pauses a shutoff while the regulator reviews it — file before the shutoff date, not after.

File a utility complaint →
Right now

Got a shutoff notice?

Call the utility before the date on the notice and ask for a payment plan — get the agreement in writing, with the amount and dates. If anyone in the home is seriously ill or depends on medical equipment, say so on that call and ask about a medical certificate or medical hold. Dialing 211 connects you to local help (charities, churches, and emergency funds) in most of the country.

What to do next:

  1. Find the shutoff date on your notice — everything else is scheduled backwards from that date.
  2. Call the utility and set up a payment plan; in several states the plan itself is your legal protection. Write down the name of the person you spoke with.
  3. Apply for energy assistance above right away — approval can pause or prevent the shutoff.
  4. If the utility will not budge, file the complaint above before the shutoff date.
  5. Never ignore a notice because you cannot pay in full — every protection on this page starts with you contacting someone.

Financial scams in Hawaii

Scammers are professional liars, and they are good at it — Americans lose billions of dollars to fraud every year, and most of it is never reported. Falling for one is not a character flaw; these crews run scripts tested on thousands of people. The good news: almost every financial scam shares the same handful of tells, and once you know them, the spell breaks.

The golden rule: anyone who pressures you to pay right now with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, a cash app, or cash is running a scam — full stop. No real agency, bank, utility, or business demands payment that way. The second someone insists on secrecy (“don’t tell your family or your bank”), hang up. Urgency plus secrecy plus a strange payment method is the whole game.

Know the signs

Government impostors

A call, text, or email claims to be the IRS, Social Security, Medicare, or the sheriff: you owe money, your benefits will stop, or there is a warrant for your arrest. Real agencies almost always contact you by mail first, never threaten arrest over the phone, and never ask for gift cards or wire transfers. Hang up and call the agency yourself at the number on its official website or your paperwork.

Know the signs

The family emergency — now with cloned voices

“Grandma, I’m in jail, I need bail money — please don’t tell Mom.” Scammers can now clone a loved one’s voice from a few seconds of online audio, so it can sound exactly like them. Defenses that work: agree on a family code word in advance, and always hang up and call your loved one back on the number you already have. Real trouble survives a callback; a scam can’t.

Know the signs

Romance and investment scams

An online sweetheart who can never meet — and eventually needs money — is a script, not a relationship. So is the friendly stranger pitching can’t-miss crypto or investment returns, sometimes showing you a fake account that looks like it is growing (“pig butchering”). Guaranteed high returns with no risk do not exist. If you have to pay fees or taxes before you can withdraw your own money, it was never real.

Know the signs

Fake checks, phishing, and tech support

A check that overpays you — for a car, a job, a prize — with a request to send part back is counterfeit; it bounces days later and you owe the bank. Texts and emails about a “locked account” or a “missed delivery” are phishing for your logins — never use their link; type the company’s address yourself. And a pop-up warning that your computer has a virus, with a number to call, is fake: real companies never do that.

Take action

Report a scam in Hawaii

File a complaint with Hawaii’s consumer protection office. They track scam patterns, investigate, and sometimes get money back — and your report warns the next person.

By phone

Free help lines

Age 60 or older? The Justice Department’s National Elder Fraud Hotline: 1-833-372-8311 (Mon–Fri, 10 a.m.–6 p.m. ET) gives you a case manager who helps you report and find local help. Anyone, any age, can call the AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline: 1-877-908-3360 (Mon–Fri, 8 a.m.–8 p.m. ET) — free, member or not, for advice on a suspicious call or support after a loss.

Already paid or shared information? Act in this order:

  1. Call your bank or card issuer right now — use the number on your card or statement, not one the scammer gave you. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment. Wires and transfers have short rescue windows; hours matter.
  2. Paid by gift card? Call the company on the back of the card immediately and say it was a scam — if the money has not been drained yet, they can sometimes freeze it. Keep the cards and receipts.
  3. Shared personal information (Social Security number, passwords, account logins)? Go to IdentityTheft.gov for a step-by-step recovery plan, and place a free credit freeze with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion so no one can open accounts in your name.
  4. Change the passwords on any account you touched or mentioned, starting with email and banking, and turn on two-factor authentication.
  5. Report it — to Hawaii’s consumer office and the FTC (links above) — and tell your family. Silence is the scammer’s best protection; a report is how the next victim gets warned.

What to bring to court in Hawaii

Walking in prepared makes the day go smoother and helps the judge see your case clearly. Use this as a packing list — a starting point, not legal advice. Your court or your lawyer may ask for more.

  1. Your photo ID and your case number. Courts run on case numbers — bring the one from your court papers.
  2. Copies of every document you filed, plus two extra sets. One set for the judge, one for the other side, one for yourself.
  3. Evidence that backs your claims, in order. Dated messages, receipts, photos, statements — a paper trail beats memory. Bring originals where you have them.
  4. A one-page timeline. What happened, in date order, and who was there. Judges love timelines.
  5. Financial records if money is at issue. Pay stubs, bank statements, bills, tax returns — bring more than you think you’ll need.
  6. A pen and paper. To write down the judge’s orders, next dates, and anything you’re told to do.

Before the day: call the courthouse (or check its website) to confirm the courtroom, the time, and anything you’re not sure about. Arrive 30 minutes early — you’ll go through security and need time to find the room. Leave anything distracting at home and silence your phone before you walk in.

Tenant rights in Hawaii

Renting comes with rights most people never hear about until something goes wrong. Here are the key ones in Hawaii.

Before you sign a lease

A lease is a contract. These checks take a few minutes before you sign and can protect your money, your home, and your security deposit later. Lease rules vary by state and sometimes by city, so use the Hawaii tenant resources and official link in this section to check the exact rule.

  1. Read it before you sign. Read every page, including addendums and rules. Never sign a lease with blank spaces: cross out a blank that does not apply or write “N/A,” and have both sides initial any handwritten change.
  2. Get every promise in writing. A promise about repairs, appliances, parking, storage, or the move-in date is hard to enforce if it is only spoken. If it matters, put it in the lease or in a signed addendum.
  3. Question clauses that take away basic protections. In many states, clauses like these are illegal or unenforceable: giving up your right to an itemized security-deposit statement; letting the landlord enter at any time without notice; giving up notice or your right to go to court before an eviction; renewing the lease automatically with a large penalty that is not spelled out clearly up front; or making you pay for all repairs, including major systems. Check Hawaii’s rules with the tenant resource in this section, and ask for a problem clause to be removed or changed before you sign.
  4. Know the money up front. Add up the full move-in cost: first month’s rent, the security deposit, and every fee. The lease should say which charges are a refundable deposit and which are nonrefundable fees, when rent is due, the exact late fee, and how rent must be paid.
  5. Document the home on move-in day. Photograph or video every room and every flaw, including walls, floors, windows, and appliances. Write existing damage on a move-in checklist, date it, and keep a copy; if the landlord gives you a form, return it on time and keep your dated copy. That record helps protect your deposit when you move out.
  6. Get your complete copy. Leave with a signed copy of the lease and every addendum, plus the landlord’s legal name and a mailing address for repair requests and legal notices. Do not rely on a promise to send your copy later.
  7. Spell out who pays for what. Check who is responsible for utilities, lawn care, snow removal, and supplying and repairing appliances. If the lease does not say, ask before you sign and get the answer written in.

What to do next:

  1. Put repair requests and complaints to your landlord in writing, and keep copies.
  2. If you get an eviction notice, don’t just move out — only a court can order an eviction.

Two ways to post bail: pay the full amount to the court yourself (you get it back when the case ends), or hire a bail bondsman — they post it for a fee, usually around 10% of the bail amount, which you do not get back.

Filming the police: your rights in Hawaii

Federal appeals courts across the country have recognized a First Amendment right to photograph and record police officers performing their official duties in public. The U.S. Supreme Court has not ruled on it directly. The right does not protect interfering with officers, trespassing, or breaking other laws while you record.

Audio recording

One-party consent

If you are part of a conversation, you may record it — including with police. (Haw. Rev. Stat. § 803-42)

Distance rules

No buffer-zone law

Hawaii has no law setting a minimum distance for filming police. You still must not interfere with officers doing their jobs.

Hawaii's eavesdropping law is one-party consent. The privacy statute (Haw. Rev. Stat. § 711-1111(1)(d)) expressly permits recording officers performing their duties when they have no reasonable expectation of privacy. The Hawaii Supreme Court held in State v. Russo (407 P.3d 137, 2017) that filming on-duty police in public is protected by the First Amendment and the state constitution.

What to do next:

  1. Keep a safe distance and don’t interfere — that’s the line the law draws.
  2. Know your state’s audio-recording rule stated above before you hit record.
  3. If you believe your rights were violated, write down exactly what happened and report it: how to report a civil rights violation.

Defamation in Hawaii

Someone posted or said something false about you? In Hawaii, defamation means a false statement of fact, shared with others, that harmed your reputation. Truth is a complete defense, and pure opinions (“I think he’s a jerk”) generally don’t count.

Deadline to sue: 2 years from when the statement was made (Single 2-year limit covers both libel and slander under HRS 657-4.). Miss it and you lose the right to file.

Anti-SLAPP: Hawaii has an anti-SLAPP law — it lets people get baseless lawsuits over speech thrown out quickly, and the person who filed can be ordered to pay the other side’s legal costs.

What to do next:

  1. Save everything. Screenshots with dates, links to the posts, and names of anyone who saw them. Don’t rely on the post staying up.
  2. Don’t fire back publicly. Responding online can hurt your case later.
  3. Talk to a lawyer who handles defamation. Many offer a free first consultation or take strong cases on contingency — you don’t pay unless you win.
